📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Which atoms give their electrons away, which ones share, and how does that single choice decide what a compound's formula has to be?
You now know that an element's place on the table reveals its valence electrons. Today you'll use that to answer a practical question: when two elements meet, how do they combine, and in what ratio? You'll see that atoms reorganize their electrons to reach a stable arrangement — and that this drive splits compounds into two great families, ionic and molecular. This is the doorway into Module 3, where bonding becomes the whole story.

📚 PART 1 · THE DRIVE TOWARD A FULL SHELL
🔗 A BRIDGE LESSON · FROM ATOMIC STRUCTURE TO BONDING
Every lesson so far has been about the structure of a single atom. This last lesson of Module 2 is the bridge into Module 3: you'll take everything you know about periodic position and valence electrons and use it to predict what happens when atoms meet — which ions an element forms, and what formula a compound must have to stay neutral. That is exactly as far as we go here. The deeper questions — what kind of bond forms and why, whether a molecule is polar, what three-dimensional shape it takes, and how separate molecules attract one another (intermolecular forces) — are the whole work of Module 3. For now, hold one idea: an atom's place on the table predicts how it will combine.
⚡ PREDICT FIRST
Sodium (Group 1) has one lonely valence electron. Chlorine (Group 17) is one electron short of a full shell. When the two meet, what do you predict happens? (Commit to a guess — you'll check it as you read.)

Recall the noble gases from the last lesson: almost completely unreactive, because their outermost shells are already full. That hints at something powerful. Atoms behave as though a full outer shell is a stable, comfortable place to be — and most atoms will gain, lose, or share electrons to reach an arrangement like a noble gas. For the main-group elements, that target is usually eight valence electrons, an idea chemists call the octet. (You'll meet the full story of bonding in Module 3; here we just need the driving force.)

An atom that gains or loses electrons is no longer electrically neutral — it becomes an ion. Lose one or more electrons and the atom is left with more protons than electrons, giving it a positive charge: a cation. Gain electrons and it has a surplus of negative charge: an anion. And here is the payoff from the periodic table: an element's group tells you, at a glance, how many electrons it tends to gain or lose — and therefore the charge of the ion it forms.

💡 WORTH KNOWING
The periodic trends from the last lesson explain why this works. Metals on the left have low ionization energy — their outer electrons are loosely held, so they let them go and become positive. Nonmetals on the right have high electron affinity — they pull electrons in, becoming negative. The table doesn't just sort the elements; it predicts which way the electrons will move. (The transition metals are less tidy — many can form more than one charge — so the clean group rule is for the main-group elements.)
📚 PART 2 · IONIC COMPOUNDS: BALANCING THE CHARGES

When a metal meets a nonmetal, electrons transfer: the metal becomes a cation, the nonmetal an anion, and the opposite charges pull together into an ionic compound. These are not really discrete molecules but vast, repeating three-dimensional arrays of alternating positive and negative ions — which is why we describe them with a formula unit, the simplest whole-number ratio of ions. There is one ironclad rule for that ratio: the total positive charge must exactly cancel the total negative charge, so the compound is electrically neutral.

Watch the logic on three examples:

Sodium + chlorine: Na+ (+1) and Cl− (−1). The charges already cancel one-to-one, so one of each → NaCl.
Magnesium + chlorine: Mg2+ (+2) and Cl− (−1). One chloride only cancels +1, so you need two chlorides to balance the +2 → MgCl2.
Aluminum + oxygen: Al3+ (+3) and O2− (−2). The smallest number both 3 and 2 divide into is 6, so you need two Al (2 × +3 = +6) and three O (3 × −2 = −6) → Al2O3.

Notice the shortcut hiding in that last one: the size of each ion's charge becomes the subscript of the other ion. This “crossover” is a quick check — but always reduce to the simplest whole-number ratio, and let the goal (a net charge of zero) be your guide. Try it yourself in the builder:

📚 PART 3 · MOLECULAR COMPOUNDS, POLYATOMIC IONS, AND TELLING THEM APART

When two nonmetals combine, neither one is willing to surrender electrons outright — both want to gain. So instead of transferring, they share electrons, and the shared pairs hold the atoms together as a true molecule. Compounds built this way are molecular (or covalent) compounds: water (H2O), carbon dioxide (CO2), and methane (CH4) are all molecules of nonmetals sharing electrons. They exist as separate, countable units — one water molecule, one carbon dioxide molecule — unlike the endless lattice of an ionic compound.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE · IS EVERY COMPOUND A MOLECULE?
No — and the vocabulary carries the idea. An ionic compound is not a molecule. The word we use tells you what kind of particle (or non-particle) you are really describing.
✗ NON-EXAMPLE — NaCl
There is no single “NaCl molecule.” A grain of salt is a vast lattice of alternating Na+ and Cl− ions. “NaCl” is a formula unit — the simplest whole-number ratio (one Na+ to one Cl−), not a discrete particle you could pluck out on its own.
✓ EXAMPLE — H2O
Water really does come in separate three-atom units: one H2O molecule can drift off entirely on its own. Here “H2O” names a real, countable particle — that is what makes it a molecule.
Quick rule: say “formula unit” for ionic compounds (metal + nonmetal, a lattice of ions) and “molecule” for covalent/molecular compounds (nonmetal + nonmetal, discrete shared-electron units).

There is one more piece worth meeting now: not every ion is a single atom. A polyatomic ion is a small group of atoms bonded together that carries an overall charge and travels as a unit. You will see these constantly, so it helps to recognize a few:

NitrateNO3−1

They balance charges the same way single ions do — you'll learn to name and combine them fully in Module 3. For now, just recognize that ammonium (NH4+) acts like a cation and the others act like anions.

So how do you tell, at a glance, which family a compound belongs to? Read the elements:

Ionic — metal + nonmetal
Electrons transfer; opposite charges attract. Examples: NaCl, MgCl2, CaO, Al2O3. (A metal or ammonium with a polyatomic ion counts too.)
Molecular — nonmetal + nonmetal
Electrons are shared; true molecules form. Examples: H2O, CO2, CH4, NH3.

Everything here is a preview of the next module. In Module 3 you'll go deeper into how the bonds actually form — ionic versus covalent — and you'll learn the full system for naming compounds and writing their formulas, including all the polyatomic ions. Don't try to memorize every name today. The one habit to carry forward is the reasoning: look at the elements, predict the ions from their groups, and let the charges balance to zero. Get that, and the rest of bonding will have a foundation to stand on.

▶  Think first, then check: Calcium is in Group 2 and fluorine is in Group 17. What ions do they form, and what is the formula of the compound they make?
Calcium (Group 2) loses two electrons to form Ca2+; fluorine (Group 17) gains one to form F−. To balance +2 you need two fluoride ions (2 × −1 = −2). The neutral formula is CaF2. Because calcium is a metal and fluorine a nonmetal, the compound is ionic.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Atoms reorganize their electrons to reach a stable, full-shell arrangement. Metals lose electrons to become cations; nonmetals gain them to become anions; the two attract into ionic compounds whose formula is whatever makes the charge zero. Nonmetals instead share electrons, forming molecular compounds. The element's address on the table predicts which path it takes — and that is the threshold of Module 3, where bonding becomes the whole story.
