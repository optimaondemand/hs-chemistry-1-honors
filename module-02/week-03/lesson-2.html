📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
There is a rule book for where every electron in an atom goes — what is it, and what does it let us predict?
Yesterday you learned that electrons live in orbitals with definite shapes and capacities (2, 6, 10, 14). Today you'll learn how nature fills those orbitals — in a specific order, following just three rules. The payoff is large: once you can write an atom's electron configuration, you can read off its valence electrons, predict how it will bond, and even explain the layout of the entire periodic table. This is the most “doing chemistry” lesson of the week.

📚 PART 1 · THREE RULES FOR FILLING ORBITALS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
If you were stacking electrons into the orbitals of an atom, where would you put the first ones? (No wrong answer — predict before you read.)

Electrons fill an atom's orbitals by following three rules. Learn these three and you can build any configuration:

  • The Aufbau principle (from the German for “building up”): electrons fill the lowest-energy orbital available before moving to a higher one — like water settling into the lowest spots first.
  • The Pauli exclusion principle: an orbital holds at most two electrons, and they must have opposite spins. No two electrons in an atom can have the identical set of all four quantum numbers.
  • Hund's rule: when several orbitals of the same energy are available (the three p orbitals, say), electrons spread out and occupy them singly, with parallel spins, before any orbital is forced to take a second electron. Electrons, like people on a bus, take an empty seat before doubling up.

The filling order

Because of how orbital energies overlap, the order is not simply 1, 2, 3 by shell. The standard sequence runs:

1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p …

Notice the famous twist: 4s fills before 3d, because the 4s orbital sits at slightly lower energy than 3d. That single quirk is why the periodic table has that block of transition metals tucked in the middle — we'll return to it in Part 3.

🔍 WHY 4s FILLS BEFORE 3d
Look at the two gold rungs. The 4s rung sits just below 3d — 4s is slightly lower in energy, even though its shell number (4) is higher than 3d's (3). Because electrons always take the lowest available rung first, 4s fills before 3d. That is why iron (Z = 26) is written [Ar] 4s2 3d6, and why the d-block — the transition metals — is tucked into the middle of the periodic table one shell “behind.” The ladder isn't a tidy 1, 2, 3 by shell precisely because of overlaps like this one.
💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW · READING A CONFIGURATION
A configuration is read in three parts. In 2p4: the 2 is the shell (n), the p is the subshell (the shape), and the superscript 4 is how many electrons sit in that subshell. Add up all the superscripts and you should get the atom's total number of electrons — a quick way to check your work.
📚 PART 2 · WRITING CONFIGURATIONS — WORKED EXAMPLES

Let's build a few. Use the filling order, respect the capacities (s holds 2, p holds 6, d holds 10), and stop when you've placed all the atom's electrons (its atomic number tells you how many).

Oxygen (Z = 8) — 8 electrons.
1s2 2s2 2p4  —  fill 1s (2), then 2s (2), then 2p with the remaining 4. Check: 2 + 2 + 4 = 8. ✓
Sodium (Z = 11) — 11 electrons.
1s2 2s2 2p6 3s1  —  the 10th electron fills 2p; the 11th starts the 3s. Check: 2 + 2 + 6 + 1 = 11. ✓
Chlorine (Z = 17) — 17 electrons.
1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p5  —  Check: 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 5 = 17. ✓
Iron (Z = 26) — 26 electrons (watch the 4s-before-3d order).
1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6 4s2 3d6  —  fill 4s (2) before starting 3d, then place the last 6 in 3d. Check: 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 = 26. ✓

Noble-gas shorthand

Full configurations get long fast. The shortcut is to replace the inner electrons with the symbol of the previous noble gas in square brackets, then write only what comes after. For sodium, the part 1s2 2s2 2p6 is exactly neon's configuration, so:

Na = 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s1  =  [Ne] 3s1   |   Cl = [Ne] 3s2 3p5   |   Fe = [Ar] 4s2 3d6

Orbital (box-and-arrow) diagrams

A configuration tells you how many electrons are in each subshell; an orbital diagram shows you how they're arranged, with each orbital a box and each electron an arrow (↑ or ↓) for its spin. Watch Hund's rule in oxygen's 2p4: the first three electrons go into the three p orbitals singly (↑ ↑ ↑), and only the fourth pairs up.

OXYGEN'S 2p SUBSHELL (2p4) — Hund's rule in action
↑↓ ↑ ↑
One orbital is paired (↑↓); the other two hold a single electron each. Spread out first, then pair.

📚 PART 3 · VALENCE ELECTRONS & THE SHAPE OF THE TABLE
💡 THE BIG IDEA · WHAT A CONFIGURATION REALLY IS
An electron configuration is not a string to memorize — it is a map of where an atom's electrons live, organized by energy level (the shell, n) and sublevel (s, p, d, f). Read that map and it hands you two powerful things. First, it shows the atom's valence electrons — the outermost electrons that do all the chemistry and decide how the atom bonds. Second, it pins the atom's exact address on the periodic table: the last sublevel being filled tells you the block, the highest shell tells you the row, and the valence count tells you the group. Configuration, valence, and periodic position are three views of the same fact — and that is what the rest of this lesson makes concrete.

The electrons in the outermost shell — the highest n — are called the valence electrons, and they are the ones that do chemistry. They are what an atom uses to bond. For sodium ([Ne] 3s1) there is 1 valence electron; for chlorine ([Ne] 3s2 3p5) there are 7 (the 3s and 3p electrons, since n = 3 is the outer shell). Counting valence electrons from a configuration is a skill you will lean on constantly in Module 3.

Here is the beautiful part. The periodic table is not arranged by accident — it is a map of electron configurations. Each region, or block, corresponds to the subshell being filled:

s-block
Groups 1–2 (filling an s subshell)
p-block
Groups 13–18 (filling a p subshell)
d-block
Transition metals (filling a d subshell)
f-block
The two rows below (filling an f subshell)

An element's position tells you its configuration, and its configuration tells you its position. The table you've used since grade school is, secretly, a picture of where electrons live.

Full: 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6 4s2 (check: 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 + 2 = 20 ✓). The 19th and 20th electrons fill 4s, which comes before 3d. Shorthand: [Ar] 4s2, since 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6 is exactly argon. Calcium has 2 valence electrons and sits in the s-block.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Three rules — fill lowest energy first (Aufbau), two per orbital with opposite spins (Pauli), spread out before pairing (Hund) — let you write the electron configuration of any atom. From it you can read the valence electrons that drive bonding, and you can see that the periodic table itself is a map of where electrons go.
