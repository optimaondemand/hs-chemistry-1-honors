📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why does hydrogen glow in only a few exact colors instead of a smooth rainbow?
Last lesson left us with a puzzle: an energized element emits not a full rainbow but a handful of precise, separate lines — and each element's set of lines is unique. The lines are evidence, and evidence demands explanation. Today you meet the model that cracked it: Niels Bohr's picture of the atom, in which electrons can occupy only certain fixed energy levels. It is one of the great moments in the long story of how we came to know the atom — and, true to that story, it is a model that works beautifully… right up to its edge.

📚 PART 1 · THE PUZZLE OF HYDROGEN'S LINES
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
Hydrogen has just one electron, yet an energized hydrogen tube glows with only a few exact colors — never a full rainbow. What do you predict is true about that one electron's energy? (No wrong answer — you'll test it in the simulation.)

Recall the evidence from Lesson 2.05: when hydrogen gas is energized, it emits a line spectrum — a small number of sharp, exact colors. If hydrogen's single electron could carry any amount of energy, the atom should be able to release light of any color, producing a smooth rainbow. It does not. The lines are discrete and reproducible. Something about the electron's energy must be restricted.

In 1913, the Danish physicist Niels Bohr proposed a bold answer. Picture the electron not as free to orbit at any distance, but as confined to certain allowed energy levels — like rungs on a ladder — labeled by a whole number n = 1, 2, 3, and so on. The electron can sit on a rung, but never between rungs. Its energy is quantized: only specific values are allowed.

📚 PART 2 · JUMPS BETWEEN LEVELS MAKE THE LINES

Here is the engine of the whole picture. When an electron absorbs a photon of exactly the right energy, it jumps up to a higher level. When it falls back down to a lower level, it releases a photon whose energy equals the gap between the two levels. Because the levels are fixed, the gaps are fixed — so only certain photon energies, and therefore only certain colors, can ever come out. That is why hydrogen's spectrum is a few exact lines and not a continuous band.

HYDROGEN ENERGY LEVELS — RUNGS GET CLOSER TOGETHER AS n RISES
n = 4  ·  −1.36 × 10−19 J
n = 3  ·  −2.42 × 10−19 J
n = 2  ·  −5.45 × 10−19 J
n = 1  ·  −2.18 × 10−18 J (ground state)
↑ absorb a photon = jump UP ↓ emit a photon = drop DOWN

Notice in the diagram that the rungs crowd closer together as n increases. The lowest level, n = 1, is the ground state — the electron's most stable, lowest-energy home. The energies are negative because the electron is bound to the nucleus; it would take energy to pull it free. For hydrogen, the energy of each level follows a clean formula:

En = −2.18 × 10−18 J ÷ n2
the allowed energy of hydrogen's electron at level n

You don't need to drill this formula — just read what it says: each level has one exact energy, set by the whole number n, and the levels squeeze together as n grows.

📚 PART 3 · A TRIUMPH — AND ITS EDGE

Bohr's model was a stunning success. Using only the idea of quantized levels, it predicted the exact wavelengths of hydrogen's spectral lines — the very lines that had been measured but never explained. For the first time, a picture of the atom matched the evidence precisely. It is hard to overstate how convincing that was.

And yet the model has a hard edge. It works for hydrogen and other one-electron systems, but it fails for atoms with more than one electron: its predictions for helium, lithium, and beyond simply do not match what we measure. A model that explains one case beautifully but breaks on the next is telling you something honest — that it has captured part of the truth, not all of it. That tension is exactly what drove physicists to build the next, deeper model of the atom, which you'll meet in the next lesson.

🔗 THE EVIDENCE CHAIN · WHY THE MODEL HAD TO CHANGE
Same three-step shape as always: an old explanation, the troubling evidence it could not account for, and the revised model. Trace it — then name the evidence yourself.
1 · OLD EXPLANATION
Rutherford's nuclear atom — electrons somewhere outside a tiny nucleus, arrangement unspecified. Nothing in it said which colors an atom should emit.
→
2 · TROUBLING EVIDENCE
Line spectra. Excite an element and it glows in only a few exact colors — sharp bright lines, not a full rainbow. Hydrogen's lines even followed a tidy numerical pattern no one could explain.
→
3 · REVISED MODEL
Bohr's model — electrons occupy only certain fixed energy levels; light is emitted only when an electron jumps between levels, so only certain colors can appear.
▶  Name the evidence: what observation could a plain nuclear atom not explain, but Bohr's energy levels could?
The discrete line spectrum — atoms emitting only certain exact colors. Fixed energy levels allow only certain jumps, so only certain photon energies (colors) are possible. Bohr's quantized levels reproduced hydrogen's measured lines exactly, which the featureless nuclear atom never could.
⚖️ MODEL LIMITATIONS · THE BOHR MODEL
✓ WHAT IT EXPLAINS
Why hydrogen (and other one-electron systems) emit only certain exact colors: the electron’s energy is quantized, so only certain level-to-level jumps — and photon energies — are possible.
✗ WHAT IT CANNOT EXPLAIN
The spectra of atoms with more than one electron (helium, lithium, and beyond); finer spectral detail; and why the levels are quantized at all. The tidy circular “orbit” is a useful picture, not the real atom.
🔬 EVIDENCE THAT FORCED REVISION
Multi-electron spectra that Bohr’s formula could not match, plus evidence that electrons behave as waves — pointing to the quantum-mechanical model (next lesson).
💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY
For decades, hydrogen's bright lines had been measured with great precision, and their spacing even followed a tidy numerical pattern — but why remained a mystery. In 1913, building on the nuclear atom that had just emerged from the gold-foil experiment, Niels Bohr proposed that the electron could occupy only certain allowed energies. The idea seemed almost reckless, yet it reproduced hydrogen's measured lines exactly. It was a turning point: the strange notion of quantized energy had earned its place because it matched the evidence.
⭐ HOW SCIENCE WORKS
The Bohr model is a perfect example of how scientific knowledge grows. A model is judged by whether it explains the evidence — and Bohr's did, for hydrogen. But when it failed for multi-electron atoms, scientists did not cling to it; they treated the failure as a clue and built something better. Models are tools, not final truths. A good one tells you where it works and where it breaks, and the breaking point is where the next discovery begins. You'll watch this happen again and again this year.

▶  Think first, then check: If the Bohr model explained hydrogen so well, why did scientists need a new model at all?
Because explaining hydrogen is not enough — a good model must work for all atoms. The Bohr model's predictions matched hydrogen (and other one-electron systems) but failed for atoms with two or more electrons, where the measured spectra didn't fit. That failure wasn't a small detail to ignore; it was evidence that the model was incomplete. Honest science follows the evidence to a better model, which is exactly what came next.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Bohr explained hydrogen's exact spectral lines with one daring idea: the electron can occupy only certain quantized energy levels. Absorbing a photon lifts it up; emitting one drops it down; and because the gaps are fixed, only certain colors appear. The model triumphed for hydrogen and failed for everything heavier — and that honest failure is what opens the door to the modern quantum picture of the atom, next.
