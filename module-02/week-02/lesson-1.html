📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How does a short string of letters and numbers — H2O, CO2, C6H12O6 — capture exactly what a substance is made of?
Last week you learned that atoms are real and that each element has its own symbol. Now we put those symbols to work. A chemical formula is the most compact language in all of science: in a handful of characters it tells you which elements are present and exactly how many atoms of each. Learn to read it — and to count from it — and you can speak the symbolic language of chemistry fluently.

📚 PART 1 · READING A MOLECULAR FORMULA
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
Glucose — the sugar your cells burn for energy — is written C6H12O6. Before reading on, how many atoms total do you think one molecule of glucose contains? (No wrong answer — you'll check it below.)

Every element has a one- or two-letter symbol — H for hydrogen, O for oxygen, Na for sodium, Fe for iron. (Notice the capitalization: the first letter is always uppercase and any second letter is always lowercase. Co is the element cobalt; CO is the compound carbon monoxide. The difference is not decoration — it changes the meaning entirely.)

A molecular formula uses those symbols, with small subscripts, to state the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule. Water is H2O: two hydrogen atoms, one oxygen atom. The subscript 2 belongs to the H right before it; the O has no subscript, so it is understood to be one. Carbon dioxide is CO2 — one carbon, two oxygen. Ethane is C2H6 — two carbon, six hydrogen. A subscript of 1 is never written; it is simply understood.

💡 WORTH KNOWING
Some elements never travel alone. Seven common elements occur naturally as diatomic molecules — pairs of identical atoms bonded together: hydrogen (H2), nitrogen (N2), oxygen (O2), fluorine (F2), chlorine (Cl2), bromine (Br2), and iodine (I2). So the oxygen you breathe is O2, not a lone O. The subscript tells you matter is often more “clumped” than its one-letter symbol suggests.
🔏 WHAT A FORMULA DOES — AND DOESN'T — TELL YOU
A chemical formula answers two questions and deliberately leaves a third for later. It tells you which elements are present and in what ratio their atoms combine — the composition of the substance. What a formula does not tell you is why those particular atoms join, what force holds them together, or what three-dimensional shape they take. Those questions — chemical bonding, molecular geometry, and polarity — are the whole subject of Module 3. For now your job is to read composition fluently; the “why” comes next, and it will build directly on the valence electrons you're about to study in the coming lessons.
📚 PART 2 · PARENTHESES, MULTIPLIERS, AND COUNTING CAREFULLY

Two more rules let you read any formula. First, parentheses group a set of atoms, and a subscript outside the parentheses multiplies everything inside. Calcium nitrate is Ca(NO3)2. The (NO3) is a group of one nitrogen and three oxygen atoms, and the 2 outside means there are two of those whole groups — so 1 calcium, 2 nitrogen, and 6 oxygen, for nine atoms in all. Second, a large number written in front of a formula — a coefficient — multiplies the entire formula. “3 H2O” means three whole water molecules: 6 hydrogen and 3 oxygen atoms.

HOW TO COUNT ATOMS IN Ca(NO3)2
Ca = 1 ➞ N inside = 1, × 2 outside = 2 ➞ O inside = 3, × 2 outside = 6 ➞ Total = 9 atoms
A subscript outside parentheses multiplies everything inside the parentheses.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
A subscript outside parentheses multiplies only the atoms inside those parentheses — not the atoms written elsewhere in the formula. In Al2(SO4)3, the “3” multiplies the S and the O inside, giving 3 sulfur and 12 oxygen — but it does not touch the aluminum, which stays at 2. Slow down and circle each group before you multiply.
📚 PART 3 · THREE KINDS OF FORMULA — AND IONIC FORMULA UNITS

A molecular formula tells you how many of each atom, but chemists actually use three different kinds of formula, each answering a different question.

Molecular formula
The actual count of each atom in one molecule. Glucose is C6H12O6; hydrogen peroxide is H2O2.
Structural formula
Shows which atom is bonded to which — the connectivity. For water, H–O–H shows both hydrogens attached to the central oxygen.
Empirical formula
The simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms. Glucose's empirical formula is CH2O; hydrogen peroxide's is HO.

To find an empirical formula from a molecular one, divide every subscript by the largest number that goes evenly into all of them. Glucose, C6H12O6, has subscripts 6, 12, and 6; the largest number dividing all three is 6, giving C1H2O1 — written CH2O. Hydrogen peroxide, H2O2, divides by 2 to give HO. When the subscripts already share no common factor — as in water, H2O — the molecular and empirical formulas are the same.

▶  Think first, then check: What is the empirical formula of butane, C4H10?
The subscripts are 4 and 10; the largest number that divides both evenly is 2. So 4÷2 = 2 and 10÷2 = 5, giving the empirical formula C2H5. Notice the empirical formula does not correspond to any real, separate molecule — it is just the simplest ratio. The molecule itself is still C4H10.

One last case matters. Ionic compounds — like table salt, NaCl — are not made of separate molecules at all. They are vast, repeating three-dimensional lattices of alternating charged particles (ions). There is no single “NaCl molecule” floating around; instead, the whole crystal is built from a 1-to-1 ratio of sodium to chlorine. So the formula NaCl is a formula unit: it states the simplest whole-number ratio of ions, not a count of atoms in a discrete molecule. Ionic formulas are therefore always written as empirical formulas.

🔄 TRANSLATE ACROSS THE THREE DOMAINS
Back in Lesson 1.01 you met chemistry's three views of matter — the macroscopic (what you see), the microscopic (the particles), and the symbolic (the written formula). A formula is the bridge between them. Read each row left to right to translate the same substance across all three, then count its atoms.
Macroscopic (what you'd see / name) Microscopic (one particle / unit) Symbolic (formula) Atoms per unit
Water — a clear liquid one molecule: 2 hydrogen bonded to 1 oxygen H2O 3 (2 H + 1 O)
Carbon dioxide — the gas you exhale one molecule: 1 carbon, 2 oxygen CO2 3 (1 C + 2 O)
Glucose — a sugar (white solid) one molecule: 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen, 6 oxygen C6H12O6 24 (6 + 12 + 6)
Table salt — a white crystal no molecule — a lattice in a 1-to-1 ratio of Na+ and Cl− ions NaCl (formula unit) 2 ions per formula unit (1 Na + 1 Cl)
Notice the last row is different: salt has no single “molecule,” so its formula unit gives a ratio of ions, not an atom count in a discrete particle. That distinction — molecule vs. formula unit — returns in Lesson 2.11.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A chemical formula is a compact, exact statement of what a substance is made of. Subscripts count atoms; parentheses group them; coefficients multiply whole formulas. A molecular formula gives the real count, a structural formula gives the connections, and an empirical formula gives the simplest ratio — the same form ionic compounds always take. With this language, you can now write down anything matter is made of.
