📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How do we count what's inside an atom — and why isn't an element's atomic mass on the periodic table a whole number?
You now know the atom has three kinds of parts. Today you learn the bookkeeping that turns that picture into numbers: how to count protons, neutrons, and electrons, how to read the symbol that names an atom exactly, and why elements come in slightly different-weight versions called isotopes. That last idea solves a small mystery you may have already noticed — that the masses on the periodic table are stubbornly not whole numbers. This is the lesson where chemistry becomes something you calculate.

📚 PART 1 · THE THREE PARTICLES, AND HOW TO COUNT THEM
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
The periodic table lists carbon's atomic mass as about 12.01, not a clean 12. What's your best guess for why a whole element would have a decimal mass? (No wrong answer — you'll find out below.)

An atom has three kinds of particles, and their properties are worth knowing cold. The proton carries a charge of +1 and a mass of about 1 atomic mass unit (amu); it sits in the nucleus. The neutron is electrically neutral (charge 0) with a mass also of about 1 amu; it too sits in the nucleus. The electron carries a charge of −1 but is tiny — only about 1/1836 amu — and it occupies the space outside the nucleus. Because electrons are so light, essentially all of an atom's mass lives in its nucleus.

ParticleChargeMass (approx.)Location
Proton+11 amuNucleus
Neutron01 amuNucleus
Electron−1≈ 1/1836 amuOutside the nucleus

Two counts organize everything. The atomic number, written Z, is the number of protons — and it is what defines the element. Every carbon atom has 6 protons; change the proton count and you change the element. The mass number, written A, is the total number of protons plus neutrons (the heavy particles in the nucleus). So a quick rearrangement gives you the neutron count whenever you need it:

protons = Z  ·  neutrons = A − Z  ·  in a neutral atom, electrons = protons = Z

🔬 HOW DO WE KNOW? · KNOWING PARTICLES WE CANNOT SEE
No one has ever seen a proton or an electron — they are far smaller than any wavelength of visible light, so no microscope can ever show one. How, then, can a table state their charge, mass, and location so confidently? Every entry was reasoned out from something an instrument could measure. The electron's negative charge came from how a cathode ray bends in an electric field (Thomson); its tiny mass from balanced oil drops (Millikan); the dense nucleus from particles that bounce off gold foil (Rutherford); and, as you'll see in the next lessons, the arrangement of electrons from the exact colors of light atoms emit (spectroscopy). We know the invisible the way a detective knows an unseen suspect — by the evidence it cannot help leaving behind.
📚 PART 2 · ISOTOPES, IONS, AND THE SYMBOL THAT SAYS IT ALL

Here is where Dalton's second postulate got refined (remember Lesson 2.01). Atoms of the same element always have the same number of protons — but they need not have the same number of neutrons. Atoms of one element that differ in neutron count are called isotopes. They share identical chemistry (same protons, same electrons) but differ in mass. Carbon, for instance, is almost always carbon-12 (6 protons, 6 neutrons), but a small fraction of carbon atoms are carbon-14 (6 protons, 8 neutrons) — the very isotope that makes radiocarbon dating possible.

Chemists capture all of this in a compact isotope symbol: the element's symbol with the mass number A as a superscript and the atomic number Z as a subscript, both written to the left. Names like “carbon-12” carry the same information in words.

14 6 C
Top (A = 14): mass number — total protons + neutrons.
Bottom (Z = 6): atomic number — number of protons (this is carbon).
Neutrons: A − Z = 14 − 6 = 8.
Read aloud: “carbon-14.”

Finally, atoms can gain or lose electrons (never protons — that would change the element) and become charged. A charged atom is an ion. Lose one or more electrons and you have more protons than electrons, giving a positive charge: a cation. Gain electrons and you have a negative charge: an anion. To count electrons in an ion, start from the proton count and adjust for the charge:

electrons = protons − (charge)   →   e.g., a +2 ion has 2 fewer electrons than protons; a −1 ion has 1 more.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
A positive charge means the atom lost electrons, not gained protons. It's easy to think “positive→more positives,” but protons never change in ordinary chemistry — that's reserved for nuclear reactions. A sodium ion, Na+, still has 11 protons; it simply has 10 electrons instead of 11. The charge comes from the imbalance, and the imbalance comes from the electrons.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLES · READING A NUCLIDE SYMBOL, STEP BY STEP
The same two rules do all the work every time: the bottom number (Z) is the protons and names the element; the top number (A) minus Z is the neutrons; electrons equal protons, then adjust for any charge. Follow all three, then try the set below yourself.
199F  (a neutral atom)
Protons = Z = 9 → 9 protons means the element is fluorine.
Neutrons = A − Z = 19 − 9 = 10.
Electrons = protons (neutral) = 9.
2713Al3+  (a cation)
Protons = Z = 13 → aluminum.
Neutrons = 27 − 13 = 14.
Electrons: charge is +3, so it lost 3 electrons → 13 − 3 = 10.
3216S2−  (an anion)
Protons = Z = 16 → sulfur.
Neutrons = 32 − 16 = 16.
Electrons: charge is −2, so it gained 2 electrons → 16 + 2 = 18.
Worth noticing: both ions above end with a familiar electron count — Al3+ with 10, S2− with 18 — the same counts as the nearest noble gases (neon, argon). That is not a coincidence, and it becomes the key to bonding in Module 3.

📚 PART 3 · WHY ATOMIC MASSES AREN'T WHOLE NUMBERS

First, the unit. The atomic mass unit (amu) is defined by international agreement so that one atom of carbon-12 weighs exactly 12 amu. Every other atomic mass is measured relative to that standard. So a single proton or neutron is about 1 amu, and a carbon-12 atom (6 + 6) is about 12 amu — a tidy whole number, as you'd hope.

So why does the periodic table say carbon is 12.01, and chlorine is 35.45? Because the number on the table is not the mass of any single atom — it is the average atomic mass: the average mass of the element's atoms as they actually occur in nature, weighted by how common each isotope is. An element that is mostly a light isotope with a trace of a heavier one will average close to the light one; an even mix lands in between. The decimals are not error — they are a faithful record of nature's isotope mixture.

Chlorine, worked step by step

Chlorine occurs as two isotopes: chlorine-35 (mass 34.969 amu, 75.77% abundant) and chlorine-37 (mass 36.966 amu, 24.23% abundant). To average by weight of abundance, multiply each isotope's mass by its abundance (as a decimal) and add:

(0.7577 × 34.969 amu) + (0.2423 × 36.966 amu)
= 26.50 amu + 8.957 amu
= 35.45 amu

Notice the answer sits much closer to 35 than to 37 — exactly as it should, because chlorine-35 is about three times as common. The average “leans” toward whichever isotope is more abundant. That is the whole idea of a weighted average, and it is why no chlorine atom actually weighs 35.45 amu: that number describes the mixture, not any individual atom.

▶  Think first, then check: An element has two isotopes, mass 10 and mass 11. Without calculating, will its average be closer to 10 or to 11 if the lighter isotope is 80% abundant?
Closer to 10. The average always leans toward the more abundant isotope, and the mass-10 isotope makes up 80% of the atoms. (Working it out: 0.80×10 + 0.20×11 = 8.0 + 2.2 = 10.2 amu — just above 10, exactly as predicted. This element is boron, whose true average is about 10.81.)
⭐ NOW & NEXT · ISOTOPES AT WORK TODAY
Isotopes are not just a bookkeeping detail — they are working tools of modern science. Hospitals use medical isotopes to image the body and treat disease; geologists read the steady decay of certain isotopes to date rocks and fossils; and researchers use the same mass-spectrometer idea from this lesson to identify substances by their exact masses. Many of these isotopes don't occur naturally at all — scientists make them in particle accelerators, which are also how the newest, heaviest elements on the periodic table are created, one atom at a time. The counting you practiced today is the foundation of an active, fast-moving field.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

The proton count (Z) names the element; protons plus neutrons (A) give the mass number; electrons balance the charge, and their imbalance makes ions. Isotopes are same-element atoms with different neutron counts — and because elements occur as a mixture of isotopes, the periodic table reports a weighted average mass, which is why those masses aren't whole numbers. You can now count and name any atom, and calculate the number nature actually hands you.
