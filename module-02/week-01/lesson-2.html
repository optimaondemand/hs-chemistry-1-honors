📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If atoms are real, what are they made of — and how did experiments crack open a particle that was supposed to be “uncuttable”?
Dalton handed chemistry a solid, indivisible sphere. Within a single century, that sphere was opened, weighed, and rebuilt — not by argument, but by a series of strikingly clever experiments, each one forcing the picture to change. Today you'll follow that chain of evidence: the discovery of the electron, the measurement of its charge, the astonishing scattering experiment that revealed the nucleus, and the particle that completed the picture. Watch how each model survives only until better evidence overturns it.

📚 PART 1 · THOMSON FINDS A PIECE OF THE ATOM

⚖️ MODEL LIMITATIONS · DALTON’S SOLID SPHERE
✓ WHAT IT EXPLAINS
Matter is made of atoms; the atoms of one element are alike; they combine in fixed whole-number ratios; mass is conserved in a reaction.
✗ WHAT IT CANNOT EXPLAIN
Electricity and charged particles; why elements differ from one another; and, above all, how a “solid, indivisible” atom could have anything inside it.
🔬 EVIDENCE THAT FORCED REVISION
Thomson’s cathode ray — a negative particle common to every metal and far lighter than any atom. The sphere had to be opened.

The first crack in Dalton's solid sphere came in the late 1890s from J. J. Thomson, working with a device called a cathode-ray tube — a sealed glass tube with most of the air pumped out and a high voltage applied across two metal plates inside. A mysterious ray traveled from the negative plate (the cathode) to the positive plate. Thomson showed that this ray was bent by electric and magnetic fields, always toward the positive side, which meant the ray carried negative charge. By measuring exactly how much the fields bent it, he determined the ray's charge-to-mass ratio — and found that whatever these particles were, they were astonishingly light, nearly two thousand times lighter than the lightest atom.

Most importantly, the ray behaved the same way no matter which metal he used for the cathode. The conclusion was inescapable and revolutionary: he had found a particle smaller than the atom itself, common to all matter. We now call it the electron. The “uncuttable” atom had been cut. To make sense of it, Thomson proposed the plum-pudding model: a ball of positive charge with tiny negative electrons scattered through it like raisins in a pudding, so that the whole atom comes out electrically neutral.

Thomson’s plum-pudding model
electrons (light dots) embedded in a positive sphere
The plum-pudding model was a genuine improvement: it accounted for the electron and for the atom being neutral overall. But notice what it assumes — that the positive charge and mass are spread smoothly throughout the whole atom. That single assumption was about to be tested, and broken.
⚖️ MODEL LIMITATIONS · THOMSON’S PLUM PUDDING
✓ WHAT IT EXPLAINS
The atom contains small negative electrons, and it is electrically neutral overall (electrons embedded in a matching positive charge).
✗ WHAT IT CANNOT EXPLAIN
How a fast, heavy particle could ever bounce sharply backward from an atom — a smooth, thin blob of charge has nothing dense enough to turn it around.
🔬 EVIDENCE THAT FORCED REVISION
Rutherford’s gold foil (next section): a few alpha particles came almost straight back — impossible for a smooth pudding.
📚 PART 2 · MILLIKAN WEIGHS THE ELECTRON

Thomson had measured the electron's charge-to-mass ratio, but not the charge or the mass on its own. Around 1909, Robert Millikan closed that gap with one of the most elegant experiments in all of physics: the oil-drop experiment. He sprayed a fine mist of oil droplets between two charged plates. Some droplets picked up a few extra electrons and so carried a tiny electric charge; by adjusting the voltage, Millikan could balance a single droplet in mid-air, with the upward electric force exactly canceling gravity. From that balance he could calculate the charge on each droplet.

The result was the beautiful part. Every charge he measured turned out to be a whole-number multiple of one small basic amount — you never found half of it, or 1.7 of it, only 1×, 2×, 3× that fundamental unit. That smallest unit had to be the charge of a single electron. Once he had the electron's charge, he could combine it with Thomson's charge-to-mass ratio and finally pin down the electron's mass — roughly 1/1836 the mass of a hydrogen atom. (Notice the echo of Lesson 2.01: just as whole-number mass ratios revealed atoms, whole-number charges revealed that electric charge itself comes in countable pieces.)

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
Why two experiments for one particle? Because some quantities can only be reached indirectly, by combining separate measurements. Thomson could get the ratio of charge to mass; Millikan could get the charge. Neither alone gives the mass — but together they do. This is a common move in science: when you can't measure the thing you want directly, you measure two things you can, and let the math deliver the third.
📚 PART 3 · RUTHERFORD FINDS THE NUCLEUS

Now comes the experiment you predicted about. Around 1909–1911, in Ernest Rutherford's lab, researchers aimed a beam of alpha particles (small, fast, positively charged particles) at an extremely thin sheet of gold foil, with a detecting screen around it to record where the particles went. If Thomson's plum pudding were right — positive charge smeared thinly everywhere — the alpha particles should have sailed almost straight through, barely nudged.

Most of them did exactly that. But a tiny fraction were deflected at large angles, and a very few bounced almost straight back. Rutherford famously described it as nearly as incredible as firing a shell at tissue paper and having it come back at you. A thin, smooth pudding could never turn a fast particle around. The only way to explain those rare, violent bounce-backs was this: nearly all of the atom's mass and all of its positive charge are packed into a tiny, dense core — the nucleus — while the rest of the atom is mostly empty space, with the light electrons far outside. Most alpha particles missed the tiny nucleus entirely (empty space); the rare few that scored a near-direct hit were flung back.

🔬 HOW SCIENTISTS KNOW · ONE SURPRISE CAN TOPPLE A MODEL
This is the same habit of mind you met in Lesson 2.01, now in fast motion. A respected model — Thomson's plum pudding, proposed by a Nobel laureate — was overturned by a handful of particles that did the “impossible.” Rutherford did not ignore the strange result or explain it away; he trusted the evidence and let it rebuild the model. Models evolve as evidence improves — and sometimes a single unexpected observation, taken seriously, is enough to demand a brand-new picture of reality.
🔬 FROM OBSERVATION TO INFERENCE · THREE EXPERIMENTS THAT REBUILT THE ATOM
No one ever saw an electron or a nucleus. Each part of the atom was inferred — reasoned out from something an instrument could actually record. Read across each row: the experiment, the raw observation, and the inference it forced.
Experiment What was observed (recorded by the instrument) What it let scientists infer
Thomson's cathode-ray tube
(1890s)		 The ray always bends toward the positive plate, and behaves identically no matter which metal is used. Atoms contain a negatively charged particle — far lighter than any atom and common to all matter (the electron). The “indivisible” atom has parts.
Millikan's oil-drop
(≈1909)		 Every droplet's charge is a whole-number multiple of one tiny amount — never a fraction of it. Charge comes in discrete units; the smallest is the charge of one electron. Combined with Thomson's ratio, this gives the electron's mass.
Rutherford's gold foil
(1909–1911)		 Most alpha particles pass straight through, but a rare few bounce almost straight back. The atom is mostly empty space, with nearly all its mass and all its positive charge in a tiny, dense nucleus.
Notice the two-step logic in every row: the observation is what the instrument recorded; the inference is the reasoning step the scientist added. Learning to keep those two apart — and to ask whether an inference is really forced by the observation — is one of the most important habits in science.
📚 PART 4 · CHADWICK COMPLETES THE NUCLEUS

Rutherford's nuclear atom had one loose end. The positive particles in the nucleus — the protons — could not account for the whole mass of most atoms; the nucleus seemed heavier than its protons alone should make it. Rutherford suspected a second, electrically neutral particle was hiding there, but a neutral particle is hard to detect precisely because it carries no charge to push on. In 1932, James Chadwick finally identified it through careful experiments: the neutron, with nearly the same mass as a proton but no charge. With the neutron in hand, the modern picture of the atom was essentially complete: a tiny nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by a cloud of electrons in mostly empty space.

Rutherford’s model deliberately left one question open: exactly how are those electrons arranged? It is tempting to picture them circling the nucleus like planets around the sun, each on a fixed track — but hold that image loosely, because it turns out to be wrong. The nuclear atom could not say why electrons don’t simply spiral into the nucleus, nor why each element glows in only a few exact colors. Those puzzles drove the next two lessons: Bohr’s energy levels (a first, historically important attempt at “orbits”), and finally the modern quantum model, in which the electron has no fixed path at all — only a cloud of probability.

▶  Think first, then check: If most alpha particles passed straight through the gold foil, what does that tell you about how much of an atom is empty space?
It tells you the atom is mostly empty space. If the atom were solidly packed with matter everywhere, the alpha particles would have been stopped or strongly deflected over and over. Instead, the vast majority sailed straight through, meeting nothing — which means the mass and positive charge must be concentrated in a very small region (the nucleus), with enormous empty space around it.
🔗 THE EVIDENCE CHAIN · WHY THE MODEL HAD TO CHANGE

Dalton's solid sphere — a tiny, uniform, indivisible ball with no internal parts.

2 · TROUBLING EVIDENCE
Cathode rays, then gold-foil bounce-backs. Thomson's tube revealed a charged particle far smaller than the atom; then a few alpha particles rebounded almost straight back off gold foil — impossible if positive charge were spread thinly.

3 · REVISED MODEL
Rutherford's nuclear atom — a minute, dense, positive nucleus holding nearly all the mass, with electrons far outside in mostly empty space.
▶  Name the evidence: which single result made a smooth, uniform atom impossible?
The rare large-angle bounce-backs in the gold-foil experiment. A diffuse “plum-pudding” blob of charge could never turn a fast alpha particle straight back; only charge and mass concentrated in a tiny nucleus could. That one observation forced the nuclear atom.
⚖️ MODEL LIMITATIONS · RUTHERFORD’S NUCLEAR ATOM
✓ WHAT IT EXPLAINS
Nearly all the mass and all the positive charge sit in a tiny, dense nucleus; the atom is mostly empty space. This accounts for the gold-foil scattering.
✗ WHAT IT CANNOT EXPLAIN
How the electrons are arranged, why they don’t spiral into the nucleus, and why each element emits only a few exact colors. It does not mean electrons circle like planets on fixed tracks.
🔬 EVIDENCE THAT FORCED REVISION
The sharp line spectra of the elements — light of only certain colors — which Bohr’s quantized energy levels (next lesson) were built to explain.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

In one century, the “uncuttable” atom was opened and rebuilt: Thomson found the electron, Millikan weighed it, Rutherford discovered the nucleus, and Chadwick added the neutron. At every step a clever experiment produced evidence that the old model could not survive — and the model changed. The atom is now a tiny dense nucleus of protons and neutrons in a vast, mostly empty space patrolled by electrons. Next, we learn to count exactly what's inside.
