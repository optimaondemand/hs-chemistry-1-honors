Module 8 Writing Assignment
Powering the Future — A Battery Argument
Word (.docx) · 100 pts · teacher-reviewed
📚 THE TASK
Every battery is a galvanic cell — a spontaneous redox reaction packaged to push electrons through a circuit. But no single chemistry is best for everything: a phone, a car, and a power grid each demand different things. In this assignment you will argue which battery (or cell) chemistry best fits a stated use case, defending your choice with the electrochemistry — cell potential, the redox couple, rechargeability, energy density — and then weighing the real costs and benefits with the honesty the question deserves.
This is a scientific argument: a claim, defended with evidence and reasoning, and accountable to the trade-offs. Choose one use case below, then choose a cell chemistry to advocate for it.
USE CASE A · ELECTRIC VEHICLE
A car needs high energy density (lots of energy per kilogram), rechargeability, a long cycle life, and reasonably fast charging. Weight matters a great deal.
USE CASE B · GRID / HOME SOLAR STORAGE
A storage bank needs low cost, a long cycle life, safety, and large capacity. It sits still, so weight matters far less.
USE CASE C · SIMPLE DISPOSABLE DEVICE
A TV remote or wall clock needs a cheap, reliable, shelf-stable cell with low energy demand. Recharging isn't needed.
You'll write it in Microsoft Word, organized into the argument sections listed further down.
⭐ WHY THIS MATTERS
This argument is one of only two teacher-reviewed pieces in Module 8 (the other is your Electrochemical Cell & Rate Build VR project). Your teacher reads it personally and responds to your reasoning — so make it your careful best, and justify your claim from the electrochemistry, not from a guess. This is exactly Quarter 4's question in action: how does chemistry shape our world? — and its virtue, Responsibility: chemistry's power comes with real costs.
🧮 YOUR TOOLKIT (USE THESE EXACT IDEAS & VALUES)
A battery is a galvanic cell: a spontaneous redox reaction drives electrons from the anode (oxidation) to the cathode (reduction) through the circuit. A positive E°cell drives the voltage; E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode, and E°cell > 0 means spontaneous (ΔG° = −nFE° < 0).
Primary vs secondary: a primary cell is single-use (the redox reaction isn't practically reversible); a secondary cell is rechargeable (an outside current drives the reaction backward — electrolysis — to restore the reactants).
Energy density ≠ voltage: voltage (volts) is the “push” per electron; energy density is how much energy is stored per kilogram (or liter). A cell can have a modest voltage but high energy density, or vice versa — don't confuse them.
Alkaline cell (primary): Zn anode / MnO2 cathode, about 1.5 V; cheap, shelf-stable, not rechargeable.
Lead-acid (secondary): about 2 V per cell; six cells ≈ 12 V (car battery); cheap and reliable but heavy (low energy density).
Lithium-ion (secondary): high voltage and high energy density, many recharge cycles — the standard for phones and EVs; uses lithium, cobalt, and nickel.
Hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell: not a fixed-charge battery but a cell fed continuous reactants: 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O; the only product is water.
🧠 YOUR REASONING TOOLKIT (how to build the argument)
Use these steps in your own words — don't just copy the headings.
Step 1 — Pin down the use case and what it demands.
Name the use case (A, B, or C) and list the two or three properties that matter most for it (e.g., for the EV: high energy density and rechargeability; for the grid: low cost and long cycle life).
Step 2 — Pick a chemistry and tie its electrochemistry to those demands.
Choose lithium-ion, lead-acid, alkaline, or an H2–O2 fuel cell. Explain it as a galvanic cell (the redox couple, that it's spontaneous, why it's primary or secondary) and connect that chemistry to what the use case needs.
Step 3 — Weigh the costs honestly, then decide.
No chemistry is free of trade-offs: materials sourcing (e.g., lithium and cobalt mining), lifespan, recyclability, safety, and environmental cost. Acknowledge the strongest objection to your choice, then explain why your chemistry is still the best fit — reasoning from evidence, not preference.
📝 STRUCTURE: WRITE THESE SECTIONS
1
Claim. One or two clear sentences naming your use case and the chemistry you say is best for it (e.g., “For an electric vehicle, a lithium-ion battery is the best chemistry,” or “For grid storage, lead-acid is the more responsible choice.”).
2
Electrochemical evidence. Lay out the chemistry of your cell: that it's a galvanic cell running a spontaneous redox reaction (name the redox couple or the half-reactions if you can), its approximate voltage, whether it's primary or secondary (and what that means for recharging), and its energy density. Use the toolkit values — and keep voltage and energy density distinct.
3
Reasoning. This is the heart of the argument. Connect the evidence to the claim step by step: this use case needs X; my chemistry provides X because of its electrochemistry; therefore it fits. Compare against at least one rival chemistry and explain why yours wins for this case (e.g., why high energy density makes lithium-ion right for an EV but is wasted on a wall clock).
4
Cost / benefit & responsibility. Weigh the real-world costs and benefits across all three dimensions — materials (sourcing and recyclability), economics (cost to make and to run), and environment & safety (impact and hazards) — alongside lifespan. Name the strongest objection to your choice and answer it. Reason from evidence, not preference (Honesty thread).
5
Conclusion. Restate your claim in light of the evidence and the trade-offs, and name the big idea: a battery is chemistry put to work, and choosing one well means matching the redox chemistry to the job — and owning its costs.
✎ LENGTH & STYLE
Aim for roughly 500–700 words across the five sections. Use correct terminology — galvanic cell, oxidation/reduction, anode/cathode, cell potential (E°cell), primary/secondary cell, energy density. The chemistry and the reasoning are what's graded — not polished prose. Justify your claim with the electrochemistry, not a hunch, and keep voltage and energy density separate.
⚠️ A NOTE ON USING AI TOOLS
An AI tool will happily recommend a battery — and it can get the chemistry wrong: overstating a cell's specs, inventing a voltage or an energy-density figure, or confusing energy density with voltage as if they were the same number. If you consult one, treat it as a draft to check, never an answer to paste: verify every electrochemical claim against this assignment's toolkit values and the redox reasoning, and re-derive that E°cell > 0 means a spontaneous (galvanic) cell yourself. The instrument proposes; your reasoning against the electrochemistry verifies. That is exactly the AI-literacy skill this assignment is built to grow.
