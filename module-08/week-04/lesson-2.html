Rate Laws & Collision Theory
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You learned last lesson that raising a reactant's concentration speeds the reaction up. But by how much? Double the concentration of one reactant and sometimes the rate doubles — and sometimes it quadruples, and sometimes it doesn't change at all. How can the same doubling produce three different results?
Last lesson gave you the five levers that change a reaction's speed. Today we make the concentration lever quantitative. The tool is the rate law, an equation that says exactly how rate depends on each reactant's concentration. Its most important — and most often misunderstood — feature is that you cannot read it off the balanced equation; you must find it from experimental data. We'll learn to do that with the method of initial rates, then look beneath the equation at collision theory: the picture of particles that must collide with enough energy and the right orientation for a reaction to occur at all.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…

I will be able to write a rate law in the form rate = k[A]m[B]n, and explain that the orders m and n come from experiment, not from the balanced equation.
2
I will be able to use the method of initial rates to find the order in each reactant, the overall order, and the rate constant k.
3
I will be able to state the collision theory — a reaction needs collisions with enough energy (the activation energy, Ea) and the correct orientation — and use it to explain why temperature has such a strong effect.

📚 PART 1 · THE RATE LAW — AND WHY YOU CAN'T READ IT OFF THE EQUATION
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
For the reaction 2 NO + O2 → 2 NO2, a student says “the equation has a 2 in front of NO, so doubling [NO] must double the rate.” Do you trust that reasoning? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

A rate law is an equation that says exactly how a reaction's rate depends on the concentrations of its reactants. For a reaction of A and B it has the form:

THE RATE LAW
rate = k[A]m[B]n
k is the rate constant; m and n are the reaction orders, found from experiment

The exponents m and n are called the reaction orders. The order in A tells you how sensitive the rate is to [A]; the order in B does the same for [B]. Their sum, m + n, is the overall order of the reaction. Here is the rule that matters most in all of kinetics:

THE CENTRAL IDEA
Reaction orders come from EXPERIMENT, not from coefficients
never copy the exponents off the balanced equation — measure them

It is tempting to copy the coefficients from the balanced equation into the rate law, but that is a trap. The balanced equation describes the overall bookkeeping of atoms; the rate law describes the actual fastest step of the reaction, which often looks nothing like the overall equation. The only honest way to get m and n is to run experiments and watch how the rate responds. The k in the equation is the rate constant: a number specific to that reaction at a given temperature. It is not a concentration — it is a fixed proportionality that increases when you raise the temperature.

▶  Think first, then check: A reaction has the rate law rate = k[A][B]2. What is its overall order, and what is the order in B?
The order in B is 2 (the exponent on [B]). The order in A is 1 (an unwritten exponent of 1). The overall order is 1 + 2 = 3 — third order. Because the order in B is 2, doubling [B] would multiply the rate by 22 = 4; because the order in A is 1, doubling [A] would simply double the rate. Notice none of this could be guessed from a balanced equation — the exponents had to be measured.
📚 PART 2 · THE METHOD OF INITIAL RATES

How do chemists actually find the orders? They run several trials of the reaction, each time changing one reactant's starting concentration while holding the others fixed, and measure the initial rate of each. By comparing trials, the response of the rate to that one concentration reveals its order:

When you double one reactant's concentration (everything else fixed):
• the rate doubles (×2) → first order in that reactant (m = 1)
• the rate quadruples (×4) → second order (m = 2)
• the rate does not change (×1) → zero order (m = 0)

The logic is just exponents at work: doubling a concentration multiplies the rate by 2 raised to that reactant's order. Since 21 = 2, 22 = 4, and 20 = 1, the factor by which the rate changes tells you the order directly.

A worked example: finding the rate law from data

For the reaction A + B → products, three trials are run:

Trial [A] (M) [B] (M) Initial rate (M/s)
10.100.100.010
20.200.100.020
30.100.200.040
Order in A — compare trials 1 and 2 ([B] fixed): [A] doubles (0.10 → 0.20) and the rate doubles (0.010 → 0.020). Rate ×2 → first order in A (m = 1).

Order in B — compare trials 1 and 3 ([A] fixed): [B] doubles (0.10 → 0.20) and the rate quadruples (0.010 → 0.040). Rate ×4 → second order in B (n = 2).

Rate law: rate = k[A][B]2 — overall order = 1 + 2 = 3 (third order).

Solve for k using Trial 1: 0.010 = k(0.10)(0.10)2 = k(0.10)(0.010) = k(0.0010).
So k = 0.010 / 0.0010 = 10 M−2·s−1.
🤖 AI AS AN INSTRUMENT · VERIFY, DON'T TRUST
Ask an AI assistant for the rate law of a reaction and it will very often hand back the coefficients of the balanced equation as the orders — the exact trap you just learned to avoid. AI is a useful instrument for setting up a problem, but it cannot know the orders without the experimental data, and it has no way to verify what it asserts. The orders must come from the data table, and you confirm them by hand — compare the trials, watch how the rate responds, and check that your k comes out the same from more than one trial. Treat the AI's answer as a claim to test against the evidence, never as the evidence itself.

📚 PART 3 · COLLISION THEORY — WHY ANY OF THIS WORKS

Beneath the rate law sits a physical picture called collision theory. Its claim is simple: a reaction happens only when reactant particles actually collide — but not just any collision will do. A collision leads to reaction only if it meets two conditions:

1. Enough energy. The colliding particles must carry at least a minimum energy — the activation energy, Ea — to break the old bonds and start forming new ones.
2. Correct orientation. The particles must be aimed so the right atoms meet. A glancing or wrongly turned collision bounces off without reacting.

Most collisions fail one test or the other, which is why reactions are slower than the sheer collision frequency would suggest. This explains the levers from last lesson: more concentration means more collisions; more surface area means more contact; and a catalyst (Lesson 8.10) lowers Ea so a larger fraction of collisions clear the bar.

Temperature deserves special attention. Raising the temperature does more than speed particles up — it sharply increases the fraction of collisions that carry at least Ea. This is captured by the Arrhenius equation, k = A·e(−Ea/RT), which shows the rate constant k climbing steeply as T rises. That steep climb is exactly the outsized temperature effect Arrhenius first noticed — and the reason a small temperature change can have a large effect on rate.

💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY
In 1889 the Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius turned the outsized effect of temperature into a single equation. Studying how reaction rates climbed as solutions were warmed, he proposed that only molecules carrying at least a certain minimum energy — what we now call the activation energy — can react, and that heating a mixture sharply raises the fraction of molecules clearing that bar. His relationship, k = A·e(−Ea/RT), tied the rate constant to temperature and to that energy barrier, and it still carries his name. The same habit of turning careful measurements into a predictive equation later led Arrhenius to reason quantitatively about how carbon dioxide in the atmosphere might warm the planet — an early example of chemistry making testable predictions about the wider world. (You may remember Arrhenius from acids and bases; this is his separate, earlier work on the speed of reactions.)

🔮 NOW · WHY THIS QUESTION STILL MATTERS
Rate laws and activation energies are not just textbook exercises — they are how scientists predict the shelf life of a medicine, the speed of a pollutant breaking down in the atmosphere, or how quickly a new fuel will combust. Researchers are currently using fast lasers and computer models to watch reactions unfold and to estimate activation energies for reactions too quick or too dangerous to time by hand, sharpening the very kind of rate law you built today.
⚡ TRY IT · TRUE OR FALSE ABOUT RATE LAWS & COLLISIONS
Decide whether each statement is true or false. You'll get instant feedback on each.
1. You can read the reaction orders straight off the coefficients in the balanced equation.
2. If doubling [A] makes the rate four times larger, the reaction is second order in A.
3. For a collision to lead to reaction, the particles need both enough energy and the correct orientation.
4. The rate constant k stays exactly the same no matter the temperature.
▶  Think first, then check: Why does a modest rise in temperature speed a reaction up so much more than the same rise in concentration would?
Raising concentration only adds more collisions; raising temperature does two things at once — it makes collisions more frequent, and it sharply raises the fraction of those collisions that carry at least the activation energy Ea. Because that fraction grows steeply (the Arrhenius e(−Ea/RT) term), a small temperature increase can push many more collisions over the energy barrier — a much bigger effect than simply crowding more particles in.
🧠 NATURE OF SCIENCE · HOW DO WE KNOW?
Notice what kind of knowledge a reaction order is. You cannot deduce it by staring at the balanced equation, however reasonable that feels — you have to run the experiment and let the data decide. Reaction orders are empirical: they are measured, and if better measurements ever disagreed with an accepted order, the order would have to be revised. Collision theory is a different kind of knowledge again — it is a model, a mental picture of colliding particles that explains why the levers work and why temperature matters so much. A good model earns its keep by explaining and predicting, but it remains a human construction, and scientists keep refining it where it falls short. Holding these two apart — measured evidence on one side, explanatory model on the other — is a large part of what it means to think like a scientist.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A rate law, rate = k[A]m[B]n, says exactly how rate depends on concentration — and its orders m and n must be found from experiment, never copied from coefficients. Using the method of initial rates, double one concentration at a time: a ×2 response is first order, ×4 is second order, ×1 is zero order; the orders' sum is the overall order, and one trial then gives the rate constant k. Beneath it all sits collision theory: reactions need collisions with enough energy (the activation energy Ea) and the right orientation, which is why temperature — raising the fraction of energetic collisions — matters so much.
