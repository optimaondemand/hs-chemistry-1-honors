🔬 HOW THIS INVESTIGATION FLOWS
Observe → Question → Predict → Investigate → Analyze → Defend → Extend. You'll notice a phenomenon, commit a prediction before you build, build your slide or poster to test it, then analyze what you made, defend it with a short Claim–Evidence–Reasoning, and extend it to the wider world. Work the stages in order — the prediction comes first, on purpose.
👀 OBSERVE & QUESTION
Observe: a glow stick fades faster in warm water than in ice water, and a car's catalytic converter needs a costly platinum-group surface to do its job. Question: what actually controls how fast a reaction runs — and how can a catalyst speed one up without being used up or changing how much product forms? Your build this week is the answer, made visible.
✅ PREDICT FIRST — COMMIT BEFORE YOU BUILD
Before you draw anything, write a committed prediction in your notebook (or a starting note on your slide/poster): “Adding a catalyst will change ______ about the reaction, but will NOT change ______.” Decide now whether a catalyst changes how fast the reaction goes, how much product forms, or both — and jot why. You'll hold your finished energy-profile diagram up against this prediction in the Analyze step. Committing first is the point; do not peek ahead.

🔍 ANALYZE · READ YOUR OWN MODEL
Stand back and look at your finished energy-profile diagram. The two curves start at the same reactants and end at the same products — only the height of the hill differs. Ask yourself: does the catalyzed (lower) curve end at a different product energy, or the same one? Now hold that against the prediction you committed before drawing. Did the model confirm your prediction or correct it? Note in a sentence what your diagram shows about which quantity a catalyst changes (the speed) and which it leaves alone (ΔG, the equilibrium, and the products).
💬 DEFEND · A SHORT CLAIM–EVIDENCE–REASONING
In 3–4 sentences (in your notebook, and folded into your recorded narration), answer the demo's driving question: “Why can a catalyst make a slow reaction race without being used up or changing how much product forms?” Structure it as Claim → Evidence → Reasoning, pointing to your own energy-profile diagram as the evidence.
▶  Write yours first, then check a model CER
Claim: A catalyst speeds the reaction up by lowering the activation energy — it does not change the reaction's direction or how much product forms.
Evidence: My energy-profile diagram shows two paths between the same reactants and products; the catalyzed path has a lower hill (lower Ea) than the uncatalyzed one, and both curves end at the same product energy.
Reasoning: By collision theory, a lower Ea means a larger fraction of collisions carry enough energy to react, so the rate rises. Because the catalyst is regenerated and lowers the barrier for the forward and reverse directions equally, ΔG, the equilibrium position, and the products stay the same — only the speed changes.
🔮 EXTEND · BEYOND THE BENCH
Connect your demo back to the opening question. The same lowered barrier you drew is what lets a catalytic converter clean exhaust and what enzymes do inside your cells. In your recording, add one sentence naming a real catalyst and the reaction it speeds — and consider the frontier: scientists are working on cheaper, earth-abundant catalysts and engineered enzymes to do this work with less energy and waste.
