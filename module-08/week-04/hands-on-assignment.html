Week 4 · Hands-On (Excel)
Rate-Law Determination from Initial-Rate Data
Assignment · 20 pts · Completion + self-check
📖 WHAT THIS BUILDS ON
From this week's lessons: the rate law rate = k[A]m[B]n (8.09) and the method of initial rates — comparing trials where one concentration changes at a time to find each reactant's order. Here you take a real set of initial-rate data into Excel, determine the orders from the data, write the rate law, and compute the rate constant k — exactly the reasoning a chemist uses at the bench.
This is your week's “doing chemistry” work, and it puts the central kinetics rule into practice: orders come from experiment, never from the coefficients.
📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
A chemist studied the reaction A + B → products by running four trials, each time changing one starting concentration while holding the other fixed, and measured the initial rate of each. In Excel, you'll enter the data table, then compare trials to find the order in A and the order in B, write the rate law, and use one trial to compute the rate constant k by formula. Excel does the arithmetic; the chemistry — reasoning out the orders from how the rate responds — is your job.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
When you double one reactant's concentration, the rate sometimes doubles and sometimes quadruples. How does watching which of those happens — trial by trial — let you read the reaction's hidden orders straight out of the data?

Note the design: trials 1&2 change only [A]; trials 1&3 change only [B]. Trial 4 changes both and is your independent check.
🧮 HOW THE METHOD WORKS (A SHORT REMINDER)
To find an order, compare two trials in which only that reactant changes, and see how the rate responds: rate ×2 → first order; rate ×4 → second order; rate unchanged (×1) → zero order. (That's 2 raised to the order: 21=2, 22=4, 20=1.) Once you have the orders, plug one trial's numbers into rate = k[A]m[B]n and solve for k.
🤖 AI AS AN INSTRUMENT · VERIFY, DON'T TRUST
If you ask an AI assistant for the rate law, it will very often hand back the coefficients of the equation as the orders — here it might guess “first order in A, first order in B” just because the equation reads A + B. That is the exact mistake this assignment trains you to avoid. The orders are hiding in the data, not the equation. Determine each order yourself by comparing the trials, and confirm your rate constant k comes out the same from more than one trial. Use AI to check your arithmetic if you like — but the orders must come from the experiment, verified by hand.
📝 WHAT TO DO IN EXCEL
1
Enter the four-row table with headers in row 1: Trial, [A] (M), [B] (M), Initial rate (M/s).
2
Find the order in A. Compare trials 1 and 2 (where [B] is fixed). In a cell, compute the rate ratio with a formula like =rate2/rate1. Decide from the ratio (×2, ×4, or ×1) whether A is first, second, or zero order, and type the order in a labeled cell m.
3
Find the order in B. Compare trials 1 and 3 (where [A] is fixed) with =rate3/rate1. Decide the order in B and type it in a labeled cell n.
4
Write the rate law in a text cell using your m and n: rate = k[A]m[B]n. Also compute the overall order in a cell: =m+n.
5
Compute k from Trial 1 in a labeled cell, using your orders. With m and n in place, a formula like =rate1/((A1)^m*(B1)^n) gives the rate constant. Then repeat with Trial 4 to confirm you get the same k.
6
In cells or a text box below the table, answer the three Analysis Questions below.
❓ ANALYSIS QUESTIONS (answer in your spreadsheet)
A. What is the order in A and the order in B? For each, name the two trials you compared and the rate ratio that told you the order.
B. Write the full rate law and state the overall order. Then show your computation of k from Trial 1, including its units.
C. A classmate says “the equation is A + B, so it must be first order in each.” Using your data, explain in one or two sentences why you cannot get the orders from the equation — and what the data actually show.
D. Justify the order in B directly from the data table. In two or three sentences, name the two trials you compared, state exactly what [B] did and what the rate did, and explain why that factor points to the order you assigned — as if you were defending it to a skeptical lab partner.
▶  Check your work — answer key
Order in A (trials 1 & 2, [B] fixed): [A] doubles (0.10 → 0.20) and the rate doubles (0.0050 → 0.0100), ratio = 2. Rate ×2 → first order in A (m = 1).

Order in B (trials 1 & 3, [A] fixed): [B] doubles (0.10 → 0.20) and the rate quadruples (0.0050 → 0.0200), ratio = 4. Rate ×4 → second order in B (n = 2).

Rate law: rate = k[A][B]2 — overall order = 1 + 2 = 3 (third order).

Rate constant from Trial 1: 0.0050 = k(0.10)(0.10)2 = k(0.10)(0.010) = k(0.0010), so k = 0.0050 / 0.0010 = 5.0 M−2·s−1.
Check with Trial 4: k = 0.0400 / [(0.20)(0.20)2] = 0.0400 / [(0.20)(0.040)] = 0.0400 / 0.0080 = 5.0 M−2·s−1. ✓ Same k — the rate law is consistent.

A. Order in A = 1 (trials 1&2, ratio 2); order in B = 2 (trials 1&3, ratio 4).
B. rate = k[A][B]2; overall order 3; k = 5.0 M−2·s−1 (units chosen so rate comes out in M/s for a third-order reaction).
C. The orders come from experiment, not the equation. The data show the reaction is second order in B (doubling [B] quadrupled the rate), which the coefficients could never have told us. Reading orders off the equation would have given the wrong rate law.
D. Model answer: Comparing Trials 1 and 3 (where [A] is held fixed at 0.10 M), [B] doubles from 0.10 M to 0.20 M and the initial rate quadruples from 0.0050 M/s to 0.0200 M/s — a rate ratio of 4. Because doubling a concentration multiplies the rate by 2 raised to that reactant's order, and 2² = 4, the reaction must be second order in B (n = 2). Holding [A] constant is what makes this fair: the ×4 response can only be due to the change in [B].
