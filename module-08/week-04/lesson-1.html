Lesson 8.08
Reaction Rates & Factors Affecting Rate
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Touch a flame to a tuft of steel wool and it flares and burns out in seconds. Leave an iron beam in the rain and it rusts over years. Iron reacting with oxygen, both times — so why does one happen in a heartbeat and the other take a lifetime?
Earlier this quarter you learned to ask whether a change can happen — whether it is spontaneous. That question, thermodynamics, tells you the direction nature favors. But it says nothing about how fast. A spontaneous reaction can be explosively quick or imperceptibly slow. This new field is kinetics: the study of reaction speed. Today you'll learn what a reaction rate actually measures — the change in concentration per unit time — and the five levers a chemist can pull to speed a reaction up or slow it down. By the end you'll understand why steel wool and an iron beam, doing the same chemistry, live on completely different clocks.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define reaction rate as the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time, and distinguish kinetics (speed) from thermodynamics (direction).
2
I will be able to list the five factors that change a reaction's rate — concentration, temperature, surface area, a catalyst, and the nature of the reactants.
3
I will be able to predict whether a given change will speed up or slow down a reaction, and explain why in terms of collisions between particles.

📚 PART 1 · SPEED IS A SEPARATE QUESTION FROM DIRECTION
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
A diamond is, ever so slowly, turning into graphite — the change is spontaneous (nature favors it). Yet diamonds last for ages. Burning steel wool is also spontaneous, but it's over in seconds. What do those two facts together tell you about “spontaneous”? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

In the last module you learned to judge whether a change is spontaneous — whether nature favors it running forward. That is the question of direction, and it belongs to thermodynamics. But knowing a reaction can happen tells you nothing about when. Gasoline and air will spontaneously react to form carbon dioxide and water, releasing energy — yet a puddle of gasoline sits in the open air for a long time without bursting into flame, until a spark sets it off. The reaction is favored; it is simply slow to get going.

The study of how fast reactions go — and what we can do to change that speed — is called kinetics. It is a genuinely separate question from spontaneity. Steel wool and an iron beam both react with oxygen to make iron oxide (rust); the chemistry is the same and both are spontaneous. The difference is entirely kinetic: the wispy threads of steel wool, with enormous surface exposed to air, burn in seconds, while the solid beam, with little surface relative to its bulk, takes years.

THE CENTRAL IDEA
Thermodynamics gives direction; kinetics gives speed
a reaction can be spontaneous and still be far too slow to see

A reaction rate measures how quickly the reaction proceeds: it is the change in concentration per unit time. As a reaction runs, reactants are used up and products build up, so we can track the rate by watching either — how fast a reactant's concentration falls, or how fast a product's rises. Written out:

rate = −Δ[reactant] / Δt   =   +Δ[product] / Δt

The minus sign on the reactant side simply keeps the rate positive: a reactant's concentration is decreasing, so Δ[reactant] is negative, and the minus sign flips it. Concentration is measured in molarity (mol/L, or M), and time usually in seconds, so a rate normally carries units of M/s (mol·L−1·s−1). When a reaction's coefficients differ, each concentration's rate is divided by its coefficient so they all agree on one rate for the reaction.

▶  Think first, then check: A reaction is known to be spontaneous, yet nothing seems to be happening in the flask after an hour. Has thermodynamics been broken?
No — nothing is broken. Spontaneity (thermodynamics) only says the reaction is favored to run forward; it never promised a speed. The reaction may simply have a very slow rate, like diamond converting to graphite or iron rusting. To explain why it crawls — and how to make it go faster — you need kinetics, which is exactly today's subject. Keep the two ideas in separate boxes: direction = thermodynamics, speed = kinetics.
📚 PART 2 · THE FIVE LEVERS THAT CHANGE A RATE

Reactions happen when particles collide. Anything that makes collisions more frequent or more effective speeds the reaction up. Five factors do exactly that, and you can reason out each one from the picture of jostling particles.

A reaction generally goes faster when…
• Concentration of reactants rises — more particles packed in a volume means more frequent collisions.
• Temperature rises — particles move faster, so they collide more often and hit harder.
• Surface area of a solid increases (grinding it to powder) — more of the solid is exposed for contact.
• A catalyst is added — it lowers the energy barrier, so more collisions succeed (you'll meet this in detail in Lesson 8.10).
• The nature of the reactants favors it — some substances are simply more reactive than others.

Each lever traces back to collisions. A log burns slowly, but the same wood as fine sawdust can ignite explosively — surface area. Food spoils in days on the counter but lasts weeks in the refrigerator — temperature. A glowing splint barely smolders in ordinary air but bursts into flame in pure oxygen — concentration. In every case you are changing how often, or how hard, the particles meet.

💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY
The careful study of reaction speed grew out of nineteenth-century chemistry, when investigators began timing reactions instead of merely identifying their products. The Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius — whose name returns in the next lesson — found in the late 1800s that raising the temperature speeds reactions far more sharply than the simple increase in collision frequency alone would suggest. That clue, that temperature has an outsized effect, pointed to the idea of an energy barrier that collisions must clear — the foundation of modern kinetics.

A worked walk-through

A piece of zinc metal is dropped into hydrochloric acid and fizzes as it produces hydrogen gas. Name three separate changes that would make it fizz faster, and say why each works.

1. Use more concentrated acid. More H⁺ ions per liter means more collisions with the zinc surface each second.
2. Warm the acid. Hotter particles move faster, colliding more often and with more energy.
3. Use powdered zinc instead of a solid lump. Powder exposes far more surface area, so far more zinc atoms are in contact with the acid at once.

All three increase the rate — each by making collisions more frequent or more energetic.

📚 PART 3 · PUTTING THE LEVERS TO WORK

Once you can name the five factors, you can both explain everyday observations and control reactions on purpose. Industry leans on this constantly: a chemical plant might run a reaction hot and at high pressure (raising temperature and concentration) and over a finely divided solid catalyst (raising surface area and lowering the barrier) so the reaction finishes in minutes rather than days. The same reasoning explains why we refrigerate food, why a campfire is built from kindling rather than whole logs, and why pure-oxygen environments demand extra fire precautions.

🔮 NOW · WHY THIS QUESTION STILL MATTERS
Controlling reaction rate is at the heart of green chemistry — the effort to make industrial chemistry cleaner and less wasteful. If chemists can speed a reaction with a clever catalyst or finer surface instead of brute-force heat, they save enormous amounts of energy. Scientists are currently exploring ways to run essential reactions at lower temperatures and milder conditions, cutting the fuel burned and the waste produced. The five levers you met today are the very dials these researchers are learning to turn more gently.

▶  Think first, then check: Steel wool burns in seconds; an iron beam rusts over years. Both are iron + oxygen. Which factor best explains the difference?
Surface area. Steel wool is a tangle of ultra-thin threads, so an enormous fraction of its iron atoms are exposed to oxygen at once — collisions are everywhere, and it flares in seconds. The beam, by contrast, hides nearly all of its iron deep inside; only the thin outer skin meets the air, so it rusts slowly over years. Same reaction, same direction — wildly different rate, set by how much surface meets the oxygen.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Spontaneity tells you a reaction's direction; kinetics tells you its speed, and the two are separate questions. A reaction rate is the change in concentration per unit time (M/s). Because reactions happen through particle collisions, anything that makes collisions more frequent or more effective speeds the reaction up: higher concentration, higher temperature, greater surface area, a catalyst, and the nature of the reactants. Hold onto this: next lesson we turn the concentration lever into a precise equation — the rate law — and uncover the collision theory that explains why these levers work at all.
