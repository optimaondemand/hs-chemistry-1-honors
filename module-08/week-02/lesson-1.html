Electrode & Cell Potentials

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Drop a strip of zinc and a strip of copper into the right solutions, wire them together, and a voltmeter reads about 1.1 volts — every time. Swap the metals for a different pair and the number changes. Where does that exact voltage come from, and why does each pair of metals have its own?
Last week you built a galvanic cell and watched a spontaneous redox reaction push electrons through a wire. Now we put a number on that push. Every cell has a cell potential — a voltage that measures how hard its reaction drives electrons. This lesson shows where that voltage comes from: each half-reaction has its own measured tendency to grab electrons, captured in a table of standard reduction potentials, and the cell's voltage is the difference between the two halves. By the end you'll read that table, identify which electrode is the cathode and which is the anode, calculate the cell potential, and judge from its sign whether the cell will run on its own.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain why electrode potentials are all measured against the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), defined as exactly 0.00 V, and read a standard reduction potential (E°) from a table.
2
I will be able to use the table to decide which half-reaction is the cathode and which is the anode, and identify the stronger oxidizing agent.
3
I will be able to calculate the standard cell potential with E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode and use its sign to decide whether the cell is spontaneous (galvanic).

📚 PART 1 · VOLTAGE IS THE PUSH BEHIND THE ELECTRONS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
A zinc–copper cell reads about 1.1 V. Replace the copper with silver and the voltage rises; replace it with a metal that holds onto electrons more weakly and the voltage falls. Before you read on — what do you think the cell's voltage is really measuring? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

A galvanic cell turns a spontaneous redox reaction into a current of electrons. The strength of that current's “push” — the electrical force driving electrons from one electrode to the other — is the cell potential, written E°cell and measured in volts (V). (The small degree sign means standard conditions: 1 M solutions, 1 atm gases, 25 °C.) A larger cell potential means a stronger drive. The zinc–copper cell you met last week reads about 1.1 V; other metal pairs read more or less, and today's goal is to predict that number rather than just measure it.

Here is the snag that shaped the whole subject: you cannot measure a single electrode's potential by itself. A voltmeter always needs two terminals — it can only read a difference. So chemists agreed on a common reference point and measured everything against it. That reference is the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), the half-reaction

2 H+(aq, 1 M) + 2 e− ⇌ H2(g, 1 atm)   at 25 °C,  assigned E° = 0.00 V

The SHE isn't truly “zero volts” in some absolute sense — it's a chosen zero, exactly like sea level is the chosen zero for altitude. Every other half-reaction's potential is then reported relative to it. That single agreement turns a tangle of cells into one clean, comparable table.

THE CENTRAL IDEA
A voltmeter reads a difference, never one electrode alone
so all potentials are measured against the SHE, defined as exactly 0.00 V
💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY
The idea that a chemical reaction could be a source of steady electricity began around 1800, when the Italian physicist Alessandro Volta stacked alternating discs of two different metals separated by brine-soaked cloth and produced a continuous current — the first battery, the “voltaic pile.” What he had stumbled onto was exactly the difference between two metals' pull on electrons. The unit of electrical potential, the volt, still carries his name. The careful comparison of one metal against another against a common reference grew into the reduction-potential table you'll use today.
▶  Think first, then check: Why did chemists have to invent a reference electrode at all? Couldn't they just measure each metal's potential directly?
No — a voltmeter has two terminals and can only report the difference in potential between them. There is no way to dip one probe into a half-cell and read its potential against “nothing.” By agreeing that one specific half-reaction (the SHE) is exactly 0.00 V, chemists gave every other half-reaction a fixed point to be measured against, the way sea level fixes the zero for measuring altitude. The numbers are relative, but because they all share the same zero, they can be compared and combined.
🔬 NATURE OF SCIENCE · HOW DO WE KNOW?
Notice something about every E° value in this course: not one of them is an absolute measurement. Each is reported relative to a reference the scientific community agreed to fix at zero — the standard hydrogen electrode. That choice is a convention, not a discovery; chemists could have pinned the zero somewhere else and every number in the table would shift, yet every difference between two half-reactions — and therefore every cell voltage you predict — would come out exactly the same. Many scientific scales work this way (sea level for altitude, the freezing point of water for the Celsius scale). Think about it: if the numbers depend on a chosen reference, in what sense are they still objective — and why does agreeing on a shared reference make the whole table trustworthy rather than arbitrary?
📚 PART 2 · READING THE STANDARD REDUCTION POTENTIAL TABLE

Every half-reaction in the table is written as a reduction — a species gaining electrons — and given a standard reduction potential, E°, measured against the SHE. The number tells you how badly that species “wants” the electrons:

• A more positive E° means the species is more easily reduced — it pulls electrons strongly — so it is a stronger oxidizing agent.
• A more negative E° means the species resists being reduced and is instead more easily oxidized — a stronger reducing agent.

Here is a short slice of the table, ordered from the strongest electron-puller at the top to the weakest at the bottom:

Reduction half-reaction E° (V) Tendency
Ag+ + e− → Ag+0.80strongest electron-puller here
Cu2+ + 2 e− → Cu+0.34pulls electrons fairly well
2 H+ + 2 e− → H20.00the reference (SHE)
Zn2+ + 2 e− → Zn−0.76resists reduction; gives up electrons

Read down the E° column: silver ions (+0.80 V) grab electrons most eagerly, so Ag+ is the strongest oxidizing agent in this list. Zinc sits at the bottom (−0.76 V); it would rather give up electrons than gain them, which makes zinc metal a good reducing agent. When you pair two half-reactions in a cell, the one higher in the table (more positive E°) wins the tug-of-war and runs as a reduction; the lower one is forced to run backward, as an oxidation.

⚠️ ONE EASY-TO-MISS RULE
E° is an intensive property — it does not change when you multiply a half-reaction by a coefficient to balance electrons. If a half-reaction needs to be doubled (say, 2 Ag+ + 2 e− → 2 Ag), its E° is still +0.80 V, not +1.60 V. Voltage is a “push per electron,” not a total you add up. You never multiply E° by the balancing coefficient.
💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY
A table of numbers like this one needed a firm theoretical footing, and the German physical chemist Walther Nernst supplied much of it. Around 1889 he derived the equation that still bears his name — the Nernst equation — showing exactly how an electrode's potential shifts as the concentrations of the dissolved species change. That result tied electrode potentials directly to thermodynamics, turning the reduction-potential table from a bare list of measurements into a predictive tool: given the conditions, you can calculate the voltage. Nernst went on to win the Nobel Prize in Chemistry (1920) for his broader work on the thermodynamics of chemical reactions. In this course we work at standard conditions (1 M, 1 atm, 25 °C), where the corrections Nernst added drop out and the table values apply directly — but the deeper lesson is his: a measured voltage is a window onto the energy of a reaction.
⚡ FLIP CARDS · READ THE TABLE
Predict the answer from the E° values above, then tap to flip and check.
Which is the stronger oxidizing agent, Ag+ or Cu2+? — tap
Ag+. Its E° (+0.80 V) is more positive than Cu2+ (+0.34 V), so Ag+ pulls electrons harder — the stronger oxidizing agent.
In a Zn–Cu cell, which metal is oxidized? — tap
Zinc. Zn2+/Zn has the lower (more negative) E°, so it can't win the electron tug-of-war — it runs in reverse and zinc metal is oxidized.
If you double 2× a half-reaction, does its E° double? — tap
No. E° is intensive — a push per electron. Doubling the half-reaction leaves E° unchanged.
Which metal would rather give up electrons, Zn or Ag? — tap
Zinc. Its very negative E° (−0.76 V) means Zn resists reduction and readily gives up electrons — the stronger reducing agent.
📚 PART 3 · CALCULATING THE CELL POTENTIAL

Now we put the two halves together. The cell potential is the gap between the two electrodes' reduction potentials, and there is one equation to remember:

THE CELL POTENTIAL EQUATION
E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode
both values taken straight from the reduction-potential table

The recipe is always the same. First, pick which half-reaction is the cathode: the one with the higher (more positive) E° — that's where reduction happens. The other, with the lower E°, is the anode, where oxidation happens (it runs in reverse). Then subtract: cathode value minus anode value. Both numbers come from the table as written — do not flip the sign of the anode's table value yourself; the subtraction already handles the reversal.

A worked calculation — the Daniell (Zn–Cu) cell

Build a cell from Zn2+/Zn (E° = −0.76 V) and Cu2+/Cu (E° = +0.34 V). Find E°cell and decide if it is spontaneous.

Step 1 — pick the cathode. Higher E° wins: Cu2+/Cu (+0.34 V) > Zn2+/Zn (−0.76 V), so copper is the cathode (reduction) and zinc is the anode (oxidation).

Step 2 — subtract, using table values as written.
  E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode = (+0.34 V) − (−0.76 V)
  E°cell = 0.34 + 0.76 = +1.10 V

Verdict: E°cell is positive, so the reaction is spontaneous — this is a galvanic cell. And +1.10 V matches the “about 1.1 volts” from the opening puzzle. The number wasn't magic; it's the gap between copper's and zinc's pull on electrons.
⚠️ THE #1 MISTAKE — THE DOUBLE-NEGATIVE
Subtracting a negative number trips up almost everyone. E°cell = (+0.34) − (−0.76) becomes (+0.34) + (0.76) = +1.10 V, not −0.42 V. Two negatives make a plus. If your cell potential comes out negative when you expected a working cell, check this subtraction first — and confirm you put the higher-E° half-reaction in the cathode slot.

🔮 THEN → NOW → FUTURE · CELL POTENTIAL IN THE WORLD
Then: the reduction-potential table was assembled slowly, one metal pair at a time, each measured against the SHE. Now: the same arithmetic — pairing two half-reactions and asking how big a voltage their difference produces — sits at the center of modern battery and fuel-cell research. Scientists are currently exploring electrode pairs and chemistries that push the cell potential higher and store more energy per gram: lithium-based and beyond-lithium designs for electric-vehicle (EV) batteries and grid storage, and hydrogen fuel cells that turn a spontaneous redox reaction straight into electric power. Every candidate is judged, in part, by the very calculation you just did: find the gap between two potentials and ask whether it gives a large, useful, spontaneous voltage. Future: which chemistry will win is genuinely unsettled — researchers are still hunting for pairs that are cheaper, safer, longer-lived, and higher-voltage, and the reduction-potential table remains the map they read.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A cell's voltage, the cell potential E°cell, measures how hard its redox reaction drives electrons. Because a voltmeter reads only a difference, every half-reaction's standard reduction potential (E°) is measured against the SHE, defined as exactly 0.00 V — a more positive E° means a stronger pull on electrons (a stronger oxidizing agent). To find a cell's voltage, put the higher-E° half-reaction at the cathode, the lower at the anode, and compute E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode (the Zn–Cu cell gives +1.10 V). A positive E°cell means the cell is spontaneous — galvanic. Hold onto that sign: next lesson it becomes the key linking voltage to free energy and equilibrium through ΔG° = −nFE°.
