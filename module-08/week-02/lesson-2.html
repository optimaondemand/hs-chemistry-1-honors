Cell Potential, Free Energy & Equilibrium
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You now have three very different-looking numbers for a reaction: a cell voltage (E°cell), a free-energy change (ΔG°), and an equilibrium constant (K). They come from three separate parts of chemistry. So why do they always agree about whether a reaction will go?
Last lesson you measured the push behind a cell's electrons as a voltage. In Module 7 you met two other ways to ask “does this reaction go?” — the Gibbs free energy change ΔG° and the equilibrium constant K. This lesson reveals that those three quantities are three views of one question. A single equation, ΔG° = −nFE°cell, converts a voltage directly into a free-energy change, and a positive voltage, a negative ΔG°, and a large K turn out to always travel together. By the end you'll compute ΔG° from a cell potential, see how the signs lock together, and understand where this fits in the grand thermodynamic story of the course.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to calculate the standard free-energy change of a cell with ΔG° = −nFE°cell, identifying n and using the Faraday constant correctly.
2

3

📚 PART 1 · THREE NUMBERS, ONE QUESTION
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
The Zn–Cu cell has a positive voltage (+1.10 V). From Module 7, a reaction that “goes” has a negative ΔG° and an equilibrium constant K bigger than 1. Before you read on — do you think these three facts are coincidences, or are they connected? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

All three quantities answer the same question — will this reaction go forward on its own, and how far? — in three different languages. E°cell answers it in volts (the electrical push). ΔG° answers it in joules (the free energy released). K answers it as a ratio of products to reactants at equilibrium (how far the reaction runs). It would be strange if they disagreed, and they don't. The bridge between the first two is a single, clean equation:

VOLTAGE TO FREE ENERGY
ΔG° = −nFE°cell
n = moles of electrons transferred · F = Faraday constant = 96,485 C/mol · (V × C = J)

Look at the minus sign. It guarantees that a positive cell voltage produces a negative ΔG° — and a negative ΔG°, from Module 7, means spontaneous. So the rule you learned last lesson (“positive E°cell → galvanic”) is exactly the same statement as “negative ΔG° → spontaneous.” The Faraday constant F = 96,485 C/mol (often rounded to 96,500) is just the charge carried by one mole of electrons; it converts “volts per electron” into “joules per mole of reaction.” And n is the number of moles of electrons the balanced reaction transfers.

💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY
The constant F honors the English scientist Michael Faraday, who in the 1830s discovered that the amount of chemical change at an electrode is proportional to the quantity of electric charge passed — the link between electricity and chemical reaction, measured by counting charge. Faraday had little formal mathematics, but his careful, relentless experiments uncovered the quantitative laws of electrochemistry. The number that bears his name — the charge on one mole of electrons — is precisely what lets us turn a measured voltage into the energy released by a reaction.
💡 THEN · TWO SCIENCES BECOME ONE
The equation ΔG° = −nFE° is a quiet landmark: it marks the exact point where thermodynamics and electrochemistry turned out to be one subject. In the 1870s the American physicist Josiah Willard Gibbs recast the question “will a reaction go?” in terms of free energy — a purely energetic kind of bookkeeping. A little over a decade later, Walther Nernst and others showed that this same free energy could be read straight off a voltmeter as a cell's potential. Two lines of inquiry that had grown up separately — the study of heat and energy on one side, the study of electricity and chemical cells on the other — met in a single equation. When you multiply n, F, and E° and attach that minus sign, you are crossing a bridge that took the better part of a century to build.
▶  Think first, then check: A cell has E°cell = +0.50 V. Without any numbers, what can you say about the sign of ΔG° and whether the reaction is spontaneous?
Because ΔG° = −nFE°, and n and F are both positive, a positive E°cell forces ΔG° to be negative. A negative ΔG° means the reaction is spontaneous — the cell is galvanic. You don't even need to compute the number to know the sign and the verdict; the minus sign in the equation does it for you.
📚 PART 2 · THE THREE-WAY SIGN RELATIONSHIP

Put all three together and a tidy picture emerges. There is a second bridge, from Module 7, between free energy and equilibrium: ΔG° = −RT ln K. Chaining the two equations links the voltage all the way to K. You won't compute K from a voltage in this course, but the direction of the relationship is worth knowing cold:

E°cell ΔG° K What it means
> 0< 0> 1spontaneous; products favored
= 0= 0= 1at equilibrium; no net drive
< 0> 0< 1nonspontaneous; reactants favored

Read any row across and every cell agrees. A positive voltage always comes with a negative ΔG° and a K greater than 1 — the reaction runs forward and ends up product-rich. Flip every sign and you get a reaction that won't go on its own. The middle row, where all three sit at their neutral value, is equilibrium: the cell voltage has dropped to zero (a “dead battery” is a cell that has reached equilibrium). One equation that captures the standard-temperature link to K, derived from these relationships, is E°cell = (0.0592 V / n) log K at 25 °C — useful to recognize, though we keep K-from-voltage qualitative here.

📚 PART 3 · COMPUTING ΔG° FROM A VOLTAGE

The calculation is short, but two things trip people up: getting n right (count the electrons the balanced reaction actually transfers) and remembering that the answer comes out in joules, which you'll usually convert to kilojoules. Here is the worked example, our Zn–Cu cell from last lesson.

A worked calculation — ΔG° for the Daniell cell

The Zn–Cu cell transfers 2 electrons (Zn → Zn2+ + 2 e−; Cu2+ + 2 e− → Cu), and from Lesson 8.03 its E°cell = +1.10 V. Find ΔG°.

Step 1 — identify n. The balanced reaction moves 2 mol of electrons, so n = 2.

Step 2 — plug into ΔG° = −nFE°.
  ΔG° = −(2)(96,485 C/mol)(1.10 V)
  ΔG° = −212,267 J  (since V × C = J)

Step 3 — convert J to kJ.
  ΔG° = −212,267 J × (1 kJ / 1000 J) ≈ −212 kJ

Verdict: ΔG° is large and negative — strongly spontaneous, exactly as the positive +1.10 V promised. Same reaction, same conclusion, two languages.
⚠️ THE SIGN-AND-UNIT TRAP
Two classic slips: (1) forgetting the minus sign, which makes a spontaneous reaction look nonspontaneous — the formula is ΔG° = minus nFE°; and (2) leaving the answer in joules and comparing it to kJ values. With F ≈ 96,485, the raw answer is in joules — a number in the hundred-thousands. Divide by 1000 to get kJ. A reasonable cell gives a ΔG° of a few hundred kJ, not a few hundred thousand kJ.

🔗 THE ARC THIS COMPLETES · WHAT DRIVES CHANGE
Step back and notice what you've just finished. In Module 5 you measured heat with enthalpy (ΔH). In Module 7 you folded heat and disorder into the single decision-maker, free energy (ΔG). Now in Module 8 you've connected that free energy directly to a voltage you can read on a meter (E°). ΔH → ΔG → E° is one long story about what drives a reaction. But a warning for what comes next: none of these tells you how fast a reaction goes. A reaction can have a huge negative ΔG° and a big positive voltage and still be unimaginably slow (diamond turning to graphite). Speed is a separate question — kinetics — and it's exactly where the rest of this module is headed.

No. ΔG° and E° tell you only DIRECTION, never speed. Speed is kinetics — coming up next in this module.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A cell's voltage, its free-energy change, and its equilibrium constant are three views of one question. The bridge is ΔG° = −nFE°cell (F = 96,485 C/mol; n = moles of electrons; answer in joules → convert to kJ). The minus sign locks the signs together: E°cell > 0 ⇔ ΔG° < 0 ⇔ K > 1 — spontaneous and product-favored. The Zn–Cu cell gives E° = +1.10 V and ΔG° ≈ −212 kJ, the same conclusion in two languages. This completes the ΔH → ΔG → E° arc on what drives change — but remember the catch: it tells you direction, never speed. Speed is kinetics, and that is exactly where this module turns next.
