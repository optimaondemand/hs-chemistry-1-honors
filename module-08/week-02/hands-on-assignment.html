Week 2 · Hands-On
Cell Potentials & ΔG° = −nFE°
Assignment · 20 pts · Completion + self-check
📖 WHAT THIS BUILDS ON
From this week's lessons: reading a standard reduction-potential table (8.03), identifying cathode and anode, computing E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode, judging spontaneity from its sign, and linking voltage to free energy with ΔG° = −nFE° (8.04). Here you'll do all of it by hand — including the double-negative subtraction and the sign-and-unit trap that snare almost everyone.
This is your week's “doing chemistry” work. Do it on paper or in your notebook — no spreadsheet needed this week.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
Given only a table of standard reduction potentials, how do you predict a cell's voltage, decide whether it will run on its own, and convert that voltage into the free energy the reaction can deliver?
🧰 YOUR TOOLBOX
E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode  (both from the table; cathode = the higher E°).
E°cell > 0 → spontaneous (galvanic) ·  E°cell < 0 → not spontaneous as written.
ΔG° = −nFE°  with F = 96,485 C/mol and n = moles of electrons transferred ·  (V × C = J).
Reduction half-reaction E° (V)
Ag+ + e− → Ag+0.80
Cu2+ + 2 e− → Cu+0.34
2 H+ + 2 e− → H20.00
Zn2+ + 2 e− → Zn−0.76
⚠️ THE TRAP — READ THIS TWICE
Two slips to avoid. (1) The double negative. When the anode's E° is negative, subtracting it ADDS: E°cell = (+0.34) − (−0.76) = 0.34 + 0.76 = +1.10 V, never −0.42 V. (2) The ΔG° sign and units. The formula has a minus sign — ΔG° = −nFE° — and with F ≈ 96,485 the raw answer is in joules. Divide by 1000 for kJ. A real cell gives a few hundred kJ, not a few hundred thousand.
📝 PART A · IDENTIFY CATHODE/ANODE AND COMPUTE E°cell (items 1–3)

For each cell, (a) name which half-reaction is the cathode and which is the anode, (b) compute E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode showing the subtraction, and (c) state whether the cell is spontaneous (galvanic).

1. A cell made from Cu2+/Cu and Ag+/Ag.
2. A cell made from Zn2+/Zn and Ag+/Ag.
3. A cell made from Zn2+/Zn and Cu2+/Cu (the Daniell cell).
📊 PART B · SPONTANEITY CHECK (items 4–5)

For each value of E°cell, state whether the cell is spontaneous (galvanic) or not spontaneous as written, and in one line say what that sign also tells you about ΔG° (positive or negative).

4. A cell has E°cell = +0.46 V.
5. A cell has E°cell = −0.34 V.
💡 WORKED EXAMPLE (the method for Parts A–B)
Cell: Zn2+/Zn (−0.76 V) paired with Cu2+/Cu (+0.34 V).
Step 1 — pick the cathode (higher E°): Cu2+/Cu at +0.34 V > Zn at −0.76 V, so copper is the cathode, zinc is the anode.
Step 2 — subtract, table values as written: E°cell = (+0.34) − (−0.76) = 0.34 + 0.76 = +1.10 V.
Step 3 — verdict: positive → spontaneous (galvanic), and so ΔG° < 0. Notice the double negative made the subtraction an addition.
🧮 PART C · FREE ENERGY FROM VOLTAGE (item 6)

Use ΔG° = −nFE° with F = 96,485 C/mol. Show the substitution, give the answer in joules, then convert to kJ and state spontaneous or not.

6. The Daniell (Zn–Cu) cell transfers n = 2 electrons and has E°cell = +1.10 V (from item 3). Compute ΔG° in joules, convert to kJ, and state whether the reaction is spontaneous. (Keep the minus sign and watch J vs. kJ!)
✍️ PART D · EXPLAIN YOUR REASONING (item 7 · self-check)

Numbers are only half the work — put the idea into words. In 2–3 sentences, explain to a classmate why a positive E°cell and a negative ΔG° are two ways of saying the exact same thing about a reaction. Refer to the minus sign in ΔG° = −nFE° and to what “spontaneous” means. Write your answer in your notebook first, then open the model answer to compare.

7. Why must a cell with a positive E°cell also have a negative ΔG°? Answer in 2–3 sentences using the equation.
▶  Model answer — open after you've written yours
In ΔG° = −nFE°, the quantities n (moles of electrons) and F (the Faraday constant) are always positive, so the leading minus sign forces ΔG° to take the opposite sign of E°cell. A positive E°cell therefore always produces a negative ΔG° — and a negative ΔG° is exactly the thermodynamic definition of a spontaneous reaction, the same verdict the positive voltage already announced. So “the cell pushes electrons on its own” (positive voltage) and “the reaction releases free energy” (negative ΔG°) are one fact in two languages, welded together by that minus sign.
🤖 AI IS AN INSTRUMENT, NOT AN ORACLE
Ask an AI tool to find a cell potential and it will often flip the E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode subtraction (putting the wrong half-reaction at the cathode, or mishandling the −0.76 V double negative) and report the wrong sign. Ask it for a ΔG° and it commonly drops the minus sign in ΔG° = −nFE° or leaves the answer in joules. Treat any AI result as a claim to verify, not an answer: confirm the higher-E° half-reaction is the cathode, redo the subtraction by hand, and check that a positive E°cell gives a negative ΔG°. Reasoning from the evidence is the skill; the calculator is just an instrument.
🔑 CHECK YOUR WORK
▶  Answer key — open after you've tried all six calculation items (item 7 has its own model answer above)
Part A — cathode/anode & E°cell:
1. Cu–Ag: Ag+/Ag is the cathode (higher E°, +0.80 V); Cu2+/Cu is the anode (+0.34 V). E°cell = (+0.80) − (+0.34) = +0.46 V → spontaneous (galvanic).
2. Zn–Ag: Ag+/Ag is the cathode (+0.80 V); Zn2+/Zn is the anode (−0.76 V). E°cell = (+0.80) − (−0.76) = 0.80 + 0.76 = +1.56 V → spontaneous (galvanic). (Double negative → addition.)
3. Zn–Cu: Cu2+/Cu is the cathode (+0.34 V); Zn2+/Zn is the anode (−0.76 V). E°cell = (+0.34) − (−0.76) = 0.34 + 0.76 = +1.10 V → spontaneous (galvanic).

Part B — spontaneity check:
4. E°cell = +0.46 V → spontaneous (galvanic); positive E°cell means ΔG° < 0.
5. E°cell = −0.34 V → not spontaneous as written (the reverse reaction is the spontaneous one); negative E°cell means ΔG° > 0.

Part C — ΔG° = −nFE° (keep the minus sign; convert J → kJ):
6. ΔG° = −(2)(96,485 C/mol)(1.10 V) = −212,267 J. Convert: −212,267 J × (1 kJ / 1000 J) ≈ −212 kJ → spontaneous (ΔG° < 0), exactly as the positive +1.10 V promised.

Trap check: if any E°cell came out negative for a cell you expected to work, you likely put the lower-E° half-reaction at the cathode, or mishandled the −0.76 V double negative. And if your ΔG° came out positive or in the hundred-thousands of kJ, you dropped the minus sign or forgot to divide joules by 1000.
