📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A battery takes a chemical reaction that wants to happen and turns it into electricity. But what if you ran that machine backward — spending electricity to force a reaction that would never happen on its own? You'd be making chemistry out of electricity. Can it be done, and can we predict exactly how much?
Everything in this module so far has run downhill: spontaneous redox reactions releasing energy as electric current. This lesson reverses the arrow. Electrolysis uses an outside power source to drive a non-spontaneous reaction — pushing electrons the “wrong” way to split water into hydrogen and oxygen, pull pure sodium and chlorine out of salt, plate a thin layer of silver onto a fork, or win aluminum from its ore. It is one of industrial chemistry's most important tools. And because the electrons are counted, the chemistry is wonderfully predictable: Faraday's law lets you calculate exactly how much metal an electric current will deposit. By the end you'll be able to tell a galvanic cell from an electrolytic one at a glance — and put real numbers on the metal a current plates out.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to describe electrolysis as using an external power source to drive a non-spontaneous redox reaction (E°cell < 0) in an electrolytic cell.
2

3
I will be able to use Faraday's law (Q = I·t; mol e− = Q/F) to find the mass of metal produced by a given current and time.

📚 PART 1 · RUNNING THE MACHINE BACKWARD
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
A galvanic cell makes electricity from a reaction that happens on its own. Suppose you wanted to run a reaction that does not happen on its own — like splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen gas. What do you think it would take to force it? (No wrong answer — just commit a guess.)

Electrolysis is the use of electrical energy from an external power source to drive a non-spontaneous redox reaction — the exact reverse of what a galvanic cell does. The device that carries it out is an electrolytic cell. Where a galvanic cell makes electricity from a downhill reaction, an electrolytic cell spends electricity to push a reaction uphill. A battery or power supply forces electrons the “wrong” way through the circuit, and because the reaction has E°cell < 0 (non-spontaneous), it stops the instant you cut the power — it needs continuous outside energy to keep going.

THE CENTRAL IDEA
Galvanic: chemistry → electricity  ·  Electrolytic: electricity → chemistry
an electrolytic cell drives a non-spontaneous reaction (E°cell < 0) with an outside power source

There is one point that trips up nearly everyone, so fix it firmly now. In both kinds of cell, the anode is always where oxidation happens and the cathode is always where reduction happens — that never changes. What flips is the sign of the terminals. In a galvanic cell the anode is negative and the cathode positive; in an electrolytic cell it is the opposite — the anode is positive and the cathode is negative — because the external power source forces that polarity. Anchor on the definitions (anode = oxidation, cathode = reduction) and let the signs follow from which kind of cell you're in.

Hold steady: anode = oxidation, cathode = reduction (in both cell types).
What flips: galvanic → anode (−), cathode (+);  electrolytic → anode (+), cathode (−).
💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY
Once Volta's battery gave chemists a steady current, electrolysis became a tool for discovery. In the early 1800s Humphry Davy passed current through molten compounds and pulled out elements no one had ever isolated — potassium, sodium, calcium, and more — literally tearing them from their compounds with electricity. A generation later, in the 1830s, Michael Faraday turned electrolysis into a quantitative science, showing in his laws of electrolysis that the amount of substance produced is tied directly to the charge passed. The constant that carries his name, the Faraday, still anchors every calculation you'll do in Part 3.
📚 PART 2 · WHAT ELECTROLYSIS IS GOOD FOR

Driving reactions uphill turns out to be enormously useful. A few of electrolysis's most important jobs:

• Electrolysis of water — splitting water into its elements: 2 H2O → 2 H2 + O2. A clean way to make hydrogen fuel.
• Electrolysis of molten NaCl — pulling salt apart into sodium metal (Na) and chlorine gas (Cl2), the industrial source of both.
• Electroplating — depositing a thin, even layer of one metal onto another, such as silver-plating a piece of tableware.
• Aluminum production — the Hall–Héroult process wins aluminum metal from its oxide by electrolysis; it is how essentially all of the world's aluminum is made.

In every case, electricity is doing work that chemistry would never do on its own: separating stable compounds, coating surfaces atom by atom, freeing reactive metals from their ores. The cost is real — these are energy-hungry processes — but the products are often impossible to get any other way.

🔮 THEN → NOW → FUTURE · WHERE THIS WEEK'S CHEMISTRY IS HEADING
Then. For two centuries the electrochemistry of this week has done steady industrial work — galvanic cells and batteries storing energy, and electrolysis splitting water, refining metals, and winning aluminum and chlorine from their compounds.

Now. Two of those threads have become front-line research. Scientists are currently exploring “green hydrogen” — running the electrolysis of water (2 H2O → 2 H2 + O2) on electricity from wind and solar, so the hydrogen fuel is made with little or no carbon emitted. At the same time, researchers are working on next-generation battery chemistries (solid-state designs, sodium-ion cells, and others) that aim for more stored energy, faster charging, better safety, and materials that are cheaper and more responsibly sourced.

Future. The open questions are hard and honest ones: can green hydrogen be made cheaply and efficiently enough to matter at scale, and can new batteries beat lithium-ion without its costs? These are unsettled — which is exactly why they are being researched right now, and why the same reactions you are learning to balance and calculate sit at the center of how the world may power itself.
📚 PART 3 · FARADAY'S LAW — COUNTING THE ELECTRONS

Here is the beautiful part: because electrolysis deposits matter by moving electrons, and we can measure the electrons, the amount of product is completely predictable. This is Faraday's law of electrolysis: the amount of substance produced is proportional to the electric charge passed. The chain of reasoning is short and always the same:

FARADAY'S LAW — THE CHAIN
Q = I · t  →  mol e− = Q / F  →  mol metal  →  mass
charge Q in coulombs (amps × seconds); F = 96,485 C/mol

Step by step: the charge passed is the current times the time, Q = I·t (amps × seconds gives coulombs). Divide by the Faraday constant, F = 96,485 C/mol, to get the moles of electrons. Then use the half-reaction to convert moles of electrons into moles of metal (the half-reaction tells you how many electrons each atom needs), and finally use the molar mass to get grams. The one habit that keeps you out of trouble: put the time in seconds before you compute Q.

💡 WORKED EXAMPLE · HOW MUCH COPPER IS PLATED?
A current of 2.00 A flows for 1.00 hour through a copper(II) solution. How many grams of copper are deposited? (Cu2+ + 2 e− → Cu; molar mass of Cu = 63.55 g/mol.)

Step 1 — time in seconds: 1.00 hour = 3600 s.
Step 2 — charge Q = I·t: 2.00 A × 3600 s = 7200 C.
Step 3 — moles of electrons: 7200 C ÷ 96,485 C/mol = 0.0746 mol e−.
Step 4 — moles of Cu: the half-reaction needs 2 e− per Cu, so 0.0746 ÷ 2 = 0.0373 mol Cu.
Step 5 — mass: 0.0373 mol × 63.55 g/mol ≈ 2.37 g Cu.
Answer: about 2.37 g of copper. Notice the “÷ 2” step — forgetting that the ion needs two electrons is the most common mistake.

🤖 AI IS AN INSTRUMENT, NOT AN ORACLE
Ask an AI tool to run a Faraday's-law problem and it will often slip on the same steps people do: leaving time in minutes or hours instead of converting to seconds, or forgetting to divide by the number of electrons in the half-reaction (the “÷ 2” for Cu2+). Treat any AI answer as a claim to verify: redo Q = I·t with the time in seconds, divide by 96,485, then divide by the electrons per atom — and sanity-check that a small current over an hour gives a few grams, not kilograms. Reasoning through the chain yourself is the skill; the tool is just an instrument.

▶  Think first, then check: A current of 1.00 A flows for 30.0 minutes through a solution of silver ions (Ag+ + e− → Ag). About how many moles of silver are deposited?
About 0.0187 mol Ag. First put time in seconds: 30.0 min = 1800 s. Then Q = I·t = 1.00 A × 1800 s = 1800 C. Moles of electrons = 1800 ÷ 96,485 = 0.01866 mol e−. Silver needs only one electron per atom (Ag+ + e− → Ag), so mol Ag = mol e− = 0.0187 mol Ag. (Unlike copper, there is no “÷ 2” here — always read the electrons-per-atom straight off the half-reaction.)

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Electrolysis runs the galvanic machine backward: an external power source drives a non-spontaneous reaction (E°cell < 0) in an electrolytic cell — making chemistry out of electricity to split water, win sodium and aluminum, and electroplate metals. The anode is always oxidation and the cathode always reduction in both cell types; only the terminal signs flip (electrolytic: anode +, cathode −). And because we count the electrons, the chemistry is exact: Faraday's law — Q = I·t, then mol e− = Q/F with F = 96,485 C/mol, then the half-reaction's electron ratio — predicts the mass of metal deposited. Hold onto this contrast: galvanic and electrolytic cells are the two directions of the same electrochemistry, and together they close the loop you began the module with — reactions making electricity, and electricity making reactions.
