Batteries & Fuel Cells
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Your phone dies, you plug it in, and a few hours later it is full again — ready to drain and refill hundreds of times. Yet when the AA battery in a flashlight runs flat, charging it does nothing; you throw it away. Both are “batteries.” Why can one be brought back to life and the other cannot?
In the last two lessons you learned that a galvanic cell turns a spontaneous redox reaction into a flow of electrons — usable electricity. A battery is simply one or more of those cells packaged to power something portable. But not all batteries are alike: some are single-use, some recharge thousands of times, and a special kind — the fuel cell — never “runs down” at all as long as you keep feeding it. This lesson sorts these devices by the one question that explains the phone-versus-AA puzzle: can the redox reaction inside be reversed? Understanding that single idea tells you why some cells are disposable, why others recharge, and why the technology in your pocket is at the center of the world's energy future.

3

📚 PART 1 · A BATTERY IS A PACKAGED GALVANIC CELL
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
A phone battery recharges hundreds of times; a flashlight AA, once dead, stays dead no matter how long you leave it on a charger. Both are galvanic cells inside. What do you think is different about the chemistry that lets one come back and not the other? (No wrong answer — just commit a guess.)

You already know the core machine. A galvanic cell pairs an oxidation (at the anode) with a reduction (at the cathode) so that electrons are forced through an external wire — and a flow of electrons through a wire is electric current. A battery is nothing more exotic than this: one or more galvanic cells packaged together to deliver portable electrical energy. The familiar 9-volt block, the coin cell in a watch, the pack in an electric car — all are galvanic cells in a convenient case, sometimes stacked in series so their voltages add.

As a battery runs, the reactants at its electrodes are slowly consumed. When they are gone, the spontaneous reaction stops and the battery is “dead.” What happens next — whether you can revive it — is the question that splits all batteries into two families.

THE CENTRAL IDEA
A battery is a galvanic cell you can carry
what divides batteries is whether the reaction inside can be reversed
📚 PART 2 · PRIMARY VS. SECONDARY CELLS

A primary cell is single-use: its redox reaction cannot be easily reversed, so once the reactants are spent the cell is discarded. The everyday example is the alkaline battery in flashlights and remotes. Inside, zinc is the anode (it is oxidized), manganese dioxide (MnO2) is the cathode (it is reduced), and a basic potassium hydroxide (KOH) paste is the electrolyte; each cell delivers about 1.5 V. Try to recharge it and almost nothing useful happens — the products won't turn cleanly back into reactants — so an alkaline cell is a one-way device.

A secondary cell is rechargeable: its reaction can be reversed by pushing current through it from an outside source, rebuilding the original reactants so the cell can discharge again. Two examples matter most:

• The lead-acid car battery — a lead (Pb) anode, a lead dioxide (PbO2) cathode, and sulfuric acid (H2SO4) electrolyte. Each cell gives about 2 V; six are wired in series for the familiar 12 V battery. The car's alternator recharges it as you drive.
• The lithium-ion battery — the workhorse of modern electronics. Lithium ions (Li+) shuttle back and forth between the two electrodes as it charges and discharges. Its high energy density (a lot of stored energy per unit mass) is why it powers phones, laptops, and electric vehicles.

So the phone-versus-AA puzzle resolves: the phone holds a lithium-ion secondary cell whose reaction reverses on the charger, while the flashlight AA is an alkaline primary cell whose reaction does not. Same underlying galvanic machine — different answer to “can it run backward?”

💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY — THE BATTERY LINEAGE
The first true battery came from the Italian physicist Alessandro Volta, who around 1800 stacked discs of two different metals separated by brine-soaked cloth — the “voltaic pile.” It was the first device to deliver a steady electric current, and the unit of electric potential, the volt, still carries his name. But Volta's pile was a primary cell — once spent, it was finished. The next leap came in 1859, when the French physicist Gaston Planté built the lead-acid cell: the first practical rechargeable (secondary) battery, whose reaction could be run backward with an outside current. It is essentially the same chemistry still starting cars today. More than a century later, the rechargeable lithium-ion cell reached consumers in the early 1990s, shuttling Li+ ions between electrodes to pack far more energy into far less weight — the cell now in nearly every phone, laptop, and electric car. Volta → Planté → lithium-ion is one long line of the same question you're studying: can the reaction inside be reversed?

📚 PART 3 · FUEL CELLS — A CELL YOU KEEP FEEDING

Both primary and secondary cells carry their reactants sealed inside, so they eventually run low. A fuel cell escapes that limit: it is a galvanic cell supplied with a continuous flow of reactants from outside, so it does not “run down” as long as fuel keeps arriving. It is less like a tank and more like a stove burner — it produces energy steadily while you feed it.

The classic example is the hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell. Hydrogen gas is fed to one electrode and oxygen (from air) to the other; the overall reaction is simply:

THE HYDROGEN–OXYGEN FUEL CELL
2 H2 + O2 → 2 H2O
it produces electricity with water as the only product

The chemistry is striking: the cell delivers electric current and its only waste product is water. Because of that clean output and steady supply, hydrogen–oxygen fuel cells have powered some buses and cars, and famously supplied electricity (and drinking water) aboard spacecraft. A fuel cell is still a galvanic cell — oxidation at the anode, reduction at the cathode — but its “battery life” is set by how long you keep the fuel flowing, not by what was sealed inside at the factory.

🔮 NOW · WHY THIS QUESTION STILL MATTERS
Battery chemistry has quietly become one of the most important problems of our century. Scientists are currently exploring how to raise energy density (more stored energy for less weight), extend lifetime, charge faster, and improve safety — while sourcing the needed materials responsibly, since mining lithium and other metals has real environmental and human costs. These are the cells that electric vehicles and large-scale grid storage depend on, so progress here shapes how the world powers itself. This is exactly the quarter's question of responsibility: the chemistry in your pocket carries consequences far beyond it.
🧩 HOW SCIENTISTS DECIDE · WEIGHING A REAL CHOICE
Choosing which cell to build into a device — a disposable alkaline cell, a rechargeable lithium-ion pack, or a hydrogen fuel cell — is not a matter of preference or of which one “sounds best.” Engineers and policymakers weigh it as a cost–benefit decision backed by evidence: How much does it cost to make and run? What materials does it need, and can they be sourced responsibly? What is the environmental impact over the cell's whole life, from mining to disposal? And do those costs justify the benefit for this job? A grid battery, a smartphone, and a city bus can each land on a different answer — and the honest answer follows the evidence, not the marketing. That is the nature of science at work in the public square, and the heart of this quarter's theme of responsibility.
▶  Think first, then check: A designer says “lithium-ion is the best battery, so every product should use it.” Name two pieces of evidence a scientist would weigh before agreeing — and explain why “best” depends on the job.
A careful answer weighs evidence, not preference. Two examples: (1) cost and materials — lithium-ion needs lithium and other metals whose mining carries environmental and human costs, so for a cheap single-use item (a greeting card, a smoke-detector backup) a low-cost primary cell may be the responsible choice; (2) the demands of the job — a device that must recharge daily and stay light (a phone, an EV) genuinely benefits from lithium-ion's high energy density and reversibility, while a stationary backup might value low cost or safety more than weight. “Best” is never absolute: it is the cell whose benefits justify its costs for that particular use, judged on the evidence.

▶  Think first, then check: A car's 12 V lead-acid battery is built from individual cells that each produce about 2 V. How are the cells arranged, and how many are there?
Six cells wired in series. When galvanic cells are connected in series, their voltages add. Six lead-acid cells at about 2 V each give 6 × 2 V = 12 V. This is the same trick used in many battery packs: stack cells in series to reach the voltage a device needs. A lead-acid battery is a secondary cell, so the car's alternator can run that 2 V chemistry backward to recharge it as you drive.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A battery is one or more galvanic cells packaged to deliver portable electricity, and the question that sorts them is whether the reaction inside can be reversed. Primary cells (like the alkaline AA — zinc anode, MnO2 cathode) are single-use; secondary cells (the lead-acid car battery, the lithium-ion cell in your phone) recharge because an outside current runs their reaction backward. A fuel cell sidesteps the limit entirely: fed a continuous flow of reactants, the hydrogen–oxygen cell (2H2 + O2 → 2H2O) makes electricity with water as its only product. Hold onto the through-line: every one of these is the galvanic cell you already know, doing a job — and in the next lesson you'll meet the same chemistry doing a job nobody wants, when metals corrode.
