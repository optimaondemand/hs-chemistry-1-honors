📖 WHAT THIS BUILDS ON
From this week's lessons: batteries as packaged galvanic cells — primary vs. secondary (8.05); corrosion as unwanted oxidation (8.06); and electrolysis — the galvanic-vs-electrolytic contrast and Faraday's law: Q = I·t, mol e− = Q/F, then the half-reaction's electron ratio to mass (8.07). Here you'll classify cells, identify electrodes, and run a plating calculation by hand — watching the two traps that snare almost everyone.
This is your week's “doing chemistry” work. Do it on paper or in your notebook — no spreadsheet needed this week.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
A galvanic cell makes electricity; an electrolytic cell spends it. Some batteries recharge and some don't. And a measured current deposits an exact mass of metal. How do you read each device correctly — and count the electrons to predict what it makes?
🧰 YOUR TOOLBOX
Galvanic = spontaneous (E°cell > 0), makes electricity ·  Electrolytic = driven (E°cell < 0), uses electricity.
Primary = single-use ·  Secondary = rechargeable.  In both cell types: anode = oxidation, cathode = reduction.
Faraday's law: Q = I · t (amps × seconds = coulombs) → mol e− = Q ÷ F, F = 96,485 C/mol → mol metal (use half-reaction) → mass.
⚠️ THE TWO TRAPS — READ THIS TWICE
Trap 1 — seconds. Time MUST be in seconds before you compute Q. Minutes × 60, hours × 3600. Leaving time in minutes makes Q (and your answer) far too small.
Trap 2 — electrons per atom. Read the half-reaction. Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu needs two electrons per atom, so you DIVIDE moles of electrons by 2. Ag+ + e− → Ag needs only one — no dividing. Forgetting this gives an answer that's off by a whole factor.
📝 PART A · CLASSIFY THE CELLS (items 1–3)

For each device, state whether it is galvanic or electrolytic, and (where it applies) primary or secondary. Give a one-line reason.

1. An alkaline AA battery powering a flashlight.
2. A cell splitting water into H2 and O2 using a plugged-in power supply.
3. The lithium-ion battery in a phone that is recharged each night.
📊 PART B · IDENTIFY THE ELECTRODES (items 4–5)

Remember: in every cell, the anode is where oxidation happens and the cathode is where reduction happens. Only the terminal sign changes between cell types.

4. In an alkaline battery, zinc is oxidized and manganese dioxide (MnO2) is reduced. Which is the anode and which is the cathode?
5. In an electrolytic cell, name which electrode is the anode and which is the cathode by their reactions, and state the sign (+ or −) of each. (Hint: the signs are opposite to a galvanic cell.)
🧮 PART C · FARADAY'S LAW (items 6–8)

Use Q = I·t, then mol e− = Q ÷ 96,485, then the half-reaction. Put time in seconds first, and divide by the electrons per atom. Show Q, mol e−, mol metal, and mass.

6. A current of 2.00 A flows for 1.00 hour through a copper(II) solution. Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu (Cu = 63.55 g/mol). Find the mass of copper deposited. (This is the worked example — follow the method.)
7. A current of 1.50 A flows for 20.0 minutes through a silver solution. Ag+ + e− → Ag (Ag = 107.87 g/mol). Find the mass of silver deposited. (Watch Trap 1 — and note silver needs only one electron.)
8. A current of 3.00 A flows for 30.0 minutes through a copper(II) solution. Find the mass of copper deposited, then compare to item 6 — what changed, and why is the mass different?
💡 WORKED EXAMPLE (the method for item 6)
2.00 A for 1.00 hour, Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu, Cu = 63.55 g/mol.
Step 1 — time in seconds: 1.00 h = 3600 s.
Step 2 — charge Q = I·t: 2.00 A × 3600 s = 7200 C.
Step 3 — moles of electrons: 7200 ÷ 96,485 = 0.0746 mol e−.
Step 4 — moles of Cu: 2 e− per Cu, so 0.0746 ÷ 2 = 0.0373 mol Cu.
Step 5 — mass: 0.0373 × 63.55 ≈ 2.37 g Cu.
Answer: about 2.37 g of copper.
✍️ PART D · EXPLAIN YOUR REASONING (item 9 — self-check)

Write 3–4 sentences in your notebook, then open the model answer to compare. There is no single “right” wording — check that your reasoning uses evidence, not preference.

9. A hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell can keep making electricity indefinitely, while an ordinary battery eventually dies. (a) Explain why, in terms of where each device gets its reactants. (b) Then name one real trade-off — cost, materials, or environmental impact — that a decision-maker should weigh before choosing a fuel cell over a battery for a given job.
▶  Model answer — open after you've written yours
(a) A battery carries its reactants sealed inside, so once they are consumed the reaction stops and the battery is dead. A fuel cell is fed a continuous flow of reactants (hydrogen and oxygen) from outside, so it keeps producing electricity — with water as its only product — for as long as the fuel keeps arriving. The difference is the source of the reactants, not the underlying galvanic chemistry.

(b) Any one well-reasoned trade-off, backed by evidence rather than preference, earns full credit — for example: cost (fuel cells and their catalysts can be expensive to build); materials (producing and storing hydrogen fuel, and the catalysts a fuel cell needs, require resources that must be sourced responsibly); or environmental impact (a fuel cell is only truly “clean” if the hydrogen is made cleanly — hydrogen produced from fossil fuels carries a carbon cost, so the whole life cycle must be weighed against the benefit). The point is that “best” depends on the job and the evidence.
🤖 AI IS AN INSTRUMENT, NOT AN ORACLE
If you ask an AI tool to do a Faraday's-law problem, it commonly leaves the time in minutes or hours instead of converting to seconds, or forgets to divide by the electrons per atom (the “÷ 2” for Cu2+). Treat any AI result as a claim to verify, not an answer: redo Q = I·t with time in seconds, divide by 96,485, then divide by the electrons in the half-reaction — and sanity-check that a small current over a few minutes plates a fraction of a gram, not kilograms. Reasoning through the chain yourself is the skill; the calculator is just an instrument.
🔑 CHECK YOUR WORK
▶  Answer key — open after you've tried all eight
Part A — classify the cells:
1. Galvanic (it produces electricity from a spontaneous reaction) and primary (single-use — an alkaline cell isn't rechargeable).
2. Electrolytic — it needs an external power supply to drive a non-spontaneous reaction (splitting water has E°cell < 0). Primary/secondary doesn't apply (it's not a battery).
3. Galvanic while discharging (it powers the phone) and secondary (rechargeable — the charger reverses the reaction).

Part B — identify the electrodes:
4. Zinc is the anode (it is oxidized); MnO2 is the cathode (it is reduced). Anode = oxidation, cathode = reduction — always.
5. In an electrolytic cell the anode is still where oxidation happens and the cathode still where reduction happens — but the signs are anode = + (positive) and cathode = − (negative), opposite a galvanic cell, because the external power source forces the polarity.

Part C — Faraday's law:
6. Q = 2.00 × 3600 = 7200 C; mol e− = 7200 ÷ 96,485 = 0.0746; mol Cu = 0.0746 ÷ 2 = 0.0373; mass = 0.0373 × 63.55 = 2.37 g Cu.
7. 20.0 min = 1200 s; Q = 1.50 × 1200 = 1800 C; mol e− = 1800 ÷ 96,485 = 0.01866; silver needs only 1 e− per atom, so mol Ag = 0.01866; mass = 0.01866 × 107.87 = 2.01 g Ag. (No “÷ by electrons” step beyond ÷ 1.)
8. 30.0 min = 1800 s; Q = 3.00 × 1800 = 5400 C; mol e− = 5400 ÷ 96,485 = 0.05597; mol Cu = 0.05597 ÷ 2 = 0.02799; mass = 0.02799 × 63.55 = 1.78 g Cu. Compared to item 6 (2.37 g): the charge passed here is smaller (5400 C vs. 7200 C) even though the current is larger, because the time is shorter — less total charge means less copper. Faraday's law depends on the charge Q = I·t, not on current alone.

Trap check: if an answer came out tiny (item 7 near 0.03 g) you may have left the time in minutes — convert to seconds and redo. If a copper answer came out about double (item 6 near 4.7 g) you forgot to divide by 2 for Cu2+.
