📖 WHAT THIS BUILDS ON
From this week's lessons: the oxidation-number rules and the OIL RIG definitions (8.01), the oxidizing-agent / reducing-agent vocabulary, and the half-reaction method for balancing redox in acidic solution. Here you'll assign oxidation numbers, name what is oxidized and reduced (and which is each agent), and balance simple half-reactions step by step — checking both atoms and charge by hand.
This is your week's “doing chemistry” work. Do it on paper or in your notebook — no spreadsheet needed this week.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
Electrons can't appear or disappear — whatever one substance loses, another gains. How do oxidation numbers let you track that transfer, and how do you balance an equation so that the electrons, the atoms, and the charge all account perfectly?
🧰 YOUR TOOLBOX
OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss (number up), Reduction Is Gain (number down). ·  Oxidized species = reducing agent; reduced species = oxidizing agent.
Oxidation-number rules: free element = 0 · monatomic ion = its charge · Group 1 = +1, Group 2 = +2 · F = −1 · O usually −2 · H usually +1 · sum = overall charge.
Half-reaction method (acidic): balance atoms → balance O with H2O → balance H with H+ → balance charge with e−.
⚠️ THE HABIT THAT CATCHES ERRORS — READ THIS TWICE
It is easy to reverse the agents: the species that is oxidized is the reducing agent, and the species that is reduced is the oxidizing agent — each is named for what it does to its partner. And a half-reaction is not finished until the charge matches on both sides, not just the atoms. Always close by adding up the charge on each side — if they don't match, your electrons are wrong.

6. Fe2+ → Fe3+  (the simplest case — no O or H to balance).

💡 WORKED EXAMPLE (the method, on a different half-reaction)
Balance MnO4− → Mn2+ in acidic solution.
Step 1 — balance atoms other than O, H: Mn is already 1 : 1. ✓
Step 2 — balance O with H2O: 4 O on the left, so add 4 H2O on the right: MnO4− → Mn2+ + 4 H2O.
Step 3 — balance H with H+: 8 H on the right (from 4 H2O), so add 8 H+ on the left: MnO4− + 8 H+ → Mn2+ + 4 H2O.
Step 4 — balance charge with e−: left charge = −1 + 8 = +7; right charge = +2. Add 5 e− to the left to bring it to +2: MnO4− + 8 H+ + 5 e− → Mn2+ + 4 H2O.
Type: electrons are gained, so this is a reduction half (Mn: +7 → +2). Charge check: left +2, right +2. ✓
✍️ PART D · EXPLAIN YOUR REASONING (SELF-CHECK)

In two or three sentences, answer in your own words — this is the reasoning behind the whole set: Why must every oxidation be matched by a reduction, and how does the charge check on a balanced half-reaction prove your electron count is right? Write your answer before you open the model response below.

▶  Model answer — open after you've written yours
Electrons cannot be created or destroyed, so any electrons one substance loses (oxidation) must be gained by another (reduction) in the same reaction — that is why the two halves always occur together, and why the electrons must cancel exactly when you add the halves. The charge check is the proof: because charge is conserved, a correctly balanced half-reaction has equal total charge on both sides. If the two sides do not match, you added the wrong number of electrons — the size of the mismatch is the size of the error. Matching charge (together with matching atoms) is what tells you the electron bookkeeping is correct.
🤖 AI IS AN INSTRUMENT, NOT AN ORACLE
If you ask an AI tool to do these, it will often mislabel the oxidizing agent (calling the oxidized species the “oxidizing” agent — it's reversed) or miscount the electrons in a half-reaction so the charge doesn't balance. Treat any AI answer as a claim to verify, not the answer: re-apply OIL RIG by hand (the oxidized species is the reducing agent), and add up the charge on both sides — if they don't match, the electron count is wrong. Reasoning from the rules and the charge balance is the skill; the tool is just an instrument.

Part A — oxidation numbers:
1. S in SO2 = +4. Two O give 2(−2) = −4; for the neutral molecule, S + (−4) = 0, so S = +4.
2. N in NO3− = +5. Three O give −6; for an ion of charge −1, N + (−6) = −1, so N = +5.
3. Cr in Cr2O72− = +6. Seven O give −14; for charge −2, 2Cr + (−14) = −2, so 2Cr = +12, Cr = +6 each.

Part B — oxidized / reduced & agents:
4. Mg: 0 → +2 (oxidized → Mg is the reducing agent). H: +1 → 0 in H2 (reduced → HCl / H+ is the oxidizing agent). Chlorine stays −1 throughout (spectator). (Hand-check the H: it goes from +1 in HCl to 0 in H2 gas.)
5. Al: 0 → +3 (oxidized → Al is the reducing agent). Cu: +2 → 0 (reduced → Cu2+ is the oxidizing agent).

Part C — half-reactions in acid:
6. Fe2+ → Fe3+ + e−. (Atoms balance; charge: left +2, right +3 + (−1) = +2. ✓) Electrons are lost → oxidation half.
7. Cr2O72− + 14 H+ + 6 e− → 2 Cr3+ + 7 H2O.
  • Balance Cr: 2 Cr3+ on the right.
  • Balance O: 7 O on the left → 7 H2O on the right.
  • Balance H: 14 H on the right → 14 H+ on the left.
  • Balance charge: left = −2 + 14 = +12; right = 2(+3) = +6; add 6 e− to the left → +12 − 6 = +6. ✓ Electrons are gained → reduction half (Cr: +6 → +3).
8. The Fe half loses 1 e−; the dichromate half gains 6 e−. Multiply the Fe half by 6, then add and cancel the 6 electrons:
  Cr2O72− + 14 H+ + 6 Fe2+ → 2 Cr3+ + 6 Fe3+ + 7 H2O.
  Charge check: left = −2 + 14 + 6(+2) = −2 + 14 + 12 = +24; right = 2(+3) + 6(+3) + 0 = 6 + 18 = +24. ✓

Agent-direction check: if you wrote that an oxidized species (like Mg or Al) was the “oxidizing” agent, flip it — the oxidized species is always the reducing agent. Charge check: if any half-reaction's two sides don't have equal total charge, your electron count is off — recount and redo.
