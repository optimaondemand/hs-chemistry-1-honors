Galvanic Cell Foundation — Begin Your Electrochemical Cell & Rate Build
VR Assignment · 20 pts · one headset session (~35–45 min)
🧘 PREFER NOT TO USE A HEADSET?
A full non-VR alternate — “Galvanic Cell Foundation (No-Headset Pathway)” — meets the exact same objectives and earns the same credit. Do one pathway, not both.
📚 WHAT YOU'RE DOING
You're starting the project you'll build across all four weeks of this module: the Electrochemical Cell & Rate Build — a working model you'll narrate on camera at the end of the module. This week you build the foundation: a galvanic (voltaic) cell. You'll set up two electrodes in two half-cells (beakers), join them with a salt bridge and an external wire, and mark which way the electrons flow. You'll label the anode (oxidation, −) and cathode (reduction, +), and draw the electron flow anode → cathode through the wire. One anchor sticky note — “Galvanic cell: a spontaneous redox reaction makes electricity; electrons flow anode → cathode” — will sit at the heart of the build all module. In later weeks you'll add a cell-potential panel (Week 2), real cells & electrolysis (Week 3), and a reaction-rate & catalysis demo (Week 4) — and finally record the full walkthrough.
🥽 WHAT YOU'LL NEED
▶  A VR headset with the OptimaXR app (or the OptimaXR desktop app).
▶  Your notes from Lessons 8.01–8.02 (oxidation/reduction and OIL RIG; anode = oxidation/−, cathode = reduction/+; electrons flow anode → cathode; the salt bridge carries ions).
▶  About 35–45 minutes in one sitting.
📍 HOW TO JOIN
Device: VR headset (or OptimaXR desktop app)
Session type: persistent (your placed objects stay between visits all module)
Session ID: see the pinned OptimaXR Sessions list in this module — join Electrochem & Rates Lab
Location: Electrochem & Rates Lab
Spawn point: Lab Bench A
When you arrive: a Whiteboard IFX facing the bench shows this week's Step 1 and the anchor rule “a spontaneous redox reaction makes electricity; electrons flow anode → cathode.” Two empty beakers, a set of electrode strips, a salt-bridge tube, and a length of wire are laid out on the tray, with labels you can pick up. A sticky note at your feet reads “Start here.” You won't have to guess what to do.
📝 WHAT TO DO, IN ORDER
This build follows an investigation arc: Observe → Question → Predict → Investigate → Analyze → Defend → Extend. Don't skip the prediction — commit to it before you build.
1
OBSERVE. At the bench, read the Whiteboard IFX and the anchor rule, and recall from Lesson 8.01 that when zinc and copper(II) touch directly in one beaker the reaction is spontaneous but its energy escapes uselessly as heat — no current. Leave open space along the bench to the right for the Week 2–4 builds (cell-potential panel, real cells & electrolysis, rate & catalysis demo).
2
QUESTION. On a sticky note, write the driving question this build answers: “How can I force this spontaneous redox reaction to send its electrons through a wire so they do useful work?”
3
PREDICT — commit before you build. On a second sticky note, write a committed prediction: in a zinc–copper (Daniell) cell, which metal will be the anode, which the cathode, and which way electrons will travel through the wire. Commit to it — you'll test it against your finished cell in Step 6.
4
INVESTIGATE — build. Spawn and place two beakers as your half-cells and stand an electrode in each (model the Daniell cell: a zinc electrode in one, a copper electrode in the other). Connect them with a salt bridge and an external wire, and add a sticky note on the salt bridge: “carries ions — keeps each side neutral.”
5
INVESTIGATE — label. Tag one electrode “Anode — oxidation (−)” and the other “Cathode — reduction (+).” Then use the 3D pen to draw an arrow along the wire showing electron flow from the anode to the cathode, labeled “e− flow: anode → cathode.”
6
ANALYZE. Compare your finished cell to your Step 3 prediction. Was your anode/cathode and electron-flow call correct? If you missed one, note on the prediction sticky what tipped you off (“An Ox, Red Cat”). Then place the anchor summary sticky note at the center — “Galvanic cell: a spontaneous redox reaction makes electricity; electrons flow anode → cathode” — and sign your name on it.
7
DEFEND. Get ready to make your case with a short Claim–Evidence–Reasoning (CER): which electrode is the anode and which the cathode, and which way electrons flow (Claim); what in your build shows it (Evidence); and why the chemistry makes it so (Reasoning). You'll submit this in the next section — a template and self-check model are provided there.
8
Lock & save. Lock your beakers, electrodes, salt bridge, wire, and labels so they aren't bumped, then take a snapshot and save it as ElectroRate_Wk1_YourName. This snapshot is your project's saved progress for next week.
9
EXTEND. Take one screenshot inside the headset that clearly shows your two half-cells, the salt bridge and wire, your anode/cathode labels, the electron-flow arrow, and your anchor note — that screenshot is what you'll submit. Then look ahead: next week you'll measure how hard this cell pushes its electrons (cell potential) and add a potential panel right here on the bench.
Need to stop partway? Because the Session is persistent, your placed and locked work will be waiting when you return. Save the ElectroRate_Wk1_YourName snapshot before you log out so nothing is lost.
📤 WHAT TO SUBMIT
1. Upload your in-headset screenshot showing the two half-cells, salt bridge and wire, anode/cathode labels, the electron-flow arrow, and your anchor note to this Canvas assignment.
2. CER reflection. In the comment box (or your notebook), write three sentences defending your cell — a Claim (which electrode is your anode and which is your cathode, and which way electrons flow), the Evidence (what in your build shows it), and the Reasoning (why the chemistry makes it so). Also note whether your Step 3 prediction matched your finished cell. (Your Claim will be used when your work appears in the class gallery walk later this module.)
▶  Self-check: a model CER (open after you've written your own)
Claim: The zinc electrode is the anode and the copper electrode is the cathode; electrons flow through the wire from the zinc anode to the copper cathode. Evidence: In my build the zinc electrode is tagged “oxidation (−)” and the copper “reduction (+),” and my 3D-pen arrow runs along the wire from zinc to copper. Reasoning: Zinc is oxidized (Zn → Zn2+ + 2 e−), and oxidation happens at the anode (“An Ox”), which produces electrons and makes it the negative terminal; those electrons are pulled to the copper cathode, where Cu2+ is reduced (“Red Cat”). Because electrons are made at the anode and used at the cathode, they must travel anode → cathode.
