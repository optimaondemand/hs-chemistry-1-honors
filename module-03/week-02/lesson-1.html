📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If atoms share electrons to form covalent bonds, how do we know which atom connects to which — and how many electrons go where?
In the last two lessons you saw atoms either transfer electrons (ionic) or share them (covalent). But "they share electrons" is a sentence, not a picture. Today you learn to draw the picture: a simple, powerful bookkeeping system — invented by G. N. Lewis with nothing but dots and dashes — that lets you map every shared and unshared electron pair in a molecule. By the end you'll be able to take a chemical formula and build its Lewis structure from scratch, using a repeatable six-step procedure. This is one of the most useful skills in all of chemistry: the structure you draw tomorrow predicts the molecule's shape, and its shape decides almost everything else.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to write the Lewis symbol for a main-group element using its valence-electron count.
2
I will be able to draw the Lewis structure of a small molecule or ion using the six-step procedure, showing bonding pairs and lone pairs.
3
I will be able to recognize when the octet rule does not apply (duets, electron-deficient atoms, expanded octets, odd-electron molecules).

📚 PART 1 · LEWIS SYMBOLS: DOTS THAT COUNT
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
You already know that an atom's chemistry is decided by its outermost electrons. If you wanted a quick, drawable way to show only those electrons — the ones that actually do the bonding — what would you draw around the element's symbol? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

In 1916, the American chemist Gilbert N. Lewis proposed a way of drawing atoms that stripped away everything except the part that does chemistry: the valence electrons, the outermost electrons an atom uses to bond. His notation is disarmingly simple. Write the element's symbol, then place one dot for each valence electron around it — on the four sides, pairing up only after each side has one. The result is a Lewis symbol. Because the number of valence electrons of a main-group element equals its group number on the periodic table (using the older 1–8 / A-group labels), you can read the dot count straight off the table.

·Na
Sodium — group 1 · 1 dot
·C· (+2 above/below)
Carbon — group 4 · 4 dots
:O: (+2 more)
Oxygen — group 6 · 6 dots
:Ne:::
Neon — group 8 · 8 dots (full octet)

Notice what the noble gas neon tells you: a complete outer shell holds eight electrons — four pairs. This is the heart of the octet rule: main-group atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons until they are surrounded by eight valence electrons, mimicking the stable arrangement of the nearest noble gas. When atoms bond, a shared pair of electrons — drawn as two dots or, more compactly, as a line — is a bonding pair. A pair of valence electrons that is not shared, sitting on a single atom, is a lone pair. Every Lewis structure is just an accounting of where the bonding pairs and lone pairs sit.

💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY
In 1916 Lewis sketched his cubical atoms and electron-pair bonds in a single paper — before anyone knew about quantum mechanics, electron spin, or orbitals. He had no way to see a shared pair of electrons; he reasoned to them from the patterns in how elements combined, much as Dalton reasoned to atoms a century earlier. The remarkable thing is that his dots-and-dashes bookkeeping survived the quantum revolution almost untouched. We now understand why it works far better than Lewis did — and yet chemists still draw structures his way, every day, because the model is simple, fast, and astonishingly predictive. It is one of the great bargains in science: a hand-drawing that keeps paying off.
📚 PART 2 · THE SIX-STEP PROCEDURE

Here is the payoff: a reliable recipe that turns a chemical formula into a structure. Follow these six steps in order and you'll draw the correct Lewis structure for the great majority of small molecules.

1 · Count total valence electrons
Add up the valence electrons of every atom (group numbers). For an anion, ADD one electron per negative charge; for a cation, SUBTRACT one per positive charge.
2 · Choose the central atom
The central atom is usually the least electronegative one (it shares the most). Hydrogen is never central — it forms only one bond.
3 · Connect with single bonds
Draw one single bond (one shared pair) from the central atom to each outer atom. Each bond uses 2 electrons.
4 · Complete the outer atoms' octets
Add lone pairs to each outer atom until it has 8 electrons (2 for hydrogen). Spend electrons from your total as you go.
5 · Put leftovers on the central atom
Any electrons still unspent become lone pairs on the central atom.
6 · Form multiple bonds if short
If the central atom lacks an octet, convert a nearby lone pair into a shared pair — make a double or triple bond — until its octet is complete.

Worked once: carbon dioxide, CO2

Step 1 — count: carbon (group 4) gives 4, each oxygen (group 6) gives 6, so 4 + 6 + 6 = 16 valence electrons total. Step 2 — central atom: carbon is less electronegative than oxygen, so carbon goes in the middle: O–C–O. Step 3 — single bonds: two C–O single bonds use 4 electrons; 12 remain. Step 4 — outer octets: give each oxygen three lone pairs (6 electrons each) to complete its octet — that uses all 12 remaining electrons. Step 5: nothing left for carbon. Step 6 — check carbon: carbon has only its two single bonds (4 electrons) — not an octet! Convert one lone pair from each oxygen into a second shared pair, making two double bonds:

:O::C::O:   →   O=C=O

Now every atom has a full octet: carbon shares four pairs (the two double bonds), and each oxygen has two bonding pairs plus two lone pairs. Count the electrons one last time — two double bonds (8) plus four oxygen lone pairs (8) is 16, exactly your starting total. Always close the loop by re-counting.

Three more, each starting from 8 valence electrons. Tap a card to see how the eight electrons get arranged:

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
Water, H2O — 8 electrons
O is central (H is never central). Two O–H single bonds use 4 electrons; H needs only a duet, so it's done. The remaining 4 electrons go on oxygen as 2 lone pairs. Oxygen: 2 bonding pairs + 2 lone pairs = octet. Bent shape (you'll see why next week).
⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
Ammonia, NH3 — 8 electrons
N is central. Three N–H single bonds use 6 electrons; each H has its duet. The remaining 2 electrons sit on nitrogen as 1 lone pair. Nitrogen: 3 bonding pairs + 1 lone pair = octet.
⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
Methane, CH4 — 8 electrons
C is central. Four C–H single bonds use all 8 electrons; each H has its duet. Carbon: 4 bonding pairs, no lone pairs = octet. Everything is used — a tidy, fully bonded structure.
▶  Think first, then check: How many total valence electrons does the ammonium ion, NH4+, have? (Watch the charge.)
Nitrogen (group 5) gives 5; four hydrogens give 4×1 = 4; total so far 9. But the +1 charge means the ion is missing one electron, so subtract 1: 8 valence electrons. Those 8 form four N–H single bonds — nitrogen has four bonding pairs, no lone pairs, a complete octet, and the whole ion carries a +1 charge. The charge rule in Step 1 is easy to forget; this is exactly why it's step one.

📚 PART 3 · WHEN THE OCTET RULE BENDS

The octet rule is a guide, not a law of nature — and a model that's honest about its limits is more useful than one that pretends to none. Four kinds of molecules break the octet, and a chemist who can spot them avoids drawing impossible structures.

Duets — hydrogen & helium
Hydrogen's first shell holds only 2 electrons, so hydrogen is "full" with a duet, not an octet. It forms exactly one bond and never carries a lone pair.
Electron-deficient — Be, B
Beryllium is often happy with ~4 electrons and boron with ~6. In BF3, boron has only three bonds (6 electrons) and no lone pair — a stable structure with an incomplete octet.
Expanded octets — period 3 and below
Atoms from the third row down can hold more than 8. PCl5 puts 10 electrons around phosphorus; SF6 puts 12 around sulfur. Second-row atoms (C, N, O, F) never expand.
Odd-electron molecules
A molecule with an odd total of valence electrons can't pair them all. NO has 11 valence electrons, so one electron is left unpaired — the octet simply can't be satisfied everywhere.
🔬 HOW SCIENTISTS KNOW · A MODEL'S LIMITS ARE PART OF THE MODEL
Watch what the exceptions teach you. The octet rule is not "wrong" because BF3 and SF6 exist — it is a powerful pattern that holds for most second-row elements and frays predictably elsewhere. A useful scientific model comes with a known map of where it works and where it doesn't. Knowing that boron runs electron-deficient and that sulfur can expand its octet is not an exception to understanding Lewis structures; it is understanding them. You'll see this same theme — useful models, honestly bounded — sharpen tomorrow when a single Lewis structure turns out not to be enough.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A Lewis structure is honest bookkeeping: count the valence electrons, place them as bonding pairs and lone pairs, and make every atom as close to an octet as it can get. The six-step procedure makes this repeatable; the exceptions — duets, electron-deficient atoms, expanded octets, odd-electron molecules — remind you that even a great model has edges. Master this drawing and you hold the key to the next two lessons: the structure you map today predicts the shape, and the shape predicts the chemistry.
