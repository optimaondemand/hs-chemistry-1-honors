📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
There are tens of millions of known compounds. How can chemists name every single one without ever inventing a new word?
A compound's formula and name carry real information — and chemists agreed long ago on a system so that “sodium chloride” means exactly NaCl to a scientist in any country. The system isn't memorization for its own sake; it's a language. Once you know whether a compound is ionic or molecular — which you can now tell from Lessons 3.01 and 3.02 — a small set of rules lets you translate a formula into a name, or a name into a formula, every time. This is the lesson where chemistry becomes something you can read and write.

📚 PART 1 · NAMING IONIC COMPOUNDS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
You already know NaCl is “sodium chloride.” Why do you think the chlorine part changed to “chloride” instead of staying “chlorine”? (No wrong answer — just commit to a guess.)

Start with the simplest case: a binary ionic compound — a metal joined to a single nonmetal. The rule is short: name the metal first, unchanged, then the nonmetal with its ending changed to “-ide.” So NaCl is sodium chloride, MgO is magnesium oxide, CaCl2 is calcium chloride, and Al2O3 is aluminum oxide. Notice that the subscripts (the 2 in CaCl2) do not appear in the name. For these simple ionic compounds the charges fix the ratio automatically, so the name doesn't need to repeat it.

But there's a wrinkle. Many transition metals can form more than one charge — iron can be Fe2+ or Fe3+, copper can be Cu+ or Cu2+. For those, “iron chloride” would be ambiguous: which iron? So we add a Roman numeral in parentheses to state the metal's charge directly. This is called the Stock system. FeCl3 is iron(III) chloride; FeCl2 is iron(II) chloride; Cu2O is copper(I) oxide; CuO is copper(II) oxide. The Roman numeral is the charge on the metal, not the subscript — a distinction worth nailing down now.

How to find the Roman numeral: In FeCl3, each Cl is −1, so three Cl give −3. The single Fe must balance that, so Fe is +3 → iron(III) chloride. The numeral is forced by the charges.
🔬 HOW SCIENTISTS KNOW · WHY A SHARED LANGUAGE MATTERS
Before the modern naming system, the same substance might be called by a dozen folk names — “oil of vitriol,” “blue copperas,” “Epsom salt.” Useful in a kitchen; useless for science, where a reader on the other side of the world must know exactly what you made. International chemistry bodies built nomenclature so a formula and its systematic name mean one thing, everywhere. Learning the rules is learning to write in the shared language of the discipline — the same reason mathematicians agree on notation. Precision in naming is a quiet form of honesty: it lets others check your work.
📚 PART 2 · POLYATOMIC IONS & MOLECULAR COMPOUNDS

Not every ion is a single atom. A polyatomic ion is a group of atoms bonded together that carries an overall charge and travels as a unit. These show up everywhere, so a handful are worth memorizing cold. When a polyatomic ion appears in a compound, you use its name as-is — you do not change it to “-ide.” So Ca(NO3)2 is calcium nitrate and (NH4)2SO4 is ammonium sulfate.

Now the other family. Molecular (covalent) compounds — two nonmetals — play by a different rule, because two nonmetals can combine in several ratios (carbon and oxygen give both CO and CO2). So here we use Greek prefixes to state exactly how many of each atom are present. The prefixes are: mono-1, di-2, tri-3, tetra-4, penta-5, hexa-6, and on up. One rule of etiquette: drop “mono-” on the first element.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE · PREFIXES ARE FOR MOLECULAR COMPOUNDS ONLY
Don't put Greek prefixes on ionic compounds. CaCl2 is calcium chloride, never “calcium dichloride” — the charges already fix the ratio, so no prefix is needed. And don't put a Roman numeral on a molecular compound. The quick test: metal + nonmetal → ionic (use “-ide,” add a Roman numeral only if the metal has variable charge); nonmetal + nonmetal → molecular (use Greek prefixes). Decide ionic vs. molecular first, then apply the matching rule.

A brief word on acids, which you'll meet properly later in the year. For now, two patterns: a binary acid (H plus one nonmetal) takes “hydro-…-ic acid” — HCl is hydrochloric acid. An oxyacid (H plus a polyatomic ion containing oxygen) is named from the ion: an “-ate” ion becomes “-ic acid” (sulfate → H2SO4, sulfuric acid) and an “-ite” ion becomes “-ous acid” (nitrite → HNO2, nitrous acid).

📚 PART 3 · WRITING FORMULAS FROM NAMES

Going the other direction — from a name to a formula — rests on one principle: a compound is electrically neutral, so the charges must add to zero. Write the cation and anion with their charges, then choose the smallest whole-number subscripts that make the total positive charge equal the total negative charge. When you need more than one of a polyatomic ion, wrap it in parentheses before adding the subscript.

Aluminum sulfate, worked step by step

Aluminum is Al3+; sulfate is SO42−.
To balance +3 against −2, find the least common multiple of 3 and 2, which is 6.
Need 2 Al (2 × +3 = +6) and 3 sulfate (3 × −2 = −6) → net zero.
Formula: Al2(SO4)3  (parentheses around SO4 because there are three of them).

A handy shortcut you may notice: the charge on one ion often becomes the subscript on the other (the “criss-cross”). It works because it guarantees the charges balance — but always reduce to the smallest whole-number ratio afterward, and check that the total charge really is zero. The shortcut is a convenience; the reasoning — charges sum to zero — is the thing to trust.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · THE TOOL CHECKS THE SPELLING, YOU CHECK THE CHEMISTRY
A naming tool like the one above — or an AI you might ask to “name this formula” — matches patterns. It can tell you whether your text matches a stored answer, but it can't tell you whether you reasoned correctly: that FeCl3 needs a (III) because three chlorides force iron to +3, or that a polyatomic ion keeps its own name. Always run the human check — do the charges add to zero? Is this ionic or molecular? — against the rules, not just the tool's verdict. The reasoning is yours; the tool only confirms the spelling.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Nomenclature is a language, and its grammar is simple once you decide ionic or molecular. Ionic: name the metal, change the nonmetal to “-ide,” add a Roman numeral only when the metal's charge can vary, and keep polyatomic ions' own names. Molecular: use Greek prefixes to count atoms. To go from name to formula, balance the charges to zero and use parentheses for repeated polyatomic ions. Master this and you can read — and write — the formula of almost any compound you'll meet this year.
