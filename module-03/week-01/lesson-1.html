📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why does a soft, explosive metal joined to a poisonous green gas make something you sprinkle on your food?
In Module 2 you learned that an atom's chemistry is decided by its electrons — especially the outermost ones. Now the payoff begins. Sodium is a metal so reactive it bursts into flame in water; chlorine is a choking yellow-green gas. Bring them together and they don't stay sodium and chlorine — they become table salt, stable enough to eat. This lesson is about the first great way atoms join: by giving and taking electrons. Once you see why an atom would surrender an electron, the periodic table you spent a module learning starts to predict what bonds will form.

📚 PART 1 · WHY ATOMS GIVE AND TAKE ELECTRONS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
A sodium atom has exactly one electron in its outer shell; a chlorine atom is one electron short of a full outer shell. If these two atoms met, what do you predict would happen to that one lonely sodium electron? (No wrong answer — just commit to a stance.)

Recall the pattern you found in Module 2: the noble gases — helium, neon, argon — are famously unreactive, and they share one trait. Their outermost electron shells are full. That full outer shell is a low-energy, stable arrangement, and it turns out to be a kind of goal that other atoms “chase.” For most main-group elements, a full outer shell means eight valence electrons — the so-called octet. Atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons until they reach an octet (a noble-gas configuration). This is one of the most useful predictive ideas in all of chemistry.

Now look at where elements sit. Metals, on the left of the table, have just a few valence electrons — only one or two or three to shed. Losing those few is the easiest path to the octet of the noble gas just below them. When an atom loses electrons, it now has more protons than electrons, so it carries a positive charge: it becomes a cation. Nonmetals, on the right, are only one, two, or three electrons short of a full shell; gaining those is their easiest path. An atom that gains electrons becomes negatively charged: an anion.

There is the salt story, atom by atom. Sodium (1 valence electron) loses it to become Na+, which now has the electron arrangement of neon. Chlorine (7 valence electrons) accepts that electron to become Cl−, which now has the arrangement of argon. One electron changes hands, and both atoms reach a stable octet. Notice the matchmaking: sodium needs to lose one, chlorine needs to gain one — a perfect 1-to-1 fit, which is exactly why the formula is NaCl.

💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY
In 1916 the German physicist Walther Kossel proposed that atoms bond by transferring electrons to reach the stable electron count of a noble gas — the picture you just walked through with sodium and chlorine. In the very same year, an American chemist named G. N. Lewis was proposing a different mechanism: atoms sharing electrons (you'll meet his idea in the next lesson). Two scientists, the same year, reaching for the same goal — a full outer shell — by two different routes. Neither was “wrong”; chemistry needed both ideas. That is a quiet lesson in perseverance: the model of the chemical bond was assembled piece by piece, by many hands.
📚 PART 2 · THE BOND, THE LATTICE, AND LATTICE ENERGY

So sodium becomes Na+ and chlorine becomes Cl−. Now there are two oppositely charged particles in the same space — and opposite charges attract. That electrostatic attraction between a positive ion and a negative ion is the ionic bond. There is no need for a special force; it is the same pull that makes a balloon stick to a wall, scaled down to atoms and made enormous.

But here is something that surprises many students. An ionic compound is not made of separate “NaCl molecules” floating around. Because every positive ion attracts every nearby negative ion (and vice versa), the ions pack together into a vast, repeating three-dimensional grid called a lattice. In a salt crystal each Na+ is surrounded by six Cl−, and each Cl− by six Na+, on and on through the whole crystal. The formula NaCl doesn't name a molecule — it names the ratio of ions (1 to 1) in that endless lattice.

🔬 WORTH KNOWING · WHAT SETS THE STRENGTH OF AN IONIC BOND
The energy released when gaseous ions snap together into a lattice is called the lattice energy, and the bigger it is, the more tightly the crystal is held together. It follows the logic of Coulomb's law — the attraction between two charges grows with the product of the charges and shrinks as the distance between them grows:
attraction ∝ (q1 × q2) ÷ d
So higher charges and smaller ions (which sit closer together) both make a stronger, higher-lattice-energy crystal.

That single relationship explains a striking comparison. In NaCl the charges are +1 and −1. In magnesium oxide (MgO) the charges are +2 and −2 — and the ions are also smaller. Plug that into the Coulomb logic: doubling each charge multiplies the charge product by four, and the smaller distance pushes it higher still. The result is that MgO has a far larger lattice energy than NaCl. You can even feel the consequence: ordinary table salt melts around 800 °C, while magnesium oxide doesn't melt until roughly 2800 °C — which is exactly why MgO is used to line high-temperature furnaces. Same kind of bond; very different strength, because the charges and sizes are different.

📚 PART 3 · WHY IONIC COMPOUNDS BEHAVE THE WAY THEY DO

Here is the part worth pausing on: you can predict the everyday properties of an ionic compound straight from the lattice picture. The properties are not a list to memorize — they are consequences of a tightly bonded grid of charged ions. Tap each card to see the property and the reason behind it.

Tap each card to reveal the property and the “why”:

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
High melting & boiling points
To melt the crystal you must overcome the attraction between every ion and its neighbors at once — an enormous amount of energy. That's why salt melts near 800 °C and MgO near 2800 °C. Bigger lattice energy → higher melting point.
⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
Hard, but brittle
Strike a crystal and a layer of ions shifts. Suddenly + ions sit next to + ions and − next to −. Like charges repel, and the crystal splits cleanly along that plane — it shatters rather than bends.
⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
Conducts only when molten or dissolved
Electric current needs moving charges. In a solid the ions are locked in the lattice — no conduction. Melt it or dissolve it in water, and the ions break free to move — now it conducts.
🔬 HOW SCIENTISTS KNOW · A MODEL THAT EARNS ITS KEEP
Notice what the “transfer electrons, then pack into a lattice” model just did: it didn't only describe salt — it predicted a whole set of properties (brittleness, high melting point, conducting only when molten or dissolved) that we can go and check in the lab. A model that makes testable predictions and survives them is a model worth keeping. Watch, though, for its limits: not every compound behaves this way, because not every bond is a clean electron transfer. That is precisely the door into the next lesson, where atoms share instead of give.
✨ THEN → NOW → FUTURE · WHY IONIC BONDING STILL MATTERS
Then: A century ago, Kossel and his contemporaries worked out that ionic compounds are held together by the electrostatic pull between transferred-charge ions in a lattice — the very picture you just built. Now: that same picture is at the center of one of today's most active fields, battery science. A battery works by moving ions; the salts you are studying (and close cousins of them) are the materials engineers tune to carry those ions faster and more safely. The push toward solid-state batteries — where a solid ionic compound replaces the flammable liquid inside today's lithium batteries — is, at bottom, a search for a lattice that lets ions slip through it easily while staying strong and stable. Future: chemists are still hunting for ionic solids that combine high ion mobility with a rugged lattice, an open problem that reaches into electric cars, grid storage, and the ceramics (like the MgO you met above) that line the hottest furnaces and spacecraft heat shields. The rule you learned today — charge and size set the strength of the lattice — is exactly the knob these researchers are turning.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

An ionic bond is electrons changing hands: a metal loses electrons to become a cation, a nonmetal gains them to become an anion, and opposite charges lock together into a vast lattice — not a molecule. The strength of that lattice (its lattice energy) grows with bigger charges and smaller ions, and from that one idea flow all the familiar behaviors of salts: high melting points, brittleness, and conducting only when the ions are free to move. Next we ask what happens when no atom is willing to simply give an electron away.
