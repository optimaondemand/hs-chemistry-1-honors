📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If two atoms both want electrons, and neither will give any away, how do they ever join?
Ionic bonding worked because a metal was glad to hand over electrons. But the air you're breathing is made of O2 and N2 — two nonmetals bonded to themselves. Neither oxygen atom is going to surrender electrons to an identical oxygen atom; there's no “giver.” So nature found another solution: instead of transferring electrons, atoms can share them. This lesson is about that second great bond — how sharing works, why some bonds are stronger than others, and why some shared bonds are lopsided enough to give a molecule a positive and a negative end.

📚 PART 1 · SHARING INSTEAD OF GIVING
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
Two hydrogen atoms each have a single electron and each “want” a full first shell of two. Neither is a metal eager to give its electron away. How might they both end up satisfied? (No wrong answer — just commit to a stance.)

When two nonmetal atoms meet, neither one will simply surrender electrons — both pull on electrons strongly. So they compromise: each atom contributes electrons to a shared pair that sits in the space between the two nuclei. Both nuclei are attracted to that same shared pair, and that mutual attraction holds the atoms together. This is a covalent bond: a bond formed by sharing one or more pairs of electrons. The beauty of it is that each atom gets to count the shared electrons toward its own octet, so both can reach a full shell at the same time — no winner, no loser.

Atoms can share more than one pair when they need to. A single bond is one shared pair (as in H–H or Cl–Cl). A double bond is two shared pairs (as in O=O, the oxygen you breathe). A triple bond is three shared pairs (as in N≡N, atmospheric nitrogen). More shared pairs means more electron “glue” pulling the nuclei together — which has real, measurable consequences for how the bond behaves.

Single bond (1 shared pair)
e.g. C–C, H–H. Longest and weakest of the three.
Double bond (2 shared pairs)
e.g. C=C, O=O. Shorter and stronger than a single bond.
Triple bond (3 shared pairs)
e.g. C≡C, N≡N. Shortest and strongest of the three.

Here is the trend worth carrying with you. As you go from single to double to triple bond between the same two kinds of atoms, the bond gets shorter and stronger. More shared pairs pull the nuclei closer (shorter bond) and take more energy to break apart (stronger bond). So for carbon–carbon bonds, strength runs C–C < C=C < C≡C, and bond length runs the opposite way. It's a tidy, dependable pattern: more shared pairs → shorter, stronger bond.

📚 PART 2 · WHO PULLS HARDER? ELECTRONEGATIVITY & POLARITY

Sharing is rarely perfectly equal. Atoms differ in how strongly they tug on the shared electrons, and that tug has a name: electronegativity (EN). It's an atom's pull on the electrons in a bond. Recall the trend from Module 2 — electronegativity generally increases as you move up and to the right on the periodic table, peaking at fluorine (about 4.0), the most electronegative element of all. When two atoms in a bond have different electronegativities, the shared pair drifts toward the more electronegative atom, and the sharing becomes lopsided.

It's worth being precise about what electronegativity really is, because casual words like “greedy” can mislead. An atom does not want or try to grab electrons the way a person wants something — electronegativity is a consequence of physics. The atom's positive nucleus pulls on the shared electrons, and the pull is stronger when the nucleus has more protons and the bonding electrons are close to it and poorly shielded by inner electrons. When a shared pair spends more of its time near the more electronegative atom, the whole molecule settles into a lower-energy arrangement — and it is that drop in energy, not any desire, that draws the electrons over. In short, electronegativity is a measurable property that comes from nuclear attraction, not a personality trait.

💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY
The idea that one atom pulls harder than another was old and vague until, in 1932, the American chemist Linus Pauling found a way to put numbers to it. Working from the energies of chemical bonds, he built the electronegativity scale you still use today — the one that assigns fluorine roughly 4.0 and drops toward the metals. That was the crucial move: turning a hand-wavy sense of “pulling power” into a measurable quantity that could actually predict whether a bond would be nonpolar, polar, or ionic. Pauling's book on the chemical bond became one of the most influential in the history of chemistry, and it is a good reminder that a big step forward is often not a new substance but a new way to measure something everyone had only been able to describe.

The size of the difference, written ΔEN, tells you what kind of bond you have — and bonding turns out to be a continuum, not three separate boxes. At one extreme, two identical atoms (ΔEN = 0) share perfectly equally: a pure (nonpolar) covalent bond. As the difference grows, sharing gets more lopsided — a polar covalent bond. Push the difference large enough and the “sharing” becomes essentially a full transfer: an ionic bond, right back where Lesson 3.01 started. The rough guideposts below are guidelines, not hard walls — nature shades smoothly from one to the next.

ΔEN (rough guide)Bond typeWhat's happening
about 0 – 0.4Nonpolar covalentElectrons shared (nearly) equally (e.g., Cl–Cl, C–H)
about 0.4 – 1.7Polar covalentShared but lopsided — partial charges form (e.g., H–Cl)
greater than ~1.7–2.0Increasingly ionicPull so unequal it's effectively a transfer (e.g., Na–Cl)

Take hydrogen chloride, HCl. Chlorine is much more electronegative than hydrogen, so the shared pair spends more time near chlorine. Chlorine takes on a slight negative charge — written δ− (“delta minus”) — and hydrogen is left slightly positive, δ+. The plus and minus signs aren't full charges (no electron was fully transferred); they mark a partial imbalance. We say the H–Cl bond is polar: it has a positive end and a negative end.

💡 WORTH KNOWING · PURE SHARING VS. LOPSIDED SHARING
When the two bonded atoms are identical — Cl–Cl, H–H, O=O — their electronegativities are equal, ΔEN is zero, and the pair is shared perfectly evenly. There's no δ+ or δ− end: the bond is nonpolar. Polarity, then, comes from a mismatch in pulling power. Same idea as a tug-of-war: two equal teams keep the rope centered; a stronger team drags it their way.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE · ATOMS DON'T “WANT” ELECTRONS
It is tempting to explain a polar bond by saying an atom “wants” or “tries to steal” electrons. Watch the difference:
✗ Not this: “In water, oxygen grabs the shared electrons because it wants a full shell.” — this hands the atom a goal it doesn't have.
✓ This: “In water, oxygen's nucleus (8 protons, lightly shielded) pulls the shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen's single proton does, so each O–H pair sits closer to oxygen — a lower-energy arrangement. That measurable pull is electronegativity.”
Same result, but the correct version explains the polarity with physics (nuclear charge and energy), not desire.

▶  Think first, then check: In an H–F bond, fluorine is far more electronegative than hydrogen. Which atom carries δ−, and is the bond polar or nonpolar?
Fluorine carries δ− (it pulls the shared pair toward itself) and hydrogen carries δ+. Because the two atoms differ in electronegativity, the sharing is lopsided — the bond is polar covalent. In fact H–F is one of the more strongly polar covalent bonds, since fluorine is the most electronegative element.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Covalent bonding is electrons shared, not given: nonmetal atoms pool electron pairs so both can reach an octet, and more shared pairs make a shorter, stronger bond. Whether the sharing is even or lopsided depends on a mismatch in pulling power — electronegativity — which places every bond somewhere on the smooth continuum from nonpolar to polar to ionic. That δ+/δ− imbalance, small as it looks, is about to explain why water behaves so strangely. Next: the rules for naming all of this.
