Marie Curie & the Atom Within — A Nuclear Case Study
Word (.docx) · 100 pts · teacher-reviewed · course-closing synthesis
📚 THE TASK
More than a century ago, a young scientist named Marie Curie chased a strange glow that no one understood — and in doing so helped prove that the atom, long thought to be the smallest, indivisible piece of matter, had a turbulent world inside it. This is your course-closing capstone — the last piece of writing in Chemistry I Honors, and your chance to pull the whole year's habits together: investigate a real problem, trace its history, weigh the evidence, and argue responsibly. You will investigate one contemporary nuclear application (nuclear energy, or medical isotopes), trace its scientific development from Becquerel and the Curies to today, evaluate the evidence, connect it to current research, and present a Claim–Evidence–Reasoning (CER) conclusion about its responsible use.
It is a case study joined to a scientific argument: a careful look at the history, then a claim defended with evidence and reasoning and accountable to the trade-offs. Choose one application below to investigate and argue about.
APPLICATION A · NUCLEAR ENERGY
Defend a position on the responsible use of nuclear power — fission reactors today, and/or the pursuit of fusion energy. Ground it in the chemistry: the decay/reaction type, the energy released through E = mc2, and the contrast of fission and fusion. Weigh low-carbon power against waste, accident risk, and the difficulty of fusion.
APPLICATION B · MEDICAL ISOTOPES
Defend a position on the responsible use of medical radioisotopes — imaging and therapy. Ground it in the chemistry: the decay type, the half-life that suits the job (e.g., a short half-life and gamma for imaging), and why a given tissue concentrates an isotope. Weigh real diagnostic and life-saving benefit against radiation dose and risk.
You'll write it in Microsoft Word, organized into the sections listed further down.
⭐ WHY THIS MATTERS
This case study is one of only two teacher-reviewed pieces in Module 9 (the other is your Nuclear Decay & Applications Exhibit VR project), and it is the final assignment of the course — the capstone that asks you to use every habit Chemistry I Honors has built: empirical questioning, evidence over preference, and honest reasoning about how science shapes the world. Your teacher reads it personally and responds to your reasoning — so make it your careful best, and ground the history, the science, and the argument in evidence, not in a guess. This is exactly Quarter 4's question in action: how does chemistry shape our world? — and its virtue, Responsibility: nuclear chemistry's power comes with real risks as well as real goods.
🧮 YOUR TOOLKIT (USE THESE EXACT IDEAS & VALUES)
The discovery of radioactivity: Henri Becquerel discovered radioactivity in 1896 (uranium salts fogged a photographic plate with no sunlight). Marie and Pierre Curie investigated it; Marie Curie coined the term radioactivity and, with Pierre, isolated two new radioactive elements, polonium and radium, in 1898. Marie Curie won the Nobel Prize in Physics (1903) and in Chemistry (1911) — the first person to win Nobel Prizes in two sciences.
What it changed: radioactivity is a property of the atom's nucleus, not of its chemistry — evidence that the atom is not an indivisible, unchanging ball. This is the Nature-of-Science idea: models change when the evidence changes.
Decay types & conservation: in a balanced nuclear equation, mass number (A) and atomic number (Z) are each conserved. Alpha (α) emits a 42He nucleus (A−4, Z−2); beta (β−) turns a neutron into a proton + electron (A same, Z+1); gamma (γ) sheds energy (no change in A or Z).
Half-life: t½ is the constant time for half a sample to decay; after n half-lives, (1/2)n remains. Carbon-14 (t½ ≈ 5730 years) dates once-living material.
Fission vs fusion: fission splits a heavy nucleus (e.g., uranium-235) into lighter nuclei + neutrons (a chain reaction); fusion combines light nuclei — 21H + 31H → 42He + 10n — and powers the Sun. Both release energy from a tiny lost mass via E = mc2.
Medical isotopes: technetium-99m (gamma, short ~6-hour half-life) for imaging; iodine-131 for thyroid therapy (the thyroid concentrates iodine).
Dose units: the gray (Gy) measures energy absorbed (1 J/kg); the sievert (Sv) weights that dose by how damaging the radiation type is. They are not the same number.
Where the field is now (broad strokes — verify before citing any specific): scientists are currently pursuing controlled fusion for power (magnetic-confinement tokamaks and inertial/laser approaches; laboratories have recently produced a fusion reaction that released more energy than was delivered to the fuel, though a working power plant is still years away); advanced and small modular fission reactors and better long-term waste strategies; and, in medicine, targeted radionuclide therapies that deliver a radioisotope directly to diseased tissue. Keep these general in your writing unless you can verify a specific figure.
🧠 YOUR REASONING TOOLKIT (how to build the case study)
Use these steps in your own words — don't just copy the headings.
Step 1 — Trace the history accurately, then bring it to now.
Lay out the arc your application rests on: Becquerel's discovery (1896), the Curies (polonium and radium, 1898; that radioactivity comes from the nucleus), and Rutherford (alpha/beta identified; the first transmutation, 1919), through to the present understanding — and then to where scientists are taking the field now. Use the toolkit dates and facts — don't invent quotes or specifics.
Step 2 — Make a claim and back it with the nuclear chemistry.
Pick application A or B and state a clear position on its responsible use. Then ground it in the chemistry that matters for your case — the decay/reaction type, half-life, the energy from E = mc2, fission vs fusion — not just a general opinion.
Step 3 — Weigh the costs honestly, then decide.
No nuclear technology is free of trade-offs: waste, accident risk, and the difficulty of fusion for power; radiation dose and risk for medicine. Name the strongest objection to your position, then answer it — reasoning from evidence, not preference (Honesty).
📝 STRUCTURE: WRITE THESE SECTIONS
Your capstone must include all six sections below. Together they carry the five moves of a full investigation — investigate a contemporary problem, analyze its history, evaluate the evidence, connect to current research, and present a CER conclusion — plus an honest societal cost/benefit.
1
The contemporary problem (investigate). Name the modern nuclear application you're investigating (A nuclear energy, or B medical isotopes) and the real-world problem or question it addresses today — for example, generating low-carbon electricity, or diagnosing and treating disease. Set up why it matters now.
2
Historical development (analyze the history). Trace the arc your application rests on: Becquerel's discovery (1896) → Marie and Pierre Curie (radioactivity named; polonium and radium, 1898; Marie Curie's Nobel Prizes in Physics 1903 and Chemistry 1911) → Rutherford (identified alpha and beta; the first transmutation, 1919) → to the present understanding. Explain the Nature-of-Science idea: radioactivity is a property of the nucleus, which helped overturn the indivisible-atom model — a case of a scientific model changing as evidence improved.
3
Claim & evidence — the nuclear chemistry (evaluate evidence). State a clear, defensible claim about the responsible use of your application (e.g., “Used responsibly, nuclear power is a justified low-carbon energy source,” or “Medical radioisotopes are worth their small radiation risk when the diagnostic benefit is real”). Then evaluate the evidence behind it: the relevant decay or reaction type, the half-life (and why it suits the job), the energy released via E = mc2, and — for energy — how fission and/or fusion work. Use the toolkit values accurately and keep fission and fusion distinct.
4
Connection to current research. Show where the field is going now: for energy, controlled fusion (tokamaks; inertial/laser approaches) or advanced/small modular reactors and waste strategies; for medicine, newer targeted radioisotope therapies. Frame it as “scientists are currently exploring…,” keep specifics general unless you can verify them, and name one open question the field has not yet settled.
5
Societal cost / benefit & responsibility. Weigh the real benefits against the real risks (low-carbon power vs waste and accident risk; diagnostic benefit vs radiation dose). Name the strongest objection to your position and answer it. Reason from evidence, not preference (Honesty thread; Responsibility virtue).
6
CER conclusion (present a Claim–Evidence–Reasoning conclusion). Close with an explicit CER statement: restate your Claim, cite the strongest Evidence (the nuclear chemistry and the cost/benefit), and give the Reasoning that ties them together. End on the big idea: the same nuclear change Curie first glimpsed can heal or harm, power or pollute — and using it well means understanding the chemistry and owning the costs.
✎ LENGTH & STYLE
Aim for roughly 600–800 words across the six sections. Use correct terminology — nucleus, radioactivity, alpha/beta/gamma decay, half-life, fission, fusion, E = mc2, dose. The history, the chemistry, the current-research connection, and the reasoning are what's graded — not polished prose. Ground your claim in the nuclear chemistry, not a hunch, and keep fission and fusion separate.
⚠️ A NOTE ON USING AI TOOLS
An AI tool will happily write you a history of Curie or a paragraph on nuclear power — and it can get things wrong: inventing a date, a half-life, or a dose or energy figure; confusing fission with fusion; or overstating how mature a technology like fusion power really is. If you consult one, treat it as a draft to check, never an answer to paste: verify every historical claim and every nuclear value against this assignment's toolkit and the primary evidence, and re-check the chemistry yourself. The instrument proposes; your reasoning verifies. That is exactly the AI-literacy skill this assignment is built to grow.
