From this week's lessons: choosing a radioisotope by matching its half-life and radiation type to the job (9.05), and the biological effects of radiation — dose units (gray vs. sievert), internal vs. external hazard, and evidence-based cost–benefit reasoning (9.06).

📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
A nuclear chemist's real skill isn't memorizing isotopes — it's reasoning from properties to the right choice. In Part A you'll pick a suitable isotope (or radiation type) for five jobs and justify each from half-life, radiation type, and how it interacts with the body or material. In Part B you'll reason about dose and safety: identify gray vs. sievert, explain an internal-vs-external hazard, and weigh a cost–benefit decision. Work each item before you open the matching ▶ Check your thinking box.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
How do I choose the right radioisotope for a job — and how do I weigh its benefits against its risks with evidence?

☣️ PART A · CHOOSE & JUSTIFY THE ISOTOPE (5 jobs)

For each job, name a suitable isotope or radiation type and justify it from half-life + radiation type + how it interacts with the body/material. (You may use the isotopes from Lesson 9.05.)

#The job
1Treat an overactive thyroid gland.
2Aim a radiation beam at a tumor from outside the body (and the same kind of source to sterilize equipment for years).
3Date a wooden artifact from an ancient settlement (a few thousand years old).
4Detect smoke in a small sealed household device.
5Continuously gauge the thickness of sheet metal moving along a production line.
▶  Check your thinking — Part A answer key
1. Iodine-131 (half-life ~8 days; β/γ) — the thyroid naturally concentrates iodine, so swallowed radioactive iodine self-targets the gland and delivers its dose right where it's needed, sparing the rest of the body.

2. Cobalt-60 (half-life ~5.3 years; γ) — gamma penetrates deeply enough to reach a tumor from outside (or to kill bacteria through packaging), and the multi-year half-life makes it a steady, long-lasting source. A ~6-hour isotope would be gone too fast.

3. Carbon-14 (half-life ~5730 years; β) — the clock length matches the age range (it works up to ~50,000 years), and wood was once living, so it took up carbon-14 while alive. A billion-year isotope like U-238 would barely have changed and couldn't resolve a few-thousand-year age.

4. Americium-241 (an α source) — alpha ionizes the air inside the chamber to allow a current, but it can't penetrate the sealed plastic housing, so it does its job without escaping the device. The weak penetration is the feature.

5. A penetrating source (e.g., a beta or gamma emitter), such as a long-lived industrial isotope — the radiation must pass through the metal so a detector on the far side reads how much gets through; thicker metal lets less through. A steady (longer half-life) source keeps the gauge calibrated over time. (Naming the radiation type + reasoning earns full credit even without a specific isotope.)
⚖️ PART B · DOSE & SAFETY REASONING (4 items)

Reason carefully — these are about thinking like a responsible scientist, not plugging in a formula.

B1. A report says a patient's tissue absorbed 0.02 J of energy per kilogram, and that this corresponds to a certain biological risk.
(a) Which unit describes the "0.02 J per kilogram absorbed" — the gray or the sievert?
(b) Which unit would you use to express the biological risk, and how is it obtained from the first?
B2. A small amount of an alpha-emitting material is genuinely dangerous if inhaled, yet nearly harmless taped to the outside of your hand. In two or three sentences, explain why the same source can be so different depending on whether it's internal or external.
B3. A patient is nervous about "any radiation at all" from a recommended diagnostic scan. Using cost–benefit reasoning (and the fact that we all receive background radiation), write a short, honest framing of how this decision should be weighed. (Take a defensible position — this is the Responsibility this quarter is about.)
B4. (Synthesis — self-check reasoning.) This week's throughline is that the same nuclear properties that make radiation hazardous are what make it useful. In three or four sentences, defend that idea using two specific examples from this week — one from a use (9.05) and one from an effect (9.06) — and finish by saying why that dual nature makes responsible, evidence-based decisions so important.
▶  Check your thinking — Part B worked answers
B1a. The gray (Gy) — it is defined as 1 joule of energy absorbed per kilogram of tissue (so 0.02 J/kg = 0.02 Gy). This counts energy only.
B1b. The sievert (Sv) expresses biological risk. You get it by multiplying the absorbed dose (in gray) by a quality factor that accounts for how damaging that radiation type is: Sv = Gy × quality factor. (For gamma the factor is ~1, so the numbers are similar; for alpha it's ~20, so the same gray means a much larger sievert.)

B2. Alpha particles are intensely ionizing but barely penetrate — from outside, the dead outer layer of skin stops them, so they never reach living cells. Inhaled, the source sits directly against living tissue with no shielding, so its high ionizing power (quality factor ~20) does serious local damage. Same particle; the hazard depends on radiation type and whether the source is internal or external.

B3. A strong answer notes that “zero radiation” was never the baseline — everyone receives background radiation (radon, cosmic rays, rocks/soil, food) all the time — so a scan adds a small dose, not a first one. Then it weighs that small, real risk against the benefit: the medical information the scan provides (catching or ruling out a serious problem). For a medically indicated scan, the benefit usually outweighs the dose, but it's a genuine trade made with evidence, case by case — not fear, not dismissal. (Any clear, evidence-based position that weighs benefit against risk earns full credit.)

B4. A strong answer picks two concrete pairings and shows the hazard-is-the-use logic in each. For example: gamma radiation's deep penetration is a real external hazard (9.06), yet that same penetration is exactly what lets Tc-99m's gamma rays escape the body to form a medical image, or lets Co-60 reach a tumor (9.05); and radiation's power to break DNA and kill cells is the danger behind cancer risk (9.06), yet that same cell-killing power is how radiation therapy destroys tumor tissue (9.05). Because every use carries the very edge that makes it dangerous, no application is automatically “safe” or “unsafe” — each is a trade to be weighed with evidence (gray vs. sievert, internal vs. external exposure, benefit vs. risk). That is why responsible, evidence-based judgment — not fear and not hype — is the real skill of this module. (Any answer with two valid examples and a clear responsibility point earns full credit.)
