📚 THE BIG PICTURE
Across this module you've assembled a Nuclear Decay & Applications Exhibit — on slides or as physical/drawn models — one week at a time: Week 1 a decay & stability model; Week 2 a half-life & energy station; Week 3 a radioisotope applications exhibit.
Now you record a narrated walkthrough of your build. The graded work is your spoken explanation of the chemistry, not the artwork — the build is the stage; your reasoning is the performance.
⭐ WHY THIS MATTERS
This walkthrough is one of only two teacher-reviewed pieces in Module 9 (the other is your Nuclear Case-Study writing assignment on Marie Curie). Your teacher will watch your recording and respond to your reasoning personally.
🎨 CHOOSE ONE FORMAT
Option A — Digital (screen-recorded slides)
Use your three weekly PowerPoint builds as the visual. Record your screen while you narrate a tour: the decay & stability model, the half-life & energy station, and the radioisotope applications exhibit.
Option B — Hands-on (physical or drawn models)
Use a built or drawn nucleus model (labeled protons and neutrons); a drawn band-of-stability chart (neutrons vs protons, noting no stable nuclei past Z = 83) with α/β/γ decay equations; a half-life decay sketch (a sample halving over successive half-lives); a fission-vs-fusion comparison drawing; and application cards for real isotopes (Tc-99m, I-131, Co-60, C-14, Am-241). Record a video narrating the same tour.
🎤 WHAT YOUR RECORDING MUST COVER (aim for 3–5 minutes)
1
Open with the big question: how do unstable nuclei change, how do we measure and date that change, and how do we use nuclear chemistry responsibly?
2
Decay & stability (Week 1). Walk your model. Explain that a nucleus is protons and neutrons (nucleons) held by the strong force, that stable nuclei fall in the band of stability with no stable nuclei beyond Z = 83, and walk the three decay types: alpha (α) emits a 42He nucleus, beta (β−) turns a neutron into a proton plus an electron, gamma (γ) sheds energy. Show that A and Z are conserved in each equation.
3
Half-life (Week 2). Show the half-life (t½) as the constant time for half a sample to decay; after n half-lives the fraction left is (1/2)n. Explain carbon-14 dating (t½ ≈ 5730 years): the fraction of 14C remaining gives a once-living thing's age.
4
Nuclear energy (Week 2). Contrast fission (a heavy nucleus like uranium-235 splits, releasing neutrons that can sustain a chain reaction) with fusion (light nuclei combine — deuterium + tritium → helium-4 + a neutron — and power the Sun). Explain that both release energy by moving products toward the iron (iron-56) peak, and that the energy comes from lost mass via E = mc2.
5
Applications & safety (Week 3). Tour your application cards: technetium-99m (imaging), iodine-131 (thyroid therapy), cobalt-60 (radiotherapy/sterilization), carbon-14 (dating), americium-241 (smoke detectors). For at least two, explain how the choice matches half-life and radiation type to the job. Close with a cost / benefit & radiation-safety statement — the properties that make isotopes hazardous also make them useful; using them well means weighing real benefits against real risks (Responsibility).
6
Close by explaining the evidence, then the through-line. Before your final line, make your reasoning explicit — state why mass number (A) and atomic number (Z) must be conserved in a decay equation, what a half-life actually tells you about a sample, why both fission and fusion release energy (products move toward the iron-56 peak; a tiny lost mass becomes energy via E = mc2), and the cost / benefit of one application you chose. Then connect the whole build into one idea: the same hidden change inside an unstable nucleus — measured by half-life and powered by E = mc2 — can heal, date, and power our world, and chemistry's job is to understand it well enough to use it responsibly.

📊 HOW THIS IS GRADED (TEACHER RUBRIC — build natively as a Canvas Rubric)
Criterion Full marks Developing Pts
Evidence-based scientific reasoning Explains the evidence, not just the labels: why mass number (A) and atomic number (Z) are conserved in every decay equation; what half-life means and how the (1/2)n logic tracks or dates a sample; why both fission and fusion release energy (products move toward the iron-56 peak; a tiny lost mass becomes energy via E = mc2); and the cost / benefit of at least one chosen application. Reasoning is justified from evidence throughout. States conclusions or names parts without explaining the underlying evidence; reasoning thin or absent. 35
Accurate chemistry The nuclear chemistry is correct at the high-school level: the band of stability (no stable nuclei past Z = 83), α/β/γ decay with the right A & Z changes, a constant half-life, the fission-vs-fusion contrast, and isotope-to-use matching are stated accurately, using the course's verified values. Contains chemistry errors — e.g., fission/fusion confused, broken A/Z conservation, mis-stated half-life logic, or invented values. 25
Clear narration & model The recorded tour is organized and easy to follow; the three builds (decay & stability model, half-life & energy station, radioisotope applications exhibit) are recognizable and support the narration; audio and visuals are usable. Disorganized or hard to follow; builds confusing, missing, or not tied to the narration; key parts unclear or inaudible. 20
Week-by-week iteration The build clearly shows all three weekly stages, and the comment lists the three builds — evidence of a project developed across the module, not assembled at the end. Only some stages present; little sign of building over time. 12
Uncertainty / limits & real-world reflection Closes by weighing a real cost against a real benefit (Responsibility) and naming a limit or uncertainty — e.g., that a model simplifies, that fusion power is not yet mature, or that the same radiation that heals can harm. No honest cost/benefit or limits; the technology is treated as risk-free or as purely dangerous. 8
