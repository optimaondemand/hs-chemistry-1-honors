Radioactive Decay & Half-Life

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A single radioactive nucleus is unpredictable — you cannot say when that one will decay. So how can a buried bone or a chunk of rock tell us, reliably, how old it is?
The answer is one of the most beautiful patterns in all of chemistry. Decay of a single atom is pure chance, yet a huge population of atoms decays on a clock so steady you can use it to read deep time. That clock is the half-life. In this lesson you'll see that radioactive decay is a first-order rate process — exactly the kind of kinetics you met in Module 8 — learn what stays constant and what doesn't, work the “fraction remaining” pattern, and meet carbon-14 dating, the activity units, and a calculator you can drive yourself.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain that radioactive decay is a first-order process (rate = kN) and that a nuclide's half-life is constant, independent of sample size, temperature, or chemical form.
2

3
I will be able to describe how carbon-14 dating works and identify the units of radioactive activity (becquerel and curie).

📚 PART 1 · DECAY IS FIRST-ORDER KINETICS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
You have a sample of a radioactive isotope. If you warm it up, crush it, or dissolve it in acid — do you think you can make it decay faster? Commit to an answer before you read on. (No wrong answer — just predict.)

In Module 8 you learned that the rate of a reaction tells you how fast it goes, and that a first-order reaction is one whose rate is proportional to the amount of stuff present. Radioactive decay is the cleanest first-order process in nature. The more radioactive nuclei you have, the more decays happen each second:

DECAY IS FIRST-ORDER
rate = kN
N = number of radioactive nuclei present · k = the decay constant (a fixed rate for each nuclide)

Here is the strange and wonderful part. Which nucleus decays next, and when, is genuinely random — you cannot predict a single atom. But with billions of atoms, the randomness averages out into a perfectly steady statistical pattern, the way a coin is unpredictable on one flip but lands heads almost exactly half the time over a million flips. That steady population behavior gives every nuclide a fixed, repeatable timing. We give that timing a name.

🔗 THE ARC THIS CONTINUES · BACK TO KINETICS
In Module 8 you studied reaction rates, rate laws, and first-order behavior for chemical reactions among atoms and molecules. Radioactive decay is the same mathematics — rate = kN, first order — but happening one level deeper, inside the nucleus, untouched by the temperature and concentration changes that controlled chemical rates. Same rate-law shape, a brand-new arena. That is why this final module can lean on tools you already own.
▶  Think first, then check: Two sealed vials hold the same isotope — one is kept in a freezer, the other in a warm oven. Does the cold sample decay more slowly?
No — both decay at exactly the same rate. The decay constant k (and therefore the half-life) is a fixed property of the nuclide and does not change with temperature, pressure, or chemical form. Heating speeds up chemical reactions because it stirs up electrons and collisions; nuclear decay happens far below all of that. This independence is exactly what lets a half-life serve as a trustworthy clock.
🔬 NATURE OF SCIENCE · HOW DO WE KNOW?
A half-life is not a number anyone chose — it is measured. Scientists count decays from real samples and find the same steady timing every time, and that timing does not budge when they heat the sample, cool it, crush it, or lock the atoms into different compounds. That is what makes it trustworthy: a quantity that stays constant no matter what you do to it is exactly the kind of empirical anchor science can build a clock on. Ask yourself: what observation would it take to convince you a half-life truly is fixed — and what would you have to see for the idea to fall apart?
📚 PART 2 · HALF-LIFE AND THE (1/2)ⁿ PATTERN

The half-life (t½) is the time it takes for half of the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay. The magic is that it is constant for a given nuclide: the first half takes the same time as the next half, and the next. Wait one half-life and half is gone; wait another and half of what remains is gone (leaving a quarter); and so on. After n half-lives, the fraction still left is:

FRACTION REMAINING AFTER n HALF-LIVES
fraction left = (1/2)n
where n = (elapsed time) ÷ (half-life) · mass left = initial × (1/2)n

Watch how fast it shrinks — each step is half of the step before:

The decay constant and the half-life are two ways of saying the same thing, tied together by t½ = 0.693 / k (the 0.693 is the natural logarithm of 2). A large k means fast decay and a short half-life; a tiny k means a long half-life. Half-lives span an astonishing range, from fractions of a second to billions of years:

⚠️ THE HALF-LIFE COUNTING TRAP
The classic mistake is to subtract halves linearly — thinking “3 half-lives means three halves are gone, so nothing is left.” Wrong. Each half-life takes half of what currently remains, never half of the original. The right move is always: count n = (elapsed time) ÷ (half-life), then apply (1/2)n. After 3 half-lives you have 1/8 left, not zero. Halving repeatedly never reaches zero — it just gets smaller and smaller.

📚 PART 3 · READING TIME: DATING, ACTIVITY, AND A CALCULATOR

A worked example — the (1/2)n pattern in action

A sample starts with 80.0 g of a nuclide whose half-life is t½ = 8 days. How much remains after 24 days?

Step 1 — count the half-lives. n = (elapsed time) ÷ (half-life) = 24 days ÷ 8 days = 3 half-lives.

Step 2 — find the fraction remaining. (1/2)3 = 1/8.

Step 3 — apply it to the mass. 80.0 g × 1/8 = 10.0 g.

Answer: after 24 days, 10.0 g remains. Notice we did not subtract three halves of 80 g; we halved, halved, and halved again: 80 → 40 → 20 → 10.

Because the clock is so steady, half-life lets us measure age. The most famous case is carbon-14 dating. Living things constantly take in carbon, so they hold a fixed, known level of radioactive carbon-14 (t½ ≈ 5730 years) while alive. The moment something dies, it stops taking in carbon, and its carbon-14 begins to decay on schedule. Measuring how much carbon-14 is left tells you how many half-lives have passed — and therefore how long ago the thing died. The method works on once-living material up to roughly 50,000 years old. For dating rocks and the Earth itself, scientists use far slower clocks like uranium-238.

💡 THEN · THE CLOCK IS DISCOVERED
Around 1902, working at McGill University, Ernest Rutherford and the chemist Frederick Soddy studied decaying thorium and reached a startling conclusion: radioactivity is one element transforming into another, and the amount left drops by a fixed fraction in each equal stretch of time. That regularity is the exponential decay law, and Rutherford gave the time for a sample to fall to half its name — the half-life. For the first time, decay had a clock. Decades later, in the late 1940s, the American chemist Willard Libby turned that clock into a ruler for time itself. Reasoning that living things take in carbon-14 at a steady level and stop at death, he developed radiocarbon dating to read the age of once-living material from how much carbon-14 remains — work recognized with the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1960. The steadiness you are calculating in this lesson is the very thing that made deep time measurable.
💡 THEN · THE NAME ON THE UNIT
How do we count decays? The becquerel (Bq) — one decay per second — is named for Henri Becquerel, who discovered radioactivity. The older, larger unit is the curie (Ci), equal to 3.7 × 1010 Bq, named in honor of Marie and Pierre Curie, who isolated radium and polonium and gave radioactivity its name. The number of decays per second a sample produces is called its activity — and because activity is proportional to the number of nuclei present, it falls by half every half-life, right alongside the mass.

⚡ FLIP CARDS · READ THE CLOCK
Predict each answer, then tap to flip and check.
After 4 half-lives, what fraction of a sample is left? — tap
1/16. (1/2)4 = 1/16 ≈ 0.0625. Halve four times: 1 → 1/2 → 1/4 → 1/8 → 1/16.
A bone has 1/4 of its original carbon-14. How old is it? (t½ ≈ 5730 yr) — tap
About 11,460 years. 1/4 = (1/2)2, so 2 half-lives passed: 2 × 5730 ≈ 11,460 yr.
Does halving 80 g repeatedly ever reach exactly 0 g? — tap
No. Each step takes half of what remains, so it shrinks toward zero but never reaches it in the model: 80 → 40 → 20 → 10 → 5 …
How many becquerels are in 1 curie? — tap
3.7 × 1010 Bq. A becquerel is 1 decay per second; the curie is the much larger unit named for the Curies.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AS A SCIENTIFIC INSTRUMENT
Half-life problems are exactly where an AI tool often slips: it may miscount the half-lives (subtracting halves linearly instead of dividing time by t½), or mix up time units (days vs. years), and then state a confident, wrong answer. Treat an AI's decay calculation the way a chemist treats any instrument reading — useful, but verified. Re-derive n = time ÷ t½ and (1/2)n by hand before you trust the number.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A single nucleus decays at random, but a population decays on a clock so steady you can read deep time with it. Decay is first-order (rate = kN); the half-life is constant for a nuclide, untouched by amount, temperature, or chemical form. After n half-lives the fraction left is (1/2)n, where n = time ÷ t½ — count the half-lives, then halve; never subtract halves linearly. Carbon-14 (t½ ≈ 5730 yr) dates once-living things; activity is measured in becquerels and curies.
