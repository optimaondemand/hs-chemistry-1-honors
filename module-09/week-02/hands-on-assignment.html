📖 WHAT THIS BUILDS ON
From this week's lessons: radioactive decay as first-order kinetics, the constant half-life, the fraction-remaining pattern (1/2)n, carbon-14 dating (t½ ≈ 5730 yr), and the iron-peak energy story behind fission and fusion.
This is your week's “doing chemistry” work. Two parts: a set of half-life problems you reason through by hand, and an Excel decay table + line graph that lets you see the exponential curve.
📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
Half-life math rests on one move done carefully: count the half-lives first (n = elapsed time ÷ t½), then apply (1/2)n. In Part A you'll work five problems — mass and fraction remaining, finding elapsed time from a fraction, and a carbon-14 dating age. In Part B you'll build a decay table in Excel and graph it, watching the exponential shape appear. Work each Part A item before you open the matching ▶ Check your thinking box.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
How much of a radioactive sample is left after a given time — and how can the amount remaining tell me how old something is?
⚠️ AI AS AN INSTRUMENT — VERIFY BEFORE YOU TRUST
If you ask an AI tool to do these half-life problems, watch it carefully. AI commonly miscounts half-lives (subtracting halves linearly instead of dividing time by t½) or mixes up the time units (days vs. years), then states a confident, wrong answer. Treat its result as an instrument reading, not the truth: re-derive n = time ÷ t½ and (1/2)n by hand and confirm the numbers yourself.

🧮 PART A · HALF-LIFE PROBLEMS (show your work)

For each: find n = elapsed time ÷ t½, then use fraction = (1/2)n and mass = initial × (1/2)n.

A1. A sample begins with 240 g of a nuclide with a half-life of 6 hours (like technetium-99m). How much remains after 24 hours?
A2. Iodine-131 has a half-life of 8 days. What fraction of an iodine-131 sample remains after 40 days?
A3. Cobalt-60 has a half-life of about 5.27 years. A medical source has decayed to 1/8 of its original amount. About how many years have passed?
A4. A wooden artifact is found to contain 1/4 of the carbon-14 that a living tree of the same size would have. Using t½ ≈ 5730 years, about how old is the artifact?
A5. A bone is dated to about 17,190 years old. Using carbon-14 (t½ ≈ 5730 yr), how many half-lives is that, and what fraction of the original carbon-14 remains?
▶  Check your thinking — Part A worked answers
A1. n = 24 h ÷ 6 h = 4 half-lives. Fraction = (1/2)4 = 1/16. Mass = 240 g × 1/16 = 15 g. (Halve four times: 240 → 120 → 60 → 30 → 15.)

A2. n = 40 days ÷ 8 days = 5 half-lives. Fraction = (1/2)5 = 1/32 (≈ 0.03125, about 3.1%).

A3. 1/8 = (1/2)3, so 3 half-lives have passed. Time = 3 × 5.27 yr ≈ 15.8 years.

A4. 1/4 = (1/2)2, so 2 half-lives have passed. Age = 2 × 5730 yr ≈ 11,460 years.

A5. n = 17,190 yr ÷ 5730 yr = 3 half-lives. Fraction remaining = (1/2)3 = 1/8 (≈ 12.5%).
📊 PART B · BUILD A DECAY TABLE & GRAPH IN EXCEL

A table of numbers becomes a story when you graph it. Start a sample at 100 g and watch it decay over six half-lives.

1
Make three columns: Time (half-lives) with rows 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6; Amount remaining (g); and Percent remaining (%).
2
Fill the amount column using a formula. Start with =100 at n = 0, then in each next row use a formula that halves the row above (for example, =B2/2). For percent, use =B2/100*100 or simply the amount ÷ starting amount.
3
Select the Time and Amount columns and insert a line chart (or scatter-with-lines). Title it “Radioactive Decay” and label the axes (x = half-lives, y = amount in grams).
4
In a cell below the chart, write one sentence: describe the shape of the curve and why it never reaches zero.
▶  Check your thinking — Part B expected table & curve
Your table should read:
n = 0 → 100 g (100%)
n = 1 → 50 g (50%)
n = 2 → 25 g (25%)
n = 3 → 12.5 g (12.5%)
n = 4 → 6.25 g (6.25%)
n = 5 → 3.125 g (3.125%)
n = 6 → 1.5625 g (1.5625%)

The curve drops steeply at first, then flattens as it approaches — but never touches — the x-axis. This is exponential decay: each step removes half of what remains, so the amount keeps getting smaller without ever reaching exactly zero. (A correct one-sentence note captures this idea.)
✎️ PART C · REASON IT OUT (WRITTEN SELF-CHECK)

Answer both prompts in 2–3 sentences each (in your notebook or the Canvas text box), in your own words — then open the model answer and compare. This is part of your honest self-check: no extra points, just a chance to be sure the ideas are solid, not only the arithmetic.

C1. In the (1/2)n model, explain why a radioactive sample never reaches exactly zero, even after many half-lives.
C2. Two students have the same nuclide but different amounts — one starts with 10 g, the other with 500 g. Will their samples share the same half-life? Explain why or why not.
▶  Check your thinking — Part C model answer
C1. Each half-life removes half of what currently remains, not a fixed amount, so you keep halving a smaller and smaller number. Halving never lands on exactly zero — it just shrinks without limit. (A real sample is a finite number of atoms and does eventually run out, but the (1/2)n model treats the amount as continuous and approaches zero without touching it.)

C2. Yes — both share the same half-life. Half-life is a fixed property of the nuclide and does not depend on how much you start with (or on temperature or chemical form). The 500 g sample undergoes more decays per second because it has more nuclei, but each sample still loses half of itself in the same span of time.
