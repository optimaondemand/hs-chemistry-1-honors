📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The alchemists spent centuries trying to turn one element into another and always failed. Yet a nuclear reactor does it every second, and the Sun has been doing it for billions of years. What changed — and where does all that energy come from?
Chemistry rearranges electrons; it cannot change one element into another. But reach inside the nucleus and you can. In this lesson you'll meet transmutation — literally changing one element into another — and the two giant nuclear processes that release the most concentrated energy we know: fission (splitting heavy nuclei) and fusion (combining light ones). Both pour out energy for the same deep reason, and both connect straight back to E = mc2 and the binding-energy story from Lesson 9.01.

📚 PART 1 · TRANSMUTATION: CHANGING THE ELEMENT
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
Medieval alchemists tried for centuries to turn lead into gold and never succeeded with their flasks and furnaces. Why do you think ordinary chemistry could never do it? Commit before you read on.

Transmutation is the changing of one element into another by altering the nucleus — specifically, by changing the number of protons. The alchemists could never manage it because every tool they had touched only electrons. The first deliberate, artificial transmutation came in 1919, when Ernest Rutherford fired alpha particles at nitrogen and detected protons coming out. He had turned nitrogen into oxygen:

RUTHERFORD'S FIRST ARTIFICIAL TRANSMUTATION (1919)
147N + 42He → 178O + 11H
an alpha particle strikes nitrogen-14, producing oxygen-17 and a proton · mass numbers (14+4 = 17+1) and atomic numbers (7+2 = 8+1) both balance

This is induced transmutation: a nucleus is bombarded with a particle to force a change. Today scientists do this on purpose in particle accelerators and cyclotrons, firing nuclei or small particles at targets at tremendous speed. This is exactly how the transuranium elements — the elements heavier than uranium, like plutonium and americium, none of which occur naturally in any abundance — are synthesized. The dream of the alchemists turned out to be real; it just required reaching inside the nucleus.

💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY
In 1919, Ernest Rutherford — the same scientist whose gold-foil experiment had revealed the nucleus a decade earlier — achieved the first artificial transmutation of an element. By bombarding nitrogen gas with alpha particles, he knocked protons loose and converted nitrogen into oxygen. For the first time, a human had deliberately changed one element into another, something every alchemist had failed to do. It confirmed that the nucleus could be probed and altered — opening the door to accelerators, synthetic elements, and the nuclear age that followed.
▶  Think first, then check: Why is making plutonium a job for a nuclear reactor or accelerator and not a chemistry lab bench?
Because making a new element means changing the number of protons in a nucleus — a nuclear change, not a chemical one. Chemistry only rearranges electrons and so can never alter an element's identity. Building a transuranium element like plutonium requires bombarding nuclei with particles (induced transmutation), which takes the energies of a reactor or an accelerator, far beyond anything a flask and a burner can do.
📚 PART 2 · FISSION AND FUSION

Two nuclear processes release staggering amounts of energy — and they are opposites. In fission, a heavy nucleus splits into lighter pieces. In fusion, light nuclei combine into a heavier one. Here is the comparison side by side:

⚡ FISSION — splitting
A heavy nucleus splits into two lighter nuclei + several neutrons + energy. Example: 23592U absorbs a neutron and splits (e.g., into barium and krypton isotopes) plus about 3 neutrons. Those neutrons can trigger more fissions — a chain reaction. Powers today's nuclear reactors and fission weapons.
☀️ FUSION — combining
Two light nuclei combine into a heavier nucleus + energy. Example: 21H + 31H → 42He + 10n (deuterium + tritium → helium-4 + a neutron). Powers the Sun and stars. Needs extreme temperature and pressure to force the positively charged nuclei together.

For fission, the key ideas are the chain reaction and critical mass. When U-235 splits, it releases neutrons; if at least one of those neutrons goes on to split another nucleus, the reaction sustains itself. The critical mass is the minimum amount of fuel needed to keep the chain going. The difference between a power plant and a bomb is control: in a reactor, control rods absorb extra neutrons and a moderator slows them down, holding the reaction steady so it releases energy gradually as heat. An uncontrolled chain reaction, with nothing to absorb the neutrons, is a fission bomb. Fusion, by contrast, is hard to start at all on Earth: the nuclei repel each other fiercely, so it takes the crushing temperature and pressure found in the core of a star.

D–T FUSION (powers the Sun and stars)
21H + 31H → 42He + 10n
deuterium + tritium → helium-4 + a neutron · A and Z balance on both sides
💡 THEN · A NUCLEUS SPLITS — AND NOBODY EXPECTED IT
In late 1938 in Berlin, the chemists Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann bombarded uranium with neutrons and were baffled to find barium — an element roughly half uranium's size — among the products. Nothing in the physics of the day said a heavy nucleus could break so cleanly in two. Hahn wrote to his longtime collaborator Lise Meitner, a physicist who had recently fled Nazi Germany and was working in exile in Sweden. Over the winter, Meitner and her nephew Otto Frisch worked out the explanation: the uranium nucleus had truly split, and the tiny mass lost in the process reappeared as energy through E = mc2. Frisch borrowed a word from biology — the way a living cell divides — and called it fission. Their interpretation was published in 1939, on the eve of the Second World War, and it changed history: the door to both nuclear energy and nuclear weapons had been opened.

📚 PART 3 · WHERE THE ENERGY COMES FROM

Here is the question that ties the whole module together: fission splits heavy nuclei and fusion combines light ones — opposite moves — so how can both release energy? The answer is the binding-energy curve from Lesson 9.01. Binding energy per nucleon peaks around iron (mass number near 56), the most stable region. Any nucleus can become more stable — and release energy — by moving toward that iron peak. Splitting a heavy nucleus moves the pieces toward iron from the right; fusing light nuclei moves the product toward iron from the left. Both downhill paths give up energy.

And that energy comes from mass itself. In both processes, the products together have slightly less mass than the starting nuclei. That tiny missing mass is converted into an enormous amount of energy through Einstein's relation:

MASS BECOMES ENERGY
E = mc2
because c (the speed of light) is so large, a minuscule mass loss releases a colossal amount of energy
🔬 NATURE OF SCIENCE · WHEN TWO IDEAS BECOME ONE
For most of the history of chemistry, mass and energy were treated as two separate things, each conserved on its own — mass balanced in the equation, energy accounted for in the heat of reaction. Einstein's E = mc2 revealed they are two faces of a single quantity: mass is a form of energy, and a nuclear reaction can turn a sliver of one into a flood of the other. This is one of the ways science advances — not only by piling up new facts, but by discovering that two things long thought unrelated are secretly the same. Ask yourself: where else in this course did a single deeper idea unify things that at first looked separate?
⭐ NOW · THE QUEST FOR FUSION POWER
Fusion is the cleanest, most abundant energy source imaginable — the same reaction that powers the Sun, with seawater as a fuel source and no long-lived chain-reaction risk. The catch is the difficulty: forcing nuclei together needs temperatures of millions of degrees, hotter than the Sun's core. Scientists are currently pursuing controlled, net-energy fusion — heating and confining a plasma using magnetic-confinement reactors (tokamaks) and laser / inertial-confinement approaches. Large international projects are working toward reactors that produce more energy than they consume. It remains one of the great open engineering problems of our time — a Grand Question still being answered.
🔄 THEN → NOW → FUTURE · THE ARC OF NUCLEAR ENERGY
THEN
Becquerel, and Marie and Pierre Curie, discovered and named radioactivity — the first hint that the nucleus itself holds enormous energy and can change on its own.
NOW
Controlled fission supplies a large share of the world's low-carbon electricity, and reactor-made medical isotopes (such as technetium-99m) image and treat patients every day.
FUTURE
Scientists are currently pursuing net-energy fusion and a new generation of advanced reactors (smaller, safer, less long-lived waste). Whether fusion becomes a practical power source is still an open question researchers are actively working to answer.
🔗 THE ARC THIS COMPLETES · WHAT DRIVES CHANGE
Trace the energy thread across the whole course. In Module 5 you measured the energy of chemical reactions as heat (enthalpy, ΔH) — energy from rearranging electrons and bonds. In Modules 7–8 you saw what drives change: free energy and cell voltage. Now, in the nucleus, you reach the deepest energy source of all — E = mc2, energy locked in mass itself. Nuclear energy dwarfs chemical energy by millions of times for the same amount of matter, because it taps the binding of the nucleus rather than the bonding of electrons. This is the final, deepest answer in the course-long story of “what drives change.”
⚡ FLIP CARDS · ENERGY FROM THE NUCLEUS
Predict each answer, then tap to flip and check.
Why do BOTH fission and fusion release energy? — tap
Both move products toward the iron peak. Binding energy per nucleon is greatest around iron; splitting heavy nuclei and fusing light ones both climb toward that most-stable region, releasing energy.
What process powers the Sun? — tap
Fusion. Light nuclei (hydrogen isotopes) combine into helium, releasing energy — the same reaction fusion researchers are trying to harness on Earth.
Where does nuclear energy actually come from? — tap
From mass. A tiny loss of mass is converted to energy via E = mc2; because c is huge, a minuscule mass change releases enormous energy.
What is the difference between a reactor and a bomb? — tap
Control. A reactor uses control rods and a moderator to hold the chain reaction steady; an uncontrolled chain reaction is a fission bomb.
▶  Think first, then check: Iron-56 sits at the binding-energy peak. Would fusing two iron nuclei release energy?
No — it would cost energy. Energy is released only when a process moves nuclei toward the iron peak. Iron is already at the most stable region, so fusing past it (or splitting below it) moves away from maximum stability and requires energy input rather than releasing it. That is exactly why stars stop producing energy once their cores build up to iron.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Reaching inside the nucleus lets us do what chemistry never could: transmute one element into another (Rutherford, 1919; accelerators make the transuranium elements). Fission splits heavy nuclei in a controllable chain reaction (reactors) or an uncontrolled one (bombs); fusion combines light nuclei and powers the Sun, with net-energy fusion still an open research frontier. Both release energy because both move products toward the iron binding-energy peak, converting a tiny bit of mass into enormous energy via E = mc2 — the deepest answer in the course's long story of what drives change.
