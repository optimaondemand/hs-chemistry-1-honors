Lesson 9.02
Nuclear Equations & Decay Types
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Last lesson you learned which nuclei are unstable. Now the question becomes: when an unstable nucleus finally lets go, what actually comes out — and what is it left behind as?
A radioactive nucleus reaches stability by ejecting particles or energy — and it leaves behind a different nucleus, often a different element entirely. This lesson gives you the small toolkit chemists use to track exactly what happens: two simple conservation rules, the handful of decay types (alpha, beta, positron, electron capture, gamma), and how to write a balanced nuclear equation for each. You'll also meet the scientists — Becquerel and the Curies and Rutherford — who first dragged this hidden world into the light.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to balance a nuclear equation by conserving total mass number (A) and total atomic number (Z) on both sides.
2
I will be able to identify alpha, beta-minus, positron, electron-capture, and gamma decay and state how each changes (or doesn't change) A and Z.
3
I will be able to rank the penetrating power of alpha, beta, and gamma radiation and explain why ionizing power runs the opposite way.

📚 PART 1 · TWO RULES THAT NEVER BREAK
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
In Module 4 you balanced chemical equations by making sure every atom appears on both sides. A nuclear reaction turns one element into another — atoms aren't conserved at all. So what do you think stays the same on both sides of a nuclear equation? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

A nuclear equation looks a lot like a chemical equation, but it tracks nuclei and nuclear particles instead of whole atoms and molecules. Balancing one comes down to two rules that never break:

THE TWO CONSERVATION RULES
Sum of mass numbers (A) is equal on both sides
Sum of atomic numbers (Z) is equal on both sides
balance the top row (A) and the bottom row (Z), and the equation is balanced

To use the rules you need the nuclide notation for the small particles too. Each is written with its own A (top) and Z (bottom), where for these particles “Z” really just means the charge:

Particle Symbol A (top) Z (bottom)
Alpha particle (helium nucleus)42He42
Beta particle (electron) 0−1e0−1
Positron 0+1e0+1
Gamma ray (photon)00γ00
💡 THEN · THE DISCOVERY OF RADIOACTIVITY
No one went looking for radioactivity — it was stumbled upon. In 1896, the French physicist Henri Becquerel left uranium salts resting on a wrapped photographic plate in a dark drawer and later found the plate fogged, as if exposed to light. The uranium was giving off its own invisible rays, with no sunlight involved. Marie and Pierre Curie took up the mystery; Marie Curie coined the word radioactivity, and together they isolated two new radioactive elements, polonium and radium, in 1898 — painstaking work with tons of ore. Marie Curie went on to win Nobel Prizes in Physics (1903, shared with Pierre Curie and Becquerel) and Chemistry (1911), becoming the first person to win Nobels in two different sciences. Meanwhile Ernest Rutherford sorted the radiation into types, naming the alpha and beta rays and showing that an alpha particle is simply a helium nucleus; the French physicist Paul Villard identified the even more penetrating gamma rays a few years later, in 1900. Their evidence did something profound: it showed that the atom — long imagined as the indivisible, unchangeable building block of matter, an idea that had stood since the ancient Greeks — can break apart and transform on its own. That overturned model is also what opened the door to modern nuclear medicine, where controlled decay now lets doctors see inside a living body. The vocabulary you're about to use is theirs.
▶  Think first, then check: A nucleus emits one alpha particle (42He). Using only the two conservation rules, how must the parent's A and Z change to make the equation balance?
The alpha particle carries away A = 4 and Z = 2. For both totals to stay equal, the daughter nucleus must have A reduced by 4 and Z reduced by 2. (Check: parent A = daughter A + 4; parent Z = daughter Z + 2.) That's all alpha decay is — the two rules in action.
📚 PART 2 · THE FIVE WAYS A NUCLEUS DECAYS

Each decay type is the band-of-stability story from Lesson 9.01 playing out. A nucleus shifts its neutron-to-proton balance toward the band — and the conservation rules tell us exactly what it emits and what it becomes.

Alpha (α) decay — A − 4, Z − 2

A heavy nucleus ejects an alpha particle, a helium-4 nucleus (42He). The daughter loses 4 from its mass number and 2 from its atomic number. This is how very heavy nuclei (Z > 83) shed bulk:

238 92U → 234 90Th + 42He

Check: top 238 = 234 + 4. Bottom 92 = 90 + 2. Balanced.

Beta-minus (β−) decay — A unchanged, Z + 1

A nucleus with too many neutrons (above the band) converts a neutron into a proton, ejecting a fast electron, the beta particle ( 0−1e). The mass number doesn't change (a neutron became a proton — still one nucleon), but the atomic number rises by 1, so it becomes the next element up:

14 6C → 14 7N +  0−1e

Check: top 14 = 14 + 0. Bottom 6 = 7 + (−1). Balanced.

Positron emission & electron capture — Z − 1

A nucleus with too few neutrons (below the band) does the reverse: it turns a proton into a neutron. It can do this two ways. In positron emission, it ejects a positron ( 0+1e), an antimatter twin of the electron, e.g. 18 9F → 18 8O +  0+1e. In electron capture, the nucleus instead pulls in one of its own inner-shell electrons. Both routes leave A unchanged and lower Z by 1 — the same net change.

Gamma (γ) emission — no change in A or Z

After one of the above, a nucleus is often left “jittery” with excess energy. It settles by releasing a gamma ray (00γ), a high-energy photon. Because the photon has no mass number and no charge, neither A nor Z changes — the nucleus is the same nuclide, just calmer. Gamma usually accompanies another decay rather than happening alone.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AS A SCIENTIFIC INSTRUMENT
The balancer above applies the two rules mechanically — but if you ask a general AI chatbot to balance a nuclear equation, treat its answer with care. AI tools can quietly miscount: dropping or adding one to A or Z, or pairing the right numbers with the wrong element symbol. You are the verifier. For every equation, confirm by hand that the mass numbers (A) sum equally on both sides and the atomic numbers (Z) do too, then check the symbol against a periodic table. Conservation of A and Z is the primary evidence; the AI is only a second opinion you check, never the authority.
📚 PART 3 · HOW FAR DOES THE RADIATION GO?

The three classic radiations behave very differently once they leave the nucleus, and the difference matters enormously for safety (the subject of Lesson 9.06). Their penetrating power — how far they travel through matter before being stopped — runs in this order:

Radiation Penetrating power Stopped by
Alpha (α)least penetratinga sheet of paper, skin, or clothing
Beta (β)moderatea few mm of aluminum or plastic
Gamma (γ)most penetratingthick lead or concrete

So alpha < beta < gamma in how far they reach. But here's the twist: their ionizing power — how strongly they tear electrons off the atoms they pass — runs the opposite way. The big, heavy, doubly charged alpha particle is the most ionizing precisely because it interacts so strongly with matter, which is also why it stops so quickly. This is why an alpha source is fairly harmless outside the body (paper or skin stops it) but dangerous if a source is swallowed or inhaled, where it can do concentrated damage. You'll build on this idea directly in Lesson 9.06.

Who first named the alpha and beta rays? — tap
Ernest Rutherford — and he showed an alpha particle is a helium nucleus. Becquerel discovered radioactivity (1896); the Curies isolated polonium and radium (1898).
Why is an alpha source dangerous if inhaled but not from across the room? — tap
Alpha is the most ionizing but least penetrating: skin/clothing stop it outside, but inside the body it deposits all that ionizing damage in one place.
⭐ THEN → NOW → FUTURE · DECAY BECOMES MEDICINE
Then: Becquerel, the Curies, and Rutherford revealed that unstable nuclei emit particles and rays entirely on their own. Now: that same decay has become one of medicine's most powerful tools. Doctors introduce tiny, carefully chosen amounts of a radioactive nuclide and let the radiation it emits map the body from the inside. Technetium-99m, a gamma emitter, is currently the most widely used isotope in diagnostic imaging, and fluorine-18, a positron emitter, is what makes a PET (positron-emission tomography) scan possible — the very gamma and positron decays you balanced a moment ago. Future: researchers are actively exploring “theranostic” isotopes that might both find and treat disease, and shorter-lived tracers that could lower the dose a patient receives. The hidden world Becquerel stumbled onto in 1896 is still opening up.
🔗 CONNECTING ACROSS THE COURSE · HOW SCIENTISTS KNOW
Notice how this discovery story echoes the nature of science you've met all year. Becquerel did not predict radioactivity — an unexpected fogged plate forced a new question, and the Curies and Rutherford followed the evidence wherever it led, even when it meant the atom was not the indivisible, unchangeable thing earlier scientists had assumed. That is the same model-change pattern you saw in Module 2, when new evidence redrew the atom itself: durable scientific knowledge is not fixed — it is revisable when the evidence demands. The same balancing discipline you learned for chemical equations in Module 4 reappears here, just with new conserved quantities (A and Z). Worth pausing on: the old atomic model didn't simply collapse — it was revised. Which parts of the Module 2 atom survived this discovery, and which parts did the new evidence overturn?
▶  Think first, then check: Fill the blank: 234 90Th → ___ +  0−1e. What is the daughter nuclide?
This is beta-minus decay (a  0−1e is emitted). Balance the rules: top, 234 = A + 0, so A = 234. Bottom, 90 = Z + (−1), so Z = 91. Element 91 is protactinium, so the daughter is 234 91Pa. (Beta-minus: A unchanged, Z up by one — exactly the rule.)

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Every nuclear equation balances two totals: the mass number A and the atomic number Z. From those two rules, every decay type follows — alpha (A−4, Z−2), beta-minus (A same, Z+1), positron / electron capture (A same, Z−1), and gamma (no change). The three classic radiations rank alpha < beta < gamma in penetrating power and the reverse in ionizing power — a fact with real safety stakes. This whole vocabulary was won by Becquerel, the Curies, and Rutherford following unexpected evidence. Next: how quickly a nucleus decays — the idea of half-life.
