📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
All year you have treated the atom's nucleus as a tiny, unchanging dot of positive charge. But here is a strange fact: some atoms are perfectly stable and sit unchanged for billions of years, while others fall apart on their own. Why are some atoms radioactive and others not?
In Module 2 you learned the atom has a dense central nucleus, and you counted its protons and neutrons. We then left the nucleus alone and spent the year on electrons — where all of chemistry's bonding and reacting happens. This final module finally looks inside the nucleus itself. Today you'll meet the particles that live there, the force that holds them together against enormous repulsion, the energy locked inside every nucleus, and the pattern — the band of stability — that decides which atoms last forever and which are doomed to decay.

📚 PART 1 · INSIDE THE NUCLEUS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
A lump of ordinary carbon and a lump of uranium are both made of atoms with a nucleus. Yet the carbon just sits there while the uranium slowly, steadily decays and gives off radiation. Before you read on — what do you think makes the difference? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

Start with what lives inside the nucleus. Packed into that tiny central region are two kinds of particle: positively charged protons and uncharged neutrons. Together, protons and neutrons are called nucleons — the particles of the nucleus. The number of protons is the atomic number, Z, and it alone decides which element you have. The total number of nucleons (protons + neutrons) is the mass number, A. So the neutron count is simply A − Z.

Chemists write all of this in a compact shorthand called nuclide notation: the mass number A goes above and the atomic number Z below, just before the element symbol. Carbon-14, for example, is written:

NUCLIDE NOTATION
14 6C
A = 14 (mass number, top) · Z = 6 (atomic number, bottom) · neutrons = A − Z = 8

Atoms of the same element always share the same Z (that's what makes them that element), but they can carry different numbers of neutrons — and therefore different mass numbers A. These variants are called isotopes. Carbon-12 (12 6C), carbon-13, and carbon-14 are all carbon (Z = 6), differing only in neutron count. A specific kind of nucleus — a particular A and Z — is called a nuclide. This is the same atomic bookkeeping you did in Module 2; now we put it to work to ask which nuclides survive and which fall apart.

💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY
That there even is a nucleus to look inside was itself a hard-won discovery — and finding it overturned the atomic model scientists had accepted. In the experiments you met in Module 2, Ernest Rutherford and his team fired alpha particles at a thin sheet of gold foil and found that a few bounced sharply backward — something impossible if the atom were the soft, evenly spread blob of charge the earlier “plum-pudding” model described. The only explanation was that nearly all of an atom's mass and all of its positive charge are concentrated in a minuscule central nucleus. A single stubborn observation forced a better model — and in Lesson 9.02 you'll watch that happen again, when unexpected evidence reveals the nucleus is not unchangeable either. Everything in this module unfolds in that tiny space Rutherford uncovered.
▶  Think first, then check: The nuclide 238 92U. How many protons, neutrons, and nucleons does it have?
Z = 92, so there are 92 protons (that's what makes it uranium). The mass number A = 238 is the total nucleon count, so there are 238 nucleons in all. Neutrons = A − Z = 238 − 92 = 146 neutrons. Notice already how many more neutrons than protons a heavy nucleus carries — that's a clue we'll return to in Part 3.
📚 PART 2 · WHAT HOLDS A NUCLEUS TOGETHER

Here is a puzzle. Like charges repel, and a nucleus is a crowd of positive protons jammed into an unimaginably small space. The electrostatic repulsion between them is enormous. By the rules of electricity alone, every nucleus should instantly blow itself apart. They don't — so something stronger must be at work.

That something is the strong nuclear force. It is an attraction that acts between nucleons (protons and neutrons alike), and it is far stronger than electrical repulsion — but only over an extremely short, sub-nuclear range. Within touching distance, the strong force easily overpowers proton repulsion and binds the nucleus together. Reach past that tiny range and it fades to nothing. This short reach is exactly why neutrons matter so much: adding neutrons adds strong-force “glue” without adding any new repulsion, which is why larger nuclei need extra neutrons to hold together.

Now for the most astonishing fact about the nucleus. If you could weigh a nucleus and then weigh all of its protons and neutrons separately, the separated pieces would weigh more than the assembled nucleus. The difference is called the mass defect (Δm). Mass appears to have gone missing — but it hasn't. That missing mass was converted into the energy that binds the nucleus together, through Einstein's famous relationship:

MASS BECOMES BINDING ENERGY
E = mc2
a tiny mass defect Δm × (speed of light)2 = a huge nuclear binding energy

The nuclear binding energy is the energy that would be required to pull a nucleus apart into its individual nucleons — the same energy that was released when those nucleons first came together. A more useful way to compare nuclei is the binding energy per nucleon: how tightly each individual nucleon is held. The larger the binding energy per nucleon, the more stable the nucleus.

When you plot binding energy per nucleon against mass number, the curve climbs steeply for light nuclei, reaches a broad peak around iron (mass number near 56), and then slowly declines for the heaviest nuclei. Iron-region nuclei are the most tightly bound — the most stable matter there is. This single curve quietly explains where nuclear energy comes from: any process that moves a nucleus toward the iron peak releases energy. Light nuclei release energy by fusing together toward iron; very heavy nuclei release energy by splitting down toward iron. We'll unpack both in Lesson 9.04 — but the reason both can release energy is right here in this one curve.

For this course, the dependable, evidence-based statement is that binding energy per nucleon peaks in the iron region (A ≈ 56). Treat an AI answer the way a chemist treats any instrument reading: useful for orientation, but checked against your trusted reference before you trust it.
📚 PART 3 · THE BAND OF STABILITY

Now we can answer the opening question. Imagine a graph with the number of protons (Z) along the bottom and the number of neutrons (N) up the side, and a dot placed for every nucleus that exists. The stable nuclides do not scatter randomly — they cluster along a narrow diagonal stripe called the band (or belt) of stability. A nucleus is stable when its neutron-to-proton balance lands inside that band.

For the lightest elements, the band runs close to a 1-to-1 line: stable light nuclei have roughly equal numbers of neutrons and protons (N/Z ≈ 1). But as Z grows, the band bends upward away from that line — heavier stable nuclei need proportionally more neutrons (N/Z climbing toward about 1.5). The reason is exactly the short-range strong force from Part 2: extra neutrons add binding attraction without adding any new proton repulsion, so big nuclei need that extra neutron glue just to hold together. And there is a hard ceiling: no nuclide beyond Z = 83 (bismuth) is stable. Every element heavier than bismuth is radioactive — including the uranium from our opening puzzle.

THE BAND OF STABILITY — AND WHICH WAY UNSTABLE NUCLEI DECAY
Above the band (too many neutrons)
Tends to undergo β− (beta-minus) decay — details in Lesson 9.02.
Inside the band (balanced N/Z)
Stable — the nucleus holds together indefinitely.
Below the band (too few neutrons)
Tends toward positron emission or electron capture.
Very heavy nuclei (Z > 83) typically undergo alpha decay to shed mass and charge.

There is one more layer to the pattern, and it rhymes with the electron shells you studied in Module 2. Just as filled electron shells make noble gases unreactive, certain “magic numbers” of protons or neutrons — 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126 — give a nucleus extra stability. And nuclei with even numbers of both protons and neutrons (“even-even”) tend to be more stable than odd ones. You won't memorize the magic numbers here; just notice that stability follows orderly rules, not chance.

⭐ NOW · CHASING THE EDGE OF STABILITY
If nothing is stable past bismuth, how far can the periodic table go? Scientists are currently exploring this very edge, synthesizing fleeting superheavy elements a few atoms at a time in particle accelerators. Some researchers suspect there may be an “island of stability” near certain magic numbers, where superheavy nuclei might survive a little longer than their neighbors. The questions about what holds matter together are not finished — they are being asked right now.

🔗 CONNECTING BACK · FINALLY INSIDE THE NUCLEUS
In Module 2 you built the atom from protons, neutrons, and electrons, and then spent the whole year on the electrons — because that is where bonding, reactions, acids and bases, and electrochemistry all happen. The nucleus, you were told, simply rides along unchanged. This module overturns that simplification: the nucleus can change too. The protons, neutrons, and isotopes you counted in Module 2 are the very foundation of nuclear chemistry — the difference is that now we ask what makes a nucleus itself stable or unstable, a question Module 2 deliberately left alone. That shift is the nature of science in action: a model that served us well all year is now revised, not discarded, because new evidence demands it — the same durable-yet-revisable pattern you saw when the plum-pudding atom gave way to Rutherford's nucleus. Ask yourself: what kind of evidence would it take to make you revise a model you trust? And this is no mere abstraction — learning to read the nucleus is exactly what makes modern nuclear medicine possible, as you'll see across this module.
▶  Think first, then check: Why does a heavy nucleus like uranium need so many more neutrons than protons to (barely) hold together?
Because the strong nuclear force acts only over a very short range, while electrical repulsion between protons reaches across the whole nucleus. In a large nucleus the protons repel one another more and more, but each added neutron contributes strong-force attraction with no extra repulsion. So heavy nuclei pile on extra neutrons (N/Z rising toward ~1.5) to supply enough binding glue. Even so, past Z = 83 the balance can no longer be maintained, and the nucleus is radioactive.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A nucleus is a crowd of nucleons — protons (Z) and neutrons — held together by the short-range strong nuclear force against fierce proton repulsion. A tiny mass defect becomes the binding energy (E = mc2) that locks the nucleus in place, and binding energy per nucleon peaks near iron-56 — the reason both fusion and fission can release energy. Whether a nuclide lasts forever or decays depends on its neutron-to-proton balance: stable nuclides cluster in the band of stability (N/Z rising for heavier elements), and nothing past Z = 83 is stable.
