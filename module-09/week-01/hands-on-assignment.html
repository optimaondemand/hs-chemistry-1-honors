📖 WHAT THIS BUILDS ON
From this week's lessons: nuclide notation and the band of stability (9.01), and the conservation of mass number (A) and atomic number (Z) across the decay types — alpha, beta-minus, positron emission, and gamma (9.02).
This is your week's “doing chemistry” work. Two parts: a set of nuclear equations you balance, and a few short reasoning items on decay type, the band of stability, and penetrating power.
📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
A nuclear equation balances on just two running totals: the mass number A and the atomic number Z. In Part A you'll fill the missing particle or daughter nuclide in six equations, using those two rules. In Part B you'll reason in words: classify a decay from how A and Z changed, predict a decay from the band of stability, and rank radiation. Work each item before you open the matching ▶ Check your thinking box — the honest attempt is where the learning happens.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
When a nucleus decays, how do I use the conservation of A and Z to find exactly what it becomes — and what it gives off?

⚠️ AI AS AN INSTRUMENT · VERIFY THE BALANCE YOURSELF
An AI tool can produce a daughter nuclide that looks right but quietly breaks a conservation rule — off by one on Z, or naming the wrong element for that Z. Do the A and Z arithmetic by hand, then confirm the element symbol against a periodic table. Conservation of A and Z is the primary evidence; the AI is just a second opinion you check, never the authority.
🧪 PART A · BALANCE THE EQUATION (6 problems)

Fill the blank (?) so that the total mass numbers (A) match and the total atomic numbers (Z) match. Use a periodic table to name the element from its Z.

A1.   226 88Ra → ? + 42He   (alpha decay)
A2.   3215P → 3216S + ?   (beta-minus decay)
A3.   2211Na → ? +  0+1e   (positron emission)
A4.   6027Co* → 6027Co + ?   (gamma emission; the * marks an energized nucleus)
A5.   ? → 222 86Rn + 42He   (find the parent of this alpha decay)
A6.   234 90Th → 234 91Pa + ?   (which particle, and what decay type is this?)
▶  Check your thinking — Part A answer key
A1. Alpha removes A = 4, Z = 2. Daughter A = 226 − 4 = 222; Z = 88 − 2 = 86 → 222 86Rn (radon). Check: 226 = 222 + 4; 88 = 86 + 2.

A2. A unchanged (32 = 32), and Z rose 15 → 16, so a beta particle was emitted:  0−1e. Check: 32 = 32 + 0; 15 = 16 + (−1).

A3. Positron emission: A unchanged, Z − 1. Daughter A = 22; Z = 11 − 1 = 10 → 2210Ne (neon). Check: 22 = 22 + 0; 11 = 10 + 1.

A4. Same nuclide before and after, so the emission carries no A and no Z: a gamma ray, 00γ. Check: 60 = 60 + 0; 27 = 27 + 0.

A5. Work backward: parent A = 222 + 4 = 226; parent Z = 86 + 2 = 88 → 226 88Ra (radium). Check: 226 = 222 + 4; 88 = 86 + 2.

A6. A unchanged (234 = 234), Z rose 90 → 91, so a beta particle,  0−1e, was emitted — this is beta-minus decay. Check: 234 = 234 + 0; 90 = 91 + (−1).
🔢 PART B · REASON IT OUT (explain in words)

Short answers — one or two sentences each.

B1. (Classify from the change.) A nucleus decays and its daughter has the same mass number A but an atomic number Z that is one higher. Which decay type was it, and what happened inside the nucleus at the particle level?
B2. (Band-of-stability prediction.) A certain nuclide sits above the band of stability — it has too many neutrons relative to its protons. Which decay type would you predict, and why does that move it toward the band?
B3. (Rank the radiation.) Rank alpha, beta, and gamma radiation from least to most penetrating, and name a material that stops each. In one sentence, why can an alpha source be relatively harmless outside the body but dangerous if inhaled?
▶  Check your thinking — Part B worked answers
B1. Beta-minus (β−) decay. A neutron converted into a proton, ejecting a fast electron ( 0−1e). The nucleon count is unchanged (so A is the same), but there is now one more proton (so Z rises by 1).

B2. Beta-minus (β−) decay. Having too many neutrons, the nucleus turns one neutron into a proton; this lowers the neutron count and raises the proton count, improving the neutron-to-proton balance and shifting the nuclide down toward the band of stability.

B3. Least to most penetrating: alpha < beta < gamma. Alpha is stopped by paper, skin, or clothing; beta by a few millimeters of aluminum or plastic; gamma needs thick lead or concrete. An alpha source is relatively harmless outside because skin or clothing stops it, but if inhaled or swallowed it deposits its strong ionizing damage directly inside tissue.
🧠 STRETCH YOUR REASONING · SELF-CHECK (not scored)

This one connects both lessons. Write two or three sentences before you open the model answer — this item isn't graded, but the thinking is exactly what the exam will ask for.

C1. A nuclide sits above the band of stability (too many neutrons). Explain, in your own words, why beta-minus decay is the natural “fix” for it — using both the band of stability (9.01) and the two conservation rules (9.02). Why does this decay leave A unchanged while raising Z by 1?
▶  Check your thinking — model answer
A nucleus above the band has too many neutrons relative to its protons, so to move toward the band it needs to trade a neutron for a proton. Beta-minus decay does exactly that: a neutron converts into a proton and ejects a fast electron ( 0−1e). By the conservation rules, one nucleon simply changed identity — a neutron became a proton — so the total nucleon count (A) is unchanged, while the proton count (Z) rises by 1 and the atom becomes the next element up. Lowering the neutron count and raising the proton count improves the neutron-to-proton ratio, shifting the nuclide down toward the band of stability. Both ideas agree: the band tells us which way the nucleus must move, and the conservation rules tell us exactly what it emits to get there.
