📚 SECTION A · MODULE 5 — GAS LAWS & THERMOCHEMISTRY

Key ideas to carry in

Module 5 put matter in motion and learned to measure it. The simple gas laws combine into the ideal gas law, PV = nRT (temperature always in kelvin), which connects pressure, volume, amount, and temperature in one relationship — and notice the n: the mole from Module 4 is right at the center. The kinetic-molecular theory explains why the laws hold: gas is mostly empty space, molecules move randomly, and average kinetic energy depends only on temperature. The second half turned to energy: heat flow, calorimetry, and enthalpy.

The gas laws
Boyle (P·V constant), Charles (V/T), Gay-Lussac (P/T), and PV = nRT (R = 0.08206). At STP one mole fills 22.4 L. Dalton: total pressure = sum of partial pressures.
KMT & real gases
Same temperature → same average KE, so lighter molecules move faster (Graham's law of effusion). Real gases deviate most at high pressure and low temperature, where size and attractions matter.
Energy & enthalpy
q = mcΔT (c_water = 4.184). Energy is conserved: q_metal = −q_water. Estimate ΔH from bond energies: bonds broken − bonds formed. Negative ΔH = exothermic.

Worked refresher: the ideal gas law & an enthalpy estimate

How many moles of gas occupy 2.0 L at 1.0 atm and 273 K?

Rearrange PV = nRT to n = PV ÷ RT:
n  =  (1.0)(2.0) ÷ [(0.08206)(273)]  =  2.0 ÷ 22.4  =  0.0893 mol
2.0 L is about one-eleventh of the 22.4 L a full mole fills at STP — and the mole is the same counting unit from Module 4.
Bond-energy ΔH for H2 + Cl2 → 2 HCl: bonds broken = 436 + 243 = 679 kJ; bonds formed = 2 × 431 = 862 kJ; ΔH ≈ 679 − 862 = −183 kJ (exothermic). And keep these ready: Boyle 1.0 atm × 6.0 L → 2.0 L gives 3.0 atm; q to warm 50.0 g water 20→80 °C = 50.0 × 4.184 × 60.0 = 12,552 J; gas over water, 760 − 23.8 = 736.2 mmHg dry; Graham He vs O2 = √(32/4) = 2.83× faster.

📚 SECTION B · MODULE 6 — LIQUIDS, SOLIDS, SOLUTIONS & COLLOIDS

Key ideas to carry in

Module 6 explained the macroscopic properties of matter from the forces between particles. Intermolecular forces (much weaker than bonds) run, weakest to strongest, London dispersion < dipole-dipole < hydrogen bonding (which needs H bonded to N, O, or F). Stronger forces mean higher boiling points, higher surface tension, lower vapor pressure. A liquid boils when its vapor pressure equals the external pressure. Solids come in four types (molecular, ionic, metallic, and very-hard high-melting covalent network). When things dissolve, like dissolves like, and the number of dissolved particles — not their identity — sets the colligative properties.

Forces & properties
Highest boiling point among similar-mass molecules → strongest IMF (often hydrogen bonding). Water's H-bonding gives it high specific heat, high surface tension, and floating ice.
Dissolving & electrolytes
Ionic solids dissolve by ion-dipole hydration. Strong electrolytes fully ionize (NaCl), weak partly (acetic acid), non not at all (sugar). Solubility of solids usually rises with temperature; gases fall.
Colligative & colloids
ΔT_f = i·K_f·m; more particles (higher van 't Hoff i) means more freezing-point depression. Colloids scatter light (the Tyndall effect); true solutions don't.

Worked refresher: freezing-point depression

What is the freezing point of a 1.00 m NaCl solution? (K_f(water) = 1.86 °C/m; for NaCl, i = 2.)

Use ΔT_f = i · K_f · m:
ΔT_f  =  2 × 1.86 × 1.00  =  3.72 °C of depression
Freezing point = 0 − 3.72 = −3.72 °C. The two ions per NaCl double the effect — CaCl2 (i = 3) would lower it even more.
Quick recall: only-dispersion molecule → CH4; very hard, non-conducting, super-high-melting → covalent network (diamond, quartz); light beam visible through milk but not salt water → colloid / Tyndall.

📚 SECTION C · MODULE 7 — THERMODYNAMICS, EQUILIBRIUM & ACID-BASE

Key ideas to carry in

Module 7 asked the deepest question: will a change happen at all, and how far? Entropy (S) measures dispersal of matter and energy; the second law says the entropy of the universe rises in any spontaneous change. Gibbs free energy ΔG = ΔH − TΔS combines enthalpy and entropy into one verdict: ΔG < 0 spontaneous. When a reaction doesn't go to completion it reaches dynamic equilibrium, described by K (products over reactants); Le Châtelier's principle predicts how a stress shifts it. The acid-base half ties it together: Brønsted-Lowry donors and acceptors, pH = −log[H3O+], and titration.

Spontaneity
ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. −/+ spontaneous at all T; +/− never; −/− only low T; +/+ only high T. (Convert ΔS from J/K to kJ/K before subtracting.)
Equilibrium
K = [products]/[reactants] (each to its coefficient; omit solids/liquids). Large K favors products. Compress → shift to fewer gas moles. A catalyst changes rate, not K.
Acids & bases
pH = −log[H3O+]; pH + pOH = 14. Kw = 1.0×10−14. A buffer is a weak acid + its conjugate base. Titration: M_aV_a = M_bV_b.

Worked refresher: free energy & an equilibrium constant

For a reaction at 298 K with ΔH = −100 kJ and ΔS = +50 J/K, find ΔG.

First convert ΔS: 50 J/K = 0.050 kJ/K. Then ΔG = ΔH − TΔS:
TΔS = 298 × 0.050 = 14.9 kJ  →  ΔG = −100 − 14.9 = −114.9 kJ
ΔG is negative, so the reaction is spontaneous. Here ΔH and ΔS both favor it, so it runs at any temperature.
Have these ready: for H2+I2⇌2HI with [H2]=[I2]=0.20 M and [HI]=1.60 M, Kc = (1.60)² ÷ (0.20×0.20) = 64; [H3O+] = 1.0×10−3 → pH = 3.00 (acidic); titration 30.0 mL of 0.100 M NaOH neutralizing 25.0 mL HCl → M_HCl = (0.0300×0.100)÷0.0250 = 0.120 M. For the Haber process (exothermic, 4 mol gas → 2 mol), low T + high P maximizes NH3 yield.

📚 SECTION D · MODULE 8 — ELECTROCHEMISTRY, REDOX & KINETICS

Key ideas to carry in

Module 8 followed change that moves electrons and change that takes time. Redox — first met as a reaction type in Module 4 — is electron transfer: OIL RIG (Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain). A galvanic cell turns a spontaneous redox reaction into electricity: oxidation at the anode (−), reduction at the cathode (+), with E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode. Positive E°cell, negative ΔG°, and K > 1 all travel together (ΔG° = −nFE°cell) — the same energy story from M5 and M7, now read off a voltmeter. Kinetics asks how fast: rate laws, collision theory, and catalysts that lower activation energy.

Redox & cells
Reduction = gain of e− (oxidation number drops). Anode = oxidation = negative terminal. Daniell cell E°cell = +1.10 V. The oxidizing agent is reduced; the reducing agent is oxidized.
Driven cells & corrosion
Electrolysis drives a non-spontaneous reaction (E°cell < 0). Faraday: Q = I·t, mol e− = Q/F (F = 96,485). Galvanizing = a sacrificial anode: the more-active metal corrodes first.
Kinetics
rate = k[A]m[B]n; orders are found experimentally (double [A], rate ×4 → second order). A catalyst lowers Ea via an alternate path — it isn't consumed and doesn't change ΔG or K.

Worked refresher: cell potential → free energy

For the Daniell cell, E°(Cu²+/Cu) = +0.34 V (cathode) and E°(Zn²+/Zn) = −0.76 V (anode). Find E°cell, then ΔG° (n = 2, F = 96,485).

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode = (+0.34) − (−0.76) = +1.10 V
ΔG° = −nFE°cell = −(2)(96,485)(1.10) = −212,267 J ≈ −212 kJ
Positive E°cell → negative ΔG° → spontaneous (galvanic). The three always agree.
Also keep cold: S in SO42− = +6; 2.00 A for 3600 s plating Cu²+ (needs 2 e−) → Q = 7200 C, mol e− = 0.0746, mol Cu = 0.0373, mass ≈ 2.37 g; doubling [A] and seeing rate ×4 → second order in A.

📚 SECTION E · MODULE 9 — NUCLEAR CHEMISTRY & ITS APPLICATIONS

Key ideas to carry in

Module 9 went where chemistry rarely does — inside the nucleus, where changes are millions of times more energetic than the electron-shuffling of ordinary reactions. Nucleons (protons + neutrons) are held by the short-range strong force; beyond Z = 83 no nucleus is stable. Unstable nuclei decay (alpha, beta, gamma), and in a nuclear equation both mass number (A) and atomic number (Z) are conserved. Decay is first-order, so each nuclide has a fixed half-life. Fission and fusion release enormous energy because a tiny mass becomes energy via E = mc². Radioisotopes serve medicine, dating, and power — benefits that must be weighed honestly against risks.

Structure & decay
Neutrons = A − Z. Alpha (42He) drops A by 4, Z by 2; beta− turns a neutron into a proton (Z + 1); gamma changes neither. Penetration: alpha < beta < gamma.
Half-life
Fraction remaining = (1/2)n after n half-lives. Half-life is constant (independent of mass, temperature, chemical form) — which is what makes carbon-14 dating work (t½ ≈ 5730 yr).
Energy & use
Both fission and fusion release energy via E = mc² (binding-energy peak near iron-56). Alpha emitters are most dangerous inside the body — type and location both matter.

Worked refresher: carbon-14 dating

A wooden artifact has 1/4 of the carbon-14 a living tree of the same size would have. With t½ ≈ 5730 yr, how old is it?

Find how many half-lives give 1/4 using (1/2)n:
1/4 = (1/2)²  →  n = 2 half-lives
age = 2 × 5730 = 11,460 years
A living thing keeps a steady 14C level; after death it decays, and the fraction left reads the age.
Quick recall: neutrons in 23592U = 235 − 92 = 143; 23892U → 23490Th + 42He (alpha); most penetrating radiation = gamma; both fission and fusion release energy via E = mc².

💡 HOW TO STUDY THIS · A NATURE-OF-SCIENCE NOTE
Study Semester 2 as one question asked five ways: what drives change, and how far and how fast does it go? Energy answers part of it (M5 thermochemistry → M7 free energy → M8 cell potential — one continuous account), forces answer another (M6 sets the properties matter shows), and equilibrium and kinetics answer the rest (how far, in M7; how fast, in M8). When a fact feels isolated, trace it back to that question. Two habits of mind run through the whole year and are worth carrying into the exam: first, a model is honest about its limits — the ideal gas law works where its assumptions hold and fails at high pressure and low temperature; equilibrium and rate are two different questions about the same reaction. Second, knowledge carries responsibility — the Haber process feeds billions, batteries and fusion shape our energy future, and nuclear technology demands an honest weighing of benefit against risk. That is chemistry “shaping our world,” the fourth Grand Question, in action. To prepare: re-read each worked example and redo it on paper without looking, run every self-check widget until the feedback is green, and revisit any module's reading checks where you stumble.
