Module 7 Writing Assignment
Equilibrium Under Pressure — A Le Châtelier CER
Word (.docx) · 100 pts · teacher-reviewed
📚 THE TASK
A reaction at equilibrium is not finished — it is balanced. Push on it, and it pushes back. Le Châtelier's principle says a system at equilibrium responds to a stress by shifting to partially counteract it. That single idea reaches far past the lab bench: it governs the chemistry of the ocean as it absorbs our carbon dioxide, and it decides how the world feeds itself through the synthesis of fertilizer.
In this assignment you will write a CER — a Claim, Evidence, and Reasoning argument — about a real equilibrium system under stress. Make a claim about how the equilibrium responds, defend it with the equilibrium chemistry and Le Châtelier's principle, reason carefully from cause to effect, and connect it to the stakes for people and the planet. Choose one of the two prompts below.
PROMPT A · OCEAN ACIDIFICATION
Rising atmospheric CO2 dissolves into seawater, feeding the equilibrium CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3−. Predict and justify how adding more CO2 shifts this system, what happens to ocean pH, and why that threatens shell-building organisms (corals, oysters, plankton).
PROMPT B · THE HABER-BOSCH PROCESS
Ammonia for fertilizer is made by N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g), ΔH = −92 kJ/mol. Predict and justify how to maximize the yield of NH3 using pressure and temperature, explain the real-world compromise industry makes, and connect it to the societal stakes of feeding the world.
You'll write it in Microsoft Word, organized into the CER sections listed further down.
⭐ WHY THIS MATTERS
This CER is one of only two teacher-reviewed pieces in Module 7 (the other is your Equilibrium & Acid-Base Station VR project). Your teacher reads it personally and responds to your reasoning — so make it your careful best, and justify every shift from the chemistry, not from a guess. This is exactly Quarter 4's question in action: how does chemistry shape our world?
🧮 YOUR TOOLKIT (USE THESE EXACT IDEAS & VALUES)
Le Châtelier's principle: a system at equilibrium shifts to partially counteract a stress. Add a reactant → shift right (toward products); add a product → shift left; remove a product → shift right.
Pressure: increasing pressure (decreasing volume) shifts toward the side with fewer moles of gas.
Temperature: treat heat as a reactant (endothermic) or a product (exothermic). For an exothermic reaction (heat is a product), lowering temperature shifts right.
pH: pH = −log[H+]; more H+ means a lower pH (more acidic).
Prompt A facts: adding CO2 drives CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3− to the right, raising [H+] and lowering ocean pH; the extra H+ also ties up carbonate (CO32−) that organisms need to build CaCO3 shells.
Prompt B facts: N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3 goes from 4 mol of gas to 2 mol of gas, so high pressure favors NH3; ΔH = −92 kJ/mol (exothermic), so low temperature favors NH3 — but low temperature is too slow, so industry compromises (~400–450 °C, ~150–300 atm, iron catalyst). Fritz Haber received the 1918 Nobel Prize in Chemistry; Carl Bosch scaled it to industry.
🧠 YOUR REASONING TOOLKIT (how to build the argument)
Use these steps in your own words — don't just copy the headings.
Step 1 — Name the equilibrium and the stress.
A: write the carbonate equilibrium and identify the stress (we keep adding CO2, a reactant). B: write the Haber equilibrium with its ΔH, and identify the levers you can change (pressure, temperature).
Step 2 — Apply Le Châtelier to predict the shift.
A: adding a reactant (CO2) shifts the system right — toward more H+. B: high pressure shifts toward the fewer-moles side (2 mol NH3), and because the reaction is exothermic, lower temperature shifts right too.
Step 3 — Trace it to the real outcome.
A: more H+ means a lower pH, and the extra H+ consumes the carbonate that shell-builders need — so reefs and shellfish suffer. B: name the temperature/rate compromise and why cheap, abundant ammonia changed the world's food supply.
📝 STRUCTURE: WRITE THESE CER SECTIONS
1
Claim. One or two clear sentences stating how the equilibrium responds and what it means (e.g., “Adding CO2 shifts the carbonate equilibrium right, lowering ocean pH and threatening shell-builders,” or “NH3 yield is maximized at high pressure and low temperature, though industry must compromise on temperature for speed.”).
2
Evidence (the equilibrium). Write the balanced equilibrium and lay out the relevant facts: which species are reactants and products, how many moles of gas are on each side (B), the sign of ΔH (B), and what stress is being applied. State the Le Châtelier rule(s) you will use.
3
Reasoning (Le Châtelier). This is the heart of the CER. Walk step by step from the stress to the predicted shift to the consequence, citing the correct rule each time: add a reactant → shift right; raise pressure → shift toward fewer gas moles; lower T (exothermic) → shift right. For A, connect the rightward shift to rising [H+] and a falling pH; for B, justify both the pressure and the temperature choice.
4
Societal cost–benefit (Responsibility). Do not just mention the stakes — weigh them. Name a genuine benefit and a genuine cost, then take a reasoned, evidence-based position on what we should do about it. For A: the benefit of the ocean absorbing our CO2 set against the cost to reefs, fisheries, and the people who depend on them. For B: how cheap ammonia underwrites the food supply of billions set against its energy use and environmental costs. State the trade-off explicitly and decide from the evidence, not from preference (Honesty & Responsibility threads).
5
Conclusion. Restate your claim in light of the evidence and reasoning, and name the big idea: a system at equilibrium answers a stress in a direction we can predict — and that prediction shapes the ocean, the food supply, and the world we live in.
✎ LENGTH & STYLE
Aim for roughly 500–700 words across the five CER sections. Write the balanced equilibrium clearly (typed or handwritten and photographed in). Use correct terminology — equilibrium, Le Châtelier's principle, shift, reactant/product, moles of gas, exothermic, pH, [H+]. The chemistry and the reasoning are what's graded — not polished prose. Justify each shift with a rule, not a hunch.
⚠️ A NOTE ON USING AI TOOLS
An AI tool will happily tell you which way an equilibrium shifts — and it can get it backwards: confidently shifting toward more gas moles under pressure, flipping the temperature rule for an exothermic reaction, or claiming a lower [H+] gives a lower pH. If you consult one, treat it as a draft to check, never an answer to paste: re-derive each shift yourself by applying Le Châtelier's principle to the balanced equilibrium, and re-reason the pH direction by hand. The instrument proposes; your reasoning against the equilibrium verifies. That is exactly the AI-literacy skill this assignment is built to grow.

📊 HOW THIS IS GRADED (TEACHER RUBRIC — build natively as a Canvas Rubric)
Criterion Full marks Developing Pts
Claim A clear, specific, scientifically defensible claim that directly answers the chosen prompt (the direction of the shift and what it means). Claim vague, missing, or only restates the question. 15
Equilibrium evidence Correct balanced equilibrium; relevant facts laid out (species, gas-mole counts and/or sign of ΔH, the stress applied) and the right Le Châtelier rule(s) named. Equation wrong/missing; facts incomplete or rules not identified. 25
Le Châtelier reasoning Steps logically from stress → predicted shift → consequence, citing the correct rule each time; pH direction (A) or both pressure & temperature choices (B) justified correctly. Shift asserted without a rule, reasoning gaps, or a reversed prediction (e.g., wrong pH or temperature direction). 30
Societal cost–benefit (Responsibility) Weighs a genuine benefit against a genuine cost (shell-builders & ocean health; or the food supply against its energy/environmental costs) and takes a reasoned, evidence-based position — the Responsibility thread in action. Only one side named, generic, or scientifically off; no trade-off actually weighed. 15
Conclusion & communication Conclusion ties back to the claim and names the big idea; correct terminology, clear organized CER form. Conclusion thin or just restates; terms misused; disorganized. 15
Total: 100 pts
The equilibrium evidence and the Le Châtelier reasoning carry the most weight — a correct prediction with no chemistry behind it earns little. Reason from the equilibrium up.
