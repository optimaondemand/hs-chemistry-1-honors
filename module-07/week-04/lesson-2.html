📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A bottle of lemon juice has a hydronium concentration of about 0.005 M; household ammonia has about 0.0000000000003 M. Comparing acids by numbers like these is awkward at best. Is there a simpler scale that turns these unwieldy figures into easy, everyday numbers?
Last lesson you learned that every aqueous solution has a hydronium concentration [H3O+], and that it is tied to [OH−] through Kw. Those concentrations are real and useful — but they span an enormous range and are clumsy to write and compare. Chemists tamed them with a clever compression called the pH scale, the same 0-to-14 scale you've seen on pool kits and soil tests. This lesson shows what pH actually is, how to convert between concentration and pH in both directions, and how pH and its partner pOH always add to a fixed total. You'll also use a calculator widget to check your conversions.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to calculate pH from [H3O+] and pOH from [OH−], and recover a concentration from a pH using [H3O+] = 10−pH.
2
I will be able to use pH + pOH = 14.00 at 25 °C to move between pH and pOH.
3
I will be able to classify a solution as acidic, basic, or neutral from its pH (neutral 7, acidic below 7, basic above 7 at 25 °C).

📚 PART 1 · WHAT pH ACTUALLY IS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
A strong acid has [H3O+] = 0.01 M and a neutral solution has [H3O+] = 0.0000001 M. If we want a scale where the acid gets a small whole number and the neutral one a middling whole number, what mathematical trick would tame numbers spread across so many powers of ten? (Just commit a guess.)

A logarithm answers the question "ten to what power gives this number?" Since hydronium concentrations are almost always small powers of ten (like 10−3 or 10−9), taking the log gives a negative number that is easy to read. Chemists flip the sign to make it positive and define pH as:

THE DEFINITION OF pH
pH = −log[H3O+]
a small pH means a large [H3O+] — the more acidic the solution

The minus sign means the scale runs "backwards" from what you might expect: lower pH means more acidic (more hydronium). And because each whole pH unit is one power of ten, a solution at pH 3 has ten times the hydronium of pH 4, and a hundred times that of pH 5. The pH scale is logarithmic — small steps in pH hide large jumps in concentration.

Worked example — concentration to pH. A solution has [H3O+] = 1.0 × 10−3 M. Then pH = −log(1.0 × 10−3) = −(−3.00) = 3.00. Because 3.00 is below 7, the solution is acidic — which makes sense, since 10−3 is far larger than the 10−7 of neutral water.

Acidic, basic, or neutral

In pure, neutral water at 25 °C, [H3O+] = 1.0 × 10−7 M, so pH = −log(10−7) = 7.00. That fixes the landmarks of the scale:

If the pH is… [H3O+] vs. [OH−] The solution is…
less than 7[H3O+] > [OH−]acidic
exactly 7[H3O+] = [OH−]neutral
greater than 7[H3O+] < [OH−]basic
💡 THEN · WHO INVENTED THE pH SCALE?
The scale you're learning was not handed down from the beginning of chemistry — a person invented it, and fairly recently. In 1909, the Danish chemist S. P. L. Sørensen, working at the Carlsberg Laboratory in Copenhagen, needed a practical way to describe how acidic a solution was. His research centered on how enzymes and proteins behave, and that behavior turned out to be exquisitely sensitive to the hydrogen-ion concentration — but those concentrations were tiny numbers spread across many powers of ten, clumsy to record and compare. Sørensen's fix was to report the negative logarithm of the hydrogen-ion concentration instead, compressing that enormous range into simple, readable numbers. He named the quantity “pH.” The idea caught on quickly because it made careful, quantitative chemistry so much easier — and more than a century later you still reach for the very same scale on a pool test strip or a garden soil kit. It is a good reminder that even the tools that feel timeless were once somebody's clever solution to a real, stubborn problem.
📚 PART 2 · pOH AND THE TWO DIRECTIONS

The hydroxide concentration gets the same treatment. pOH = −log[OH−]. And because [H3O+][OH−] = Kw = 1.0 × 10−14 at 25 °C, taking −log of both sides gives a wonderfully simple partnership:

pH AND pOH ALWAYS ADD UP
pH + pOH = 14.00  (at 25 °C)
know either one and you instantly know the other

Going backwards: pH to concentration

To undo a logarithm you raise 10 to the power. So from a pH you recover the hydronium concentration with [H3O+] = 10−pH. Here is the full round trip:

Worked example — pH to everything else. A solution has pH = 9.00.
• pOH = 14.00 − 9.00 = 5.00.
• [H3O+] = 10−9 = 1.0 × 10−9 M.
• [OH−] = 10−pOH = 10−5 = 1.0 × 10−5 M.
Since pH 9.00 is above 7, the solution is basic — and indeed [OH−] (10−5) is greater than [H3O+] (10−9), as it must be.

🤖 AI AS AN INSTRUMENT, NOT AN ORACLE
If you ask an AI chatbot to "find the pH," it will hand you a confident number — but AI tools sometimes drop the minus sign in pH = −log[H3O+] and report a negative pH, or mix up pH and pOH. A negative pH for ordinary lemon juice is a tell-tale error. Treat any AI answer as a draft to be checked against the rules you just learned: recompute by hand, confirm the sign, and make sure acidic solutions land below 7. The calculator above is a verification tool, not a substitute for understanding.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

pH is just a logarithm that compresses huge ranges of hydronium concentration into easy numbers: pH = −log[H3O+], with lower pH meaning more acidic. Its partner is pOH = −log[OH−], and the two always satisfy pH + pOH = 14.00 at 25 °C. To go backwards, raise 10 to the power: [H3O+] = 10−pH. Neutral is 7, acidic is below 7, basic is above 7. Carry this fluency forward — next you'll ask why different acids reach such different pH values.
