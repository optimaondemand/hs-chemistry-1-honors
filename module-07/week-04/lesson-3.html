📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Stomach acid (hydrochloric acid) can dissolve metal, yet vinegar — also an acid — is mild enough to put on a salad. Both donate protons to water. So what makes one acid fierce and another gentle, even at the same concentration?
You now know what an acid is — a proton donor — and how to measure the acidity of a solution with pH. The missing piece is how completely a given acid actually donates its protons. Some let go of nearly every proton the instant they hit water; others release only a tiny fraction and hold the rest back. That single difference — complete versus partial donation — separates strong acids and bases from weak ones, and it is captured by an equilibrium constant. This lesson sorts the common acids and bases, introduces Ka and Kb as strength meters, and uncovers a neat seesaw rule: the stronger an acid, the weaker the base it leaves behind.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…

2

3

📚 PART 1 · STRONG MEANS COMPLETE, WEAK MEANS PARTIAL
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
Two beakers each hold 1.0 M acid — one hydrochloric acid (HCl), one acetic acid (vinegar). The HCl solution has a far lower pH. Since both are 1.0 M, what must differ between them to explain the gap? (Just commit a guess.)

A strong acid ionizes essentially completely in water: nearly every molecule donates its proton. We write the reaction with a single forward arrow because so little un-ionized acid remains — HCl → H+ + Cl−. There are only a handful of common strong acids worth memorizing:

The common strong acids:
HCl  ·  HBr  ·  HI  ·  HNO3  ·  H2SO4  ·  HClO4

The strong bases are likewise a short list: the group-1 (alkali metal) hydroxides such as NaOH and KOH, together with the heavier group-2 hydroxides Ca(OH)2, Sr(OH)2, and Ba(OH)2. These dissociate completely to release hydroxide ions.

A weak acid or weak base ionizes only partially. Most of the molecules stay whole; only a small fraction transfer a proton at any moment. Because both the un-ionized and ionized forms coexist, we write a double arrow — it is a true equilibrium. Anything not on the strong lists above is weak: acetic acid (vinegar), carbonic acid, ammonia, and the vast majority of acids and bases you'll ever meet.

THE KEY DISTINCTION
Strong = ionizes ~100%  ·  Weak = ionizes only partially
“strong” describes how completely it ionizes — not how concentrated it is
📚 PART 2 · MEASURING WEAK-ACID STRENGTH WITH Ka

For a weak acid, "partial" is vague — we want a number. Because the partial ionization is an equilibrium, it has an equilibrium constant, given a special name: the acid ionization constant, Ka. For a generic weak acid HA dissolving in water:

HA  +  H2O  ⇌  H3O+  +  A−      Ka = [H3O+][A−] / [HA]

Read Ka as you read any K: a larger Ka means the equilibrium lies further toward products — more protons donated, a stronger acid. A tiny Ka means the acid barely ionizes. Acetic acid, the acid in vinegar, has Ka ≈ 1.8 × 10−5 — a small number, confirming it is weak. (Bases get the parallel constant Kb, the base ionization constant; larger Kb means a stronger base.)

Those exponents are awkward, so — just like pH — chemists take a negative log: pKa = −log Ka. The sign flips the comparison: a smaller pKa means a stronger acid. Acetic acid's pKa is about 4.7; an acid with pKa = 2 is stronger still.

Two scales, one idea. Larger Ka → stronger acid. Smaller pKa → stronger acid. They always agree — pKa is just Ka written on a friendlier scale, the same trick that turned [H3O+] into pH.
Connecting back to the models. Ka and Kb don't replace the definitions from Lesson 7.07 — they live inside the Brønsted-Lowry model. Every Ka measures exactly how eagerly a proton donor lets go of its H+; every Kb measures how eagerly a proton acceptor takes one. The same proton-transfer idea that broadened Arrhenius's definition is now something chemists can put a precise number on — a model made not just wider, but measurable.

The strong acid. At equal concentration it releases far more H3O+, so its pH is lower. Strength, not concentration, drives the gap.
📚 PART 3 · THE CONJUGATE SEESAW

Here is the elegant payoff. Recall from Lesson 7.07 that when an acid donates a proton, it becomes its conjugate base. If an acid donates protons eagerly (it is strong), then its conjugate base has little tendency to take a proton back — it is weak. Conversely, a weak acid that clings to its proton leaves behind a conjugate base that grabs protons readily. The two strengths move like opposite ends of a seesaw:

THE SEESAW RULE
The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base
…and the weaker the acid, the stronger its conjugate base

An example makes it concrete. HCl is a strong acid; its conjugate base, Cl−, is so weak that it does essentially nothing to water (which is why chloride is just a spectator ion). Acetic acid is a weak acid; its conjugate base, the acetate ion, is a meaningfully strong-ish base that can pull protons from water — a fact you'll use next week when salts of weak acids make solutions turn basic.

🌍 THEN → NOW → FUTURE · ACIDS, BASES & THE OCEAN
Then. In the late 1800s Svante Arrhenius first tied acidity to ions in water — and, remarkably, the same chemist also reasoned early on that burning fossil fuels would load the atmosphere with carbon dioxide.

Now. Some of that atmospheric CO2 dissolves into seawater and forms carbonic acid, a weak acid of exactly the kind you met today. Oceanographers report that the average pH of surface seawater has fallen measurably since the industrial era — a shift usually called ocean acidification. Because pH is logarithmic, even a seemingly small drop reflects a sizeable rise in [H3O+].

Future. Scientists are still working to pin down how far and how fast this will go, and how shell- and reef-building organisms (corals, oysters, plankton) will fare as the carbonate chemistry of the ocean shifts. It remains an open, active area of research — and the strong-vs-weak and pH tools from this week are the very ones researchers use to study it.

▶  Think first, then check: HCl is a strong acid and HF is a weak acid. Which has the stronger conjugate base, Cl− or F−, and why?
F− is the stronger conjugate base. By the seesaw rule, the weaker acid (HF) leaves behind the stronger conjugate base. HCl is so strong that its conjugate base Cl− is essentially inert in water, while F− — from the weaker HF — retains some real tendency to accept a proton. Stronger acid → weaker conjugate base.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Acid and base strength is about how completely a substance ionizes, not how much is dissolved. A strong acid or base ionizes essentially completely (the short memorized lists: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO3, H2SO4, HClO4; NaOH, KOH, and the Ca/Sr/Ba hydroxides); everything else is weak and ionizes only partially, measured by Ka (larger = stronger) or pKa (smaller = stronger). And the seesaw rule ties it together: the stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base. Next module-week, this rule explains why dissolving certain salts makes water acidic or basic.
