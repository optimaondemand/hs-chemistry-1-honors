📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Squeeze lemon juice onto a small cut and it stings; bite into it and your mouth puckers. Stir a spoon of baking soda into water and the slick, bitter liquid feels nothing like the lemon. What, at the level of atoms, makes one substance an “acid” and another a “base” — and is there a single idea that captures both?
People have sorted substances into "acids" and "bases" for centuries by taste, feel, and how they react — sour and corrosive on one side, bitter and slippery on the other. But a sorting rule is not an explanation. This lesson moves from describing acids and bases to defining them by what their particles actually do. You'll meet three definitions that grew broader over time, settle on the one chemists use most — acids and bases as a transfer of protons — and learn to spot the matched pairs that transfer leaves behind. By the end you'll be able to look at a reaction and say which species gave a proton, which took it, and what that makes them.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define an acid and a base by the Arrhenius and Brønsted-Lowry models, and explain why the proton-transfer model is broader.
2
I will be able to identify the conjugate acid-base pairs in a reaction, recognizing that the members of a pair differ by exactly one H+.
3
I will be able to explain how water is amphoteric and write its autoionization, including the value of Kw at 25 °C.

📚 PART 1 · FROM TASTE TESTS TO A DEFINITION
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
Lemon juice stings a cut and tastes sour; it is an acid. Something specific in the juice is responsible. What do you think an acid actually releases or does in water that gives it these properties? (No wrong answer — just commit a guess.)

The first modern definition came from the Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius. In his picture, an acid is a substance that produces hydrogen ions (H+) when dissolved in water, and a base is a substance that produces hydroxide ions (OH−) in water. Hydrochloric acid fits perfectly: HCl → H+ + Cl−. So does sodium hydroxide: NaOH → Na+ + OH−. The Arrhenius model is clean, and for many everyday acids and bases it is all you need.

But it has a blind spot. Ammonia, NH3, clearly behaves as a base — its water solution is slippery and turns litmus blue — yet ammonia contains no hydroxide to hand out. The Arrhenius rule can't explain it, and it ties the whole idea of acids and bases to one solvent, water. A better definition would explain ammonia and free acids and bases from depending on water at all.

📚 PART 2 · ACIDS AND BASES AS PROTON TRANSFER

A hydrogen ion, H+, is just a bare proton — a hydrogen atom that has lost its only electron. The broader definition centers everything on that single proton. In the Brønsted-Lowry model, an acid is a proton (H+) donor and a base is a proton acceptor. Acids and bases are no longer two separate kinds of substance: they are two roles in a single hand-off, the giving and taking of a proton.

THE CENTRAL IDEA
Acid = proton donor  ·  Base = proton acceptor
an acid-base reaction is the transfer of one H+ from the acid to the base

Watch this resolve the ammonia puzzle. When ammonia meets water: NH3 + H2O ⇌ NH4+ + OH−. Ammonia accepts a proton from water (so NH3 is the base), and water donates the proton (so water is the acid here). No hydroxide needed to start — the model explains the very thing Arrhenius could not, because it looks at the proton hand-off rather than at what dissolves to make OH−.

💡 THEN · A DEFINITION THAT GREW
The idea of an acid widened in three steps. In the 1880s, Svante Arrhenius tied acids and bases to ions formed in water (H+ and OH−). Then, in 1923, two chemists working independently — Johannes Brønsted in Denmark and Thomas Lowry in England — published the same broader idea in the same year: acids and bases are proton donors and acceptors. That same year, the American chemist Gilbert N. Lewis proposed a definition broader still, in terms of electron pairs. Each model didn't discard the one before — it contained it and reached further. This is the nature of science in miniature: a useful idea is refined, not thrown away, as it is asked to explain more.

One question, three answers that grew

All three definitions are answers to a single scientific question — “what makes something an acid or a base?” — asked by chemists who kept running into cases the older answer could not handle. Each later model keeps everything the model before it explained and stretches to cover more. Read the table across: notice what each answer does well, and what piece of evidence forced chemists to reach for the next one.

Model An acid / a base is… What it explains well (its strength) Where it breaks down (what forced the next model)
Arrhenius
1880s		 Acid produces H+ in water; base produces OH− in water. Simple and quantitative for common water-based acids and bases (HCl, NaOH). Can't explain a base like ammonia (no OH− to give), and works only in water.
Brønsted-Lowry
1923		 Acid donates a proton (H+); base accepts one. Explains ammonia, works in any solvent, and defines conjugate pairs. This course's working model. Still needs a proton to change hands — it can't classify acid-base reactions in which no H+ moves at all.
Lewis
1923		 Acid accepts an electron pair; base donates one. The broadest: covers every proton-transfer reaction and reactions with no proton involved. So broad it is more than you usually need — harder to apply for quick, everyday judgments.

Notice the pattern down the last column: each model is not wrong, just incomplete for some new case — a base with no hydroxide, a reaction in a non-water solvent, a reaction with no proton at all. That is exactly how a scientific idea grows: it is broadened to fit new evidence, not thrown out.

🔮  Nature of science — think first: The definition of an acid was broadened twice without being thrown away. Does that mean Arrhenius was “wrong”? What does this pattern tell you about how scientific knowledge changes?
Arrhenius was not wrong — his definition still correctly describes HCl and NaOH in water, and chemists still use it every day. It was incomplete: new evidence (bases like ammonia, reactions in other solvents, reactions with no proton) showed the idea needed to reach further. Scientific knowledge is durable yet revisable — it holds up for the cases it was built on, and it is broadened, not discarded, when honest evidence demands more. A good model earns trust precisely because it can grow.

Conjugate acid-base pairs

When an acid donates its proton, what remains is a species that could take a proton back — so it is itself a base. The acid and what it becomes are a conjugate acid-base pair: two species that differ by exactly one H+. Look at HCl dissolving in water:

HCl  +  H2O  →  H3O+  +  Cl−
acid       base     conj. acid   conj. base

HCl gives up a proton to become Cl−, so HCl and Cl− are one conjugate pair (they differ by one H+). Water accepts the proton to become H3O+, so H2O and H3O+ are the other conjugate pair. Every Brønsted-Lowry reaction has exactly two such pairs — one for the proton donated, one for the proton accepted. The trick to spotting them: find the two species that look identical except for a single hydrogen and a single unit of charge.

⚡ FLIP CARDS · IDENTIFY THE CONJUGATE PARTNER
For each species, predict its conjugate partner (add or remove one H+), then tap to flip and check.
🧊 The acid HNO3 — tap to reveal its conjugate base
NO3−. Remove one H+ from HNO3 and the charge drops by one. HNO3 / NO3− are a conjugate acid-base pair.
💧 The base NH3 — tap to reveal its conjugate acid
NH4+. A base accepts a proton, so add one H+: NH3 becomes NH4+. NH4+ / NH3 are a conjugate pair.
⚛️ The base OH− — tap to reveal its conjugate acid
H2O. Add one H+ to hydroxide and it becomes water. H2O / OH− are a conjugate pair — the same water that can also be an acid.
🍲 The acid H3O+ — tap to reveal its conjugate base
H2O. Remove one H+ from the hydronium ion and you are back to water. H3O+ / H2O are a conjugate pair.
📚 PART 3 · WATER PLAYS BOTH ROLES

You may have noticed something strange. With HCl, water accepted a proton and acted as a base. With ammonia, water donated a proton and acted as an acid. Water is amphoteric (also called amphiprotic): it can be either a proton donor or a proton acceptor, depending on its partner. That flexibility is one reason water is so central to chemistry.

Water is so willing to play both roles that it even reacts with itself. In any sample of pure water, a small fraction of molecules pass a proton from one to another — a process called autoionization:

2 H2O  ⇌  H3O+  +  OH−

One water molecule donates a proton (acting as acid) and another accepts it (acting as base), producing one hydronium ion (H3O+) and one hydroxide ion (OH−). Because this is an equilibrium, it has an equilibrium constant — given its own name, the ion-product constant for water, Kw:

THE ION-PRODUCT OF WATER
Kw = [H3O+][OH−] = 1.0 × 10−14  (at 25 °C)
in pure, neutral water [H3O+] = [OH−] = 1.0 × 10−7 M

This single relationship is quietly powerful: in any water solution at 25 °C, the product of the hydronium and hydroxide concentrations is always 1.0 × 10−14. Push one up and the other must come down. Knowing one concentration lets you find the other — the foundation of the pH calculations you'll do in the next lesson.

🔗 ONE STEP FURTHER · THE LEWIS DEFINITION
In the same year as Brønsted and Lowry, Gilbert N. Lewis reached even further by shifting attention from the proton to the electron pair it attaches to. A Lewis acid is an electron-pair acceptor, and a Lewis base is an electron-pair donor. Every Brønsted-Lowry base donating a lone pair to grab a proton is also a Lewis base — but the Lewis model also captures reactions with no proton at all. For this course, the Brønsted-Lowry proton-transfer view is your main working tool; just know the Lewis idea is the widest of the three.
⚡ TRY IT · PROTON DONORS, ACCEPTORS & PAIRS
Decide whether each statement is true or false. You'll get instant feedback on each.
1. A Brønsted-Lowry acid is a proton donor.
2. The two members of a conjugate acid-base pair differ by two hydrogen ions.
3. Water can act as either a proton donor or a proton acceptor (it is amphoteric).
4. When HCl donates its proton, its conjugate base is H3O+.
▶  Think first, then check: In the reaction HF + H2O ⇌ H3O+ + F−, name both conjugate acid-base pairs and say which species is the acid.
HF donates a proton, so HF is the acid and its conjugate base is F− (the HF / F− pair). Water accepts the proton, acting as the base, and its conjugate acid is H3O+ (the H2O / H3O+ pair). Each pair differs by exactly one H+.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Acids and bases are best understood not as two kinds of substance but as two roles in a single hand-off: a Brønsted-Lowry acid donates a proton, a base accepts one. What an acid becomes after donating, and what a base becomes after accepting, are its conjugate partner — differing by exactly one H+. Water can play either role (it is amphoteric) and even reacts with itself, fixing the product [H3O+][OH−] = Kw = 1.0 × 10−14 in every aqueous solution at 25 °C. Hold onto that constant: it is the bridge to the pH scale you'll build next.
