📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A chemical cold pack feels cold — it absorbs heat from your hand — yet it begins the instant you snap it, all on its own. An exothermic reaction releases heat and also tends to happen on its own. So if a process can be spontaneous whether it gives off heat or takes it in, what is the single quantity that actually decides?
In the last two lessons you met two competing drives: reactions tend to release energy (a favorable change in enthalpy, ΔH), and the universe tends toward greater disorder (a favorable change in entropy, ΔS). Sometimes these pull the same way; sometimes they fight. This lesson introduces the one bookkeeping quantity that combines them and gives a final verdict on spontaneity: the Gibbs free energy change, ΔG. With a single sign — positive or negative — ΔG tells you whether a reaction will go forward on its own, run backward, or sit balanced at equilibrium. By the end you'll be able to compute ΔG, read its sign, and predict how temperature can flip a reaction from "won't go" to "will go."
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to calculate the Gibbs free energy change using ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, converting units carefully so enthalpy and entropy match.
2
I will be able to use the sign of ΔG to decide whether a process is spontaneous (ΔG < 0), nonspontaneous (ΔG > 0), or at equilibrium (ΔG = 0).
3
I will be able to predict, from the signs of ΔH and ΔS, whether a reaction is spontaneous at all temperatures, no temperatures, only low temperatures, or only high temperatures.

📚 PART 1 · ONE NUMBER TO DECIDE SPONTANEITY
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
An instant cold pack gets noticeably colder when you snap it — it pulls heat out of its surroundings, the opposite of "releasing energy." Yet it starts dissolving the moment you break the inner pouch, with no help from you. If giving off heat isn't what makes it go, what else could be driving an endothermic process to happen on its own? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

In Lesson 7.02 you learned the real test of spontaneity: a process is spontaneous when the entropy of the whole universe increases (ΔSuniv > 0). That rule is always true, but it is awkward to use — it forces you to track the surroundings as well as the reaction. In 1876 the American physicist J. Willard Gibbs found a way to fold both halves of the universe into a single quantity that depends only on the system: the Gibbs free energy, G. Its change during a reaction is what we actually compute.

THE GIBBS FREE ENERGY EQUATION
ΔG = ΔH − TΔS
T is the absolute temperature in kelvin (K). ΔH is the enthalpy change; ΔS is the entropy change.

Read the equation as a competition. The ΔH term carries the pull toward lower energy (exothermic reactions, ΔH < 0, are favorable). The −TΔS term carries the pull toward greater disorder (an entropy increase, ΔS > 0, makes this term negative and therefore favorable). Temperature sets how loudly the entropy term speaks: the hotter it is, the more TΔS matters. The verdict comes from the sign of the result.

ΔG < 0  →  the process is spontaneous (releases free energy; called exergonic).
ΔG > 0  →  the process is nonspontaneous as written (the reverse direction is spontaneous).
ΔG = 0  →  the system is at equilibrium — no net change in either direction.
💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY
By the 1870s chemists were split on a basic question: what makes a reaction go? A popular view held that a reaction's driving force was simply how much heat it gave off — the more exothermic, the more “favorable.” But that idea ran into evidence it couldn't explain: some reactions absorb heat (like the cold pack) and still happen entirely on their own. Heat release alone clearly wasn't the whole story. Working quietly at Yale, Josiah Willard Gibbs settled the matter in a long, densely mathematical paper that reorganized chemical thermodynamics around the energy of a system. His move was to combine two competing drives — the pull toward lower energy (ΔH) and the pull toward greater disorder (ΔS) — into a single quantity whose sign, not heat alone, decides spontaneity. His work was so abstract that European scientists were slow to grasp it, but self-driven endothermic reactions are exactly what his free energy explained. The quantity you'll calculate today still bears his name.
▶  Think first, then check: A reaction has ΔG = +25 kJ at room temperature. Will it run forward on its own?
No. A positive ΔG means the forward reaction is nonspontaneous — it will not proceed on its own as written. What is spontaneous is the reverse reaction, which would have ΔG = −25 kJ. To force the forward direction, you'd have to supply energy from outside.
📚 PART 2 · THE FOUR-SIGN CHART — WHEN TEMPERATURE DECIDES

Because ΔG = ΔH − TΔS has temperature inside it, the sign of ΔG can change as you heat or cool a reaction. The four combinations of ΔH and ΔS signs give four distinct behaviors. This little chart is one of the most useful tools in chemistry — learn to reason it out rather than memorize it.

ΔH ΔS −TΔS term Spontaneous?
− (exo)+ (more disorder)negativeYes — at ALL temperatures
+ (endo)− (less disorder)positiveNever — at any temperature
− (exo)− (less disorder)positiveSpontaneous only at LOW T
+ (endo)+ (more disorder)negativeSpontaneous only at HIGH T

The logic is simpler than it looks. When ΔH and ΔS both favor the reaction (top row), nothing can stop it — ΔG is negative at every temperature. When both oppose it (second row), no temperature can save it. The interesting cases are the mixed rows, where one term helps and the other fights: there, temperature is the referee. For the two bottom rows, the temperature where the tug-of-war ties (ΔG = 0) is exactly T = ΔH / ΔS — above it, the entropy term wins; below it, the enthalpy term wins.

A familiar example of the bottom row: ice melting. Melting absorbs heat (ΔH > 0) and increases disorder (ΔS > 0). That's the "high-T" case — spontaneous only when it's warm enough. Above 0 °C the entropy term wins and ice melts on its own; below 0 °C the enthalpy term wins and water freezes instead. The crossover temperature is exactly the melting point.

📚 PART 3 · CALCULATING ΔG — MIND THE UNITS

Putting numbers into ΔG = ΔH − TΔS is straightforward arithmetic, but there is one trap that catches nearly everyone the first time: ΔH is usually given in kilojoules (kJ) while ΔS is given in joules per kelvin (J/K). You cannot subtract joules from kilojoules — the units must match. Convert ΔS to kJ/K (divide by 1000), or convert ΔH to J, before you subtract. Here is the worked example.

A worked calculation

A reaction has ΔH = −100 kJ, ΔS = +50 J/K, run at T = 298 K (about room temperature). Is it spontaneous?

Step 1 — fix the units. Convert ΔS to kJ/K so it matches ΔH:
  ΔS = 50 J/K × (1 kJ / 1000 J) = 0.050 kJ/K

Step 2 — compute the entropy term TΔS.
  TΔS = 298 K × 0.050 kJ/K = 14.9 kJ

Step 3 — subtract.
  ΔG = ΔH − TΔS = (−100 kJ) − (14.9 kJ) = −114.9 kJ

Verdict: ΔG is negative, so the reaction is spontaneous. (It makes sense: ΔH favors it and ΔS favors it — the top row of the chart, spontaneous at all T.)
⚠️ THE #1 MISTAKE
If you forget to convert and plug in ΔS = 50 (as if it were kJ/K), you'd get TΔS = 298 × 50 = 14,900, and ΔG = −100 − 14,900 = −15,000 — off by a factor of a thousand. Always check: does the entropy term come out as a sensible handful of kilojoules, not a giant number? Mismatched units are the most common error on free-energy problems.

✨ NOW · CURRENT RESEARCH: THE ENERGY LEDGER OF FUSION
Free-energy thinking — weighing what a process releases against what it costs — sits at the heart of one of today's biggest scientific pushes: fusion energy, the same reaction that powers the Sun. The dream is a power source that releases far more energy than it consumes. In recent years, fusion laboratories reported experiments in which a fuel target released more energy from the fusion reaction than the laser energy delivered to it — a milestone scientists call ignition. The harder, still-unsolved question is the whole-system efficiency: can the energy out exceed the energy needed to run the entire facility, not just the energy hitting the target? Researchers are currently working on more efficient lasers, magnetic confinement, and reactor designs to close that gap. It is a real-world version of exactly the bookkeeping you did today — tallying energy in versus energy out and asking whether the net result is favorable.

Highly endothermic with only a modest entropy gain — effectively the "never" case under ordinary conditions. Splitting water needs an outside energy push (electrolysis).
🔗 LOOKING AHEAD · ΔG CONNECTS TO EQUILIBRIUM
There is one more equation that ties free energy to the topic of your next lesson. Under standard conditions, ΔG° = −RT ln K, where K is the equilibrium constant. You don't need to compute with it yet — just notice the link it reveals: a strongly negative ΔG° means a large K (products strongly favored), and a positive ΔG° means a small K (reactants favored). Free energy and equilibrium are two views of the same thing, and the next lesson opens up the equilibrium side.
▶  Think first, then check: ΔH = +30 kJ, ΔS = +100 J/K, T = 298 K. Compute ΔG and state the verdict.
Convert ΔS: 100 J/K = 0.100 kJ/K. Entropy term: TΔS = 298 × 0.100 = 29.8 kJ. Then ΔG = (+30) − (29.8) = +0.2 kJ — barely positive, so just barely nonspontaneous at 298 K. This is the "high-T" case (ΔH>0, ΔS>0): warm it only slightly more and the entropy term tips ΔG negative. The crossover temperature is T = ΔH/ΔS = 30 kJ / 0.100 kJ/K = 300 K.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Spontaneity is decided by a single quantity, the Gibbs free energy change: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. A negative ΔG means the process goes on its own; a positive ΔG means it won't (the reverse will); ΔG = 0 marks equilibrium. Because temperature multiplies the entropy term, the four combinations of ΔH and ΔS signs give four behaviors — always, never, low-T-only, and high-T-only — so temperature can flip a reaction's fate. Watch your units: ΔH in kJ, ΔS in J/K, so convert before you subtract. Hold onto the closing link: a negative ΔG° points to a large equilibrium constant K — the bridge into the next lesson.
