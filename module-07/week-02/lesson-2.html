Chemical Equilibria & the Equilibrium Constant
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Seal a colorless gas in a tube and watch it slowly turn brown — then stop. The color holds steady and never changes again, even though you've added nothing and taken nothing away. The reaction looks finished. But is it really? Or is something still happening that you simply can't see?
Until now you've treated reactions as a one-way street: reactants go to products, full stop. But most reactions are reversible — products can turn back into reactants — and in a closed container they settle into a state where the forward and reverse reactions run at exactly the same speed. The amounts stop changing, yet the reaction never truly stops. This is chemical equilibrium, and chemists capture it in a single number, the equilibrium constant K, that says how far a reaction goes before it balances. This lesson builds K from a balanced equation, distinguishes the dynamic "looks-still-but-isn't" nature of equilibrium, and introduces a companion number, the reaction quotient Q, that predicts which way a reaction will move.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to describe dynamic equilibrium as the state where the forward and reverse reaction rates are equal and concentrations stay constant in a closed system.
2
I will be able to write the equilibrium-constant expression K from a balanced equation, omitting pure solids and liquids, and interpret a large or small K.
3
I will be able to compare the reaction quotient Q to K to predict whether a reaction will shift toward products or toward reactants.

📚 PART 1 · THE REACTION THAT STOPS CHANGING BUT NEVER STOPS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
In a sealed flask, a reaction's color deepens for a while and then holds perfectly steady — no further change for hours. Nothing is added or removed. What is the most likely explanation for why the color stops changing? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

Behind everything in this lesson is one driving question: how do chemical systems reach balance? The answer starts with an observation you can watch. Many reactions are reversible: as products build up, they begin reacting back into reactants. We write a reversible reaction with a double arrow (⇌) instead of a single one. Start with only reactants in a closed container, and at first the forward reaction is fast (lots of reactant) while the reverse is slow (no product yet). As reactant is consumed the forward rate falls; as product accumulates the reverse rate rises. Eventually the two rates meet. From that instant on, the concentrations of everything stop changing.

DYNAMIC EQUILIBRIUM
forward rate = reverse rate
concentrations stay constant — but the reaction is still running in both directions

The word dynamic matters. Equilibrium is not a reaction that has stopped. Molecules are still converting forward and backward every moment — it is just that they do so at equal rates, so the net amount of each substance holds steady. It is like a busy escalator station where exactly as many people step on as step off each minute: the crowd size never changes, but no one is standing still. Two conditions are required for this to hold: a closed system (nothing escaping or being added), and constant temperature.

Four signatures of chemical equilibrium:
✓  The forward and reverse rates are equal.
✓  Macroscopic concentrations (and color, pressure, etc.) are constant — steady and non-zero, with both reactants and products still present (not necessarily in equal amounts).
✓  The reaction has not stopped — it is dynamic, running both ways.
✓  It requires a closed system at constant temperature.
▶  Think first, then check: At equilibrium, are the concentrations of reactants and products necessarily equal to each other?
No — this is a common mix-up. At equilibrium the rates are equal, not the concentrations. The amounts of reactants and products simply stop changing; they can be very different from each other. A reaction that strongly favors products will sit at equilibrium with lots of product and little reactant. "Equal rates" is the definition; "equal concentrations" is not.
📚 PART 2 · THE EQUILIBRIUM CONSTANT K

Once a reaction reaches equilibrium, the ratio of products to reactants — each raised to the power of its coefficient — settles to a fixed value at a given temperature. That value is the equilibrium constant, K. For a general reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, the expression is products over reactants:

THE EQUILIBRIUM-CONSTANT EXPRESSION
K = [C]c[D]d ÷ ([A]a[B]b)
products on top, reactants on the bottom — each concentration raised to its coefficient

Two rules make these expressions reliable:

Rule 1 — products over reactants, each to its coefficient. The coefficient from the balanced equation becomes the exponent.
Rule 2 — omit pure solids and pure liquids. Their "concentration" doesn't change, so they are left out of K entirely. Only gases and dissolved (aqueous) species appear.

Kc versus Kp

For gas reactions you can write K two ways. Kc uses molar concentrations (mol/L, shown in square brackets). Kp uses partial pressures of the gases instead. They describe the same equilibrium from two measurement choices — concentrations or pressures — and either is valid as long as you're consistent. For reactions in solution, only Kc applies.

A worked expression

For H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2 HI(g):
  Kc = [HI]2 ÷ ([H2][I2])
The coefficient 2 on HI becomes the exponent on [HI]; H2 and I2 each have a coefficient of 1. At a particular temperature this equilibrium has Kc = 64 — a value well above 1, which tells you products (HI) are favored.

Now one with a solid: CaCO3(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO2(g).
Both CaCO3 and CaO are pure solids, so they drop out. Only the gas remains:
  Kc = [CO2]

What the size of K tells you

If K is… Then at equilibrium…
large (K » 1)Mostly products — the reaction proceeds far to the right.
small (K « 1)Mostly reactants — very little product forms.
near 1Significant amounts of both reactants and products.
🧠 THINK LIKE A SCIENTIST · NATURE OF SCIENCE
Notice the two different jobs being done here. The observation that a closed reaction settles to a steady, unchanging mixture — and that its product-to-reactant ratio always lands on the same value at a given temperature — is a kind of law: it describes a pattern nature reliably follows, without saying why. The equilibrium constant K belongs to a model: it explains and predicts, letting us calculate how far a reaction will go and — with Q — which way an unbalanced mixture will move. Laws tell us what happens; models tell us why and what next. Keeping the two straight is one of the clearest habits of scientific thinking, and equilibrium is a textbook case of the difference.

📚 PART 3 · THE REACTION QUOTIENT Q — PREDICTING THE DIRECTION

K describes a reaction at equilibrium. But what if your mixture isn't there yet — how do you know which way it will move? You compute the same products-over-reactants ratio, but with the current (not necessarily equilibrium) concentrations. That value is the reaction quotient, Q. It has the exact same form as K; the only difference is that Q uses whatever amounts you have right now. Then you compare Q to K.

Comparison Meaning Which way it shifts
Q < Ktoo few productsshifts forward (right) to make more product
Q > Ktoo much productshifts reverse (left) to remake reactant
Q = Kjust rightalready at equilibrium — no net shift

The intuition: a reaction always moves in the direction that brings Q closer to K. If Q is too small (not enough product), the reaction runs forward to build product up until Q rises to meet K. If Q is too large (too much product), it runs backward. When Q lands on K, the motion stops being net — you're at equilibrium.

A worked prediction

For H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2 HI(g), the equilibrium constant is Kc = 64. Suppose a flask currently holds [HI] = 1.0 M, [H2] = 1.0 M, and [I2] = 1.0 M.

Compute Q: Q = [HI]2 ÷ ([H2][I2]) = (1.0)2 ÷ (1.0 × 1.0) = 1.0
Compare: Q = 1.0 is much less than K = 64, so Q < K.
Predict: the reaction shifts forward (right) — it makes more HI until Q climbs to 64.
💡 THEN · AN IDEA THAT TOOK TIME TO ACCEPT
In the early 1800s many chemists pictured a reaction as a one-way trip: reactants simply became products and the process was over. But careful measurements kept turning up reactions that seemed to stall with both reactants and products still present — evidence the one-way picture couldn't explain. In the 1860s two Norwegian scientists, Cato Guldberg and Peter Waage, offered a different account: a reaction's rate depends on the “active mass” (concentration) of the substances present, so as products build up the reverse reaction speeds until the two rates balance. Their law of mass action is exactly the products-over-reactants logic behind K, and it reframed equilibrium not as a stopped reaction but as a balance of opposing rates. Like Gibbs's work in the previous lesson, their idea was first published in a form that took years to be widely understood. It became a bedrock principle only after it survived repeated testing against measured equilibrium mixtures — a good reminder that even a correct idea has to earn acceptance through evidence.
✨ NOW & NEXT · EQUILIBRIUM AT INDUSTRIAL SCALE
Now: the same K-and-Q reasoning is a working tool in industry. When engineers design a process to make ammonia for fertilizer (N2 + 3 H2 ⇌ 2 NH3) — the reaction that feeds much of the world — they tune temperature, pressure, and when to pull product out of the mix precisely to keep the system pushing forward, squeezing the highest possible yield from each pass. Reading K and Q is how they choose those settings. Next: researchers are currently exploring new catalysts and reactor designs that might reach useful yields at far lower temperature and pressure, which would cut the enormous energy cost of today's process. It isn't solved yet, but the goal is a greener chemistry built on exactly the equilibrium ideas you are learning now.
⚡ FLIP CARDS · WHICH WAY WILL IT SHIFT?
For a reaction with K = 10, predict the shift from each Q, then tap to flip and check.
🔹 Q = 2 (K = 10) — tap
Q < K → shifts forward (right). Too few products; the reaction makes more product until Q rises to 10.
🔸 Q = 50 (K = 10) — tap
Q > K → shifts reverse (left). Too much product; the reaction runs backward, remaking reactant until Q falls to 10.
⚫ Q = 10 (K = 10) — tap
Q = K → at equilibrium. No net shift — the mixture is already balanced.
🧩 Q = 0 (only reactants, K = 10) — tap
Q < K → shifts forward. With no product yet, Q = 0, far below K, so the reaction proceeds strongly forward.
▶  Think first, then check: A reaction has K = 0.01. If you compute Q = 5 for your current mixture, which way will it shift, and what does that say about the mixture?
Q = 5 is much greater than K = 0.01, so Q > K and the reaction shifts reverse (left), remaking reactants. The small K (0.01 « 1) already tells you this reaction barely favors products at all, so a mixture rich in product is far from where it wants to be — it will run backward strongly until Q drops all the way to 0.01.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A reversible reaction in a closed system reaches dynamic equilibrium: the forward and reverse rates become equal, concentrations hold constant, but the reaction never actually stops. The equilibrium constant K — products over reactants, each raised to its coefficient, with pure solids and liquids omitted — captures how far the reaction goes: large K means products are favored, small K means reactants. To predict the direction of a mixture not yet at equilibrium, compute the same ratio as the reaction quotient Q and compare: Q < K shifts right, Q > K shifts left, Q = K is equilibrium.
