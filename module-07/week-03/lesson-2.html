📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You start with known amounts of reactants, and the reaction settles at equilibrium. The forward reaction used up some reactant and made some product — but exactly how much? If you can measure just one equilibrium concentration, can you recover all the others, and even the value of K?
Last lesson you predicted the direction a stressed equilibrium would move. Today you put numbers on it. The tool is a simple piece of bookkeeping called the ICE table — Initial, Change, Equilibrium — that tracks every concentration from the moment you mix the reactants to the moment the system settles. The key insight is that the reaction can only consume and produce in fixed proportions, set by the balanced equation's coefficients. Once you write those proportions into the “Change” row, the rest is algebra. By the end you'll be able to find an unknown equilibrium concentration, or compute K itself, from a single measured value.

📚 PART 1 · THE BOOKKEEPING OF EQUILIBRIUM
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
You seal 1.00 M of H2 and 1.00 M of I2 in a flask. They react: H2 + I2 ⇌ 2HI. When the system reaches equilibrium you measure the HI and find 1.60 M. Before reading on — how much H2 do you think is left? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

An equilibrium problem almost always gives you some information — the amounts you started with, plus one measured value at equilibrium — and asks for the rest. The challenge is keeping track of how much of each substance was consumed or produced. The ICE table is a three-row ledger that does exactly that:

I — Initial: the concentrations right after mixing, before any reaction.
C — Change: how much each concentration changes as the system reaches equilibrium, written in terms of x using the coefficients.
E — Equilibrium: Initial + Change, the concentrations once the system settles.
THE ONE RULE THAT MAKES IT WORK
The “Change” row uses the balanced coefficients
reactants decrease (−), products increase (+), each scaled by its coefficient times x

If a reaction is A + 3B ⇌ 2C, then for every x of A that reacts, 3x of B reacts and 2x of C forms. The Change row would read −x, −3x, +2x. The coefficients are the gears that lock the amounts together — this is the same conservation of atoms you used when balancing equations, now used to count change.

🔬 A NOTE ON METHOD · WHERE THE BOOKKEEPING COMES FROM
The ICE table is not a natural law — it is a piece of method, a tidy way to organize a calculation so you don't lose track of what changed. The chemistry it feeds, though, rests on a real discovery. In the 1860s and 1870s two Norwegian scientists, Cato Guldberg and Peter Waage, worked out the law of mass action: at equilibrium the concentrations of products and reactants settle into a fixed ratio — the equilibrium constant K — set by the balanced equation. That law is why the K expression you learned in Lesson 7.04 works at all. The ICE table simply gives you a disciplined place to put the numbers before you plug them into K. This is worth noticing as a habit of the quantitative sciences: a good bookkeeping method turns a tangled problem into ordinary algebra, letting the underlying law do the real work. Keep the two ideas distinct — the law is the science; the table is the tool.
📚 PART 2 · A WORKED EXAMPLE — SOLVING FOR K

Let's solve the problem from the opener completely. We seal 1.00 M H2 and 1.00 M I2 in a flask (no HI yet) and let it reach equilibrium: H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2HI(g). At equilibrium we measure [HI] = 1.60 M. Find Kc.

Step 1 — Build the ICE table. Because HI is formed, the reaction runs forward, so H2 and I2 each change by −x and HI by +2x (coefficient 2).

[H2][I2][HI]
Initial1.001.000
Change−x−x+2x
Equilibrium1.00 − x1.00 − x2x

Step 2 — Use the measured value to find x. The equilibrium HI is 2x, and we measured it as 1.60 M:

2x = 1.60  →  x = 0.80

Step 3 — Fill in the equilibrium concentrations.

[H2] = 1.00 − 0.80 = 0.20 M  ·  [I2] = 1.00 − 0.80 = 0.20 M  ·  [HI] = 2(0.80) = 1.60 M

Step 4 — Write the Kc expression and plug in. Remember HI's coefficient of 2 becomes an exponent:

Kc = [HI]² / ([H2][I2]) = (1.60)² / (0.20 × 0.20)
= 2.56 / 0.04 = 64

Kc = 64 is comfortably greater than 1, which tells you products are favored — consistent with most of the iodine and hydrogen ending up as HI. The ICE table turned one measured number into the full picture of the equilibrium.

📚 PART 3 · THE OTHER DIRECTION — SOLVING FOR A CONCENTRATION

The ICE method runs both ways. If you already know K and the starting amounts, you can solve for the unknown x — and from x, every equilibrium concentration. Set up the same three rows, write the equilibrium concentrations in terms of x, substitute them into the K expression, and solve the resulting equation for x.

🔢 A USEFUL SHORTCUT — THE SMALL-x APPROXIMATION
When K is very small (much less than 1), only a tiny bit of reactant converts, so x is tiny compared with the initial concentration. In that case a term like “1.00 − x” is almost exactly 1.00, and you may approximate it as 1.00 to avoid a messy quadratic. It's a convenience worth knowing about now; you'll lean on it more in later chemistry. (A good rule of thumb: the approximation is fair when x turns out to be less than about 5% of the initial value.)
🤖 AI AS AN INSTRUMENT, NOT AN ORACLE
You could paste an equilibrium problem like the one below into an AI assistant and get an answer in seconds — but AI tools regularly slip on exactly this kind of task: dropping a coefficient, forgetting to square the [HI] term, or mishandling the −x. Treat any AI answer as a proposal to be checked, not a result to be trusted. Build your own ICE table first, work out x by hand, and use it as the standard the AI must match. If the AI's number disagrees with your table, find out which of you dropped a step — that verification against your own evidence is the real skill here.

▶  Think first, then check: Why must we know whether the reaction runs forward or reverse before writing the “Change” row signs?
Because the signs flip. If the system reaches equilibrium by running forward, reactants decrease (−x) and products increase (+x). If it must run reverse (for example, if you start with only products), reactants increase (+x) and products decrease (−x). You can decide the direction the same way you did in Lesson 7.04 — compare the reaction quotient Q to K. Get the direction right first, and the algebra follows cleanly.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

The ICE table — Initial, Change, Equilibrium — turns an equilibrium problem into ordinary algebra. Its one essential rule is that the Change row uses the balanced coefficients: reactants fall by their coefficient times x, products rise by theirs. Find x from a measured value, fill in the equilibrium row, and you can compute K or recover a missing concentration. When K is very small, the small-x approximation can save you a quadratic. Hold onto this method: it is the same bookkeeping you'll use next week for acids and bases, where the “reaction” is an acid donating a proton to water.
