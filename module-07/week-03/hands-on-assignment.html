📖 WHAT THIS BUILDS ON
From this week's lessons: Le Châtelier's principle (7.05) — predicting the direction an equilibrium shifts under a concentration, pressure, or temperature stress — and the ICE-table method (7.06) for computing K or an unknown equilibrium concentration. This set puts both to work: you'll predict shifts and run the numbers.
This is your week's “doing chemistry” work, and the Le Châtelier reasoning here is exactly what your module CER writing assignment will draw on.
📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
Work the seven problems and the reasoning question (Problem 8) below in your notebook (no spreadsheet needed this week). Show your ICE tables and the arithmetic for the calculations, and write out your reasoning for Problem 8 in full sentences — the thinking is the point. When you finish, open the answer keys to check yourself and correct anything you missed.
🔢 REMINDERS YOU'LL NEED
• Le Châtelier: a stressed equilibrium shifts to partially counteract the stress. Add → shift away; remove → shift toward. Higher pressure → toward fewer gas moles. For temperature, treat heat as a reactant (endothermic) or product (exothermic). A catalyst does not shift the position.
• ICE table: Initial / Change / Equilibrium; the Change row uses the balanced coefficients (reactants −, products +).
• K expression: K = [products]coeffs / [reactants]coeffs.
📝 THE PROBLEMS (work all seven, plus the reasoning question, in your notebook)
Part A — Predict the Le Châtelier shift (left, right, or no shift):

1. For 2SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇌ 2SO3(g), which way does the equilibrium shift if you (a) add more O2, and (b) remove SO3 as it forms?

2. For N2O4(g) ⇌ 2NO2(g) (1 mol gas ⇌ 2 mol gas), which way does the equilibrium shift if you (a) increase the pressure by shrinking the container, and (b) add a catalyst?

3. The reaction CO(g) + 2H2(g) ⇌ CH3OH(g) is exothermic. Which way does it shift if you (a) raise the temperature, and (b) lower the temperature?

Part B — Set up an ICE table and solve:

4. For H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2HI(g), a flask starts with 0.80 M H2 and 0.80 M I2 (no HI). At equilibrium [HI] = 1.20 M. Build the ICE table, find x, and report the equilibrium [H2] and [I2].

5. Using your results from Problem 4, calculate Kc = [HI]² / ([H2][I2]).

6. For N2O4(g) ⇌ 2NO2(g), a flask starts with 1.00 M N2O4 and no NO2. At equilibrium [NO2] = 0.40 M. Build the ICE table (Change: −x for N2O4, +2x for NO2), find x, and report the equilibrium [N2O4]. Then compute Kc = [NO2]² / [N2O4].

7. For the reaction A(g) ⇌ B(g) with Kc = 4.0, a flask starts with 1.00 M A and no B. Let x be the amount of A that reacts. Set up the ICE table, write Kc = x / (1.00 − x), and solve for x to find the equilibrium [A] and [B].

Part C — Explain your reasoning (write 2–3 sentences):

8. A classmate says, “If we add an iron catalyst to the reaction in Problem 6, we'll get more NO2 at equilibrium.” In your own words, explain whether they are right, and why — connect your answer to what a catalyst does to the forward and reverse reactions. Then check yourself against the model response below.
▶  Check your work — answer key
Part A — shifts:
1. (a) Adding O2 (a reactant) → shifts right. (b) Removing SO3 (a product) → shifts right to replace it.
2. (a) Increasing pressure → shifts toward fewer gas moles: 1 mol (left) vs. 2 mol (right), so shifts left (toward N2O4). (b) A catalyst → no shift (it only speeds the approach to equilibrium).
3. The reaction is exothermic, so heat is a product. (a) Raising T adds a product → shifts left. (b) Lowering T removes a product → shifts right (toward methanol).

Part B — calculations:
4. Change: −x, −x, +2x. [HI] = 2x = 1.20 → x = 0.60. [H2] = 0.80 − 0.60 = 0.20 M; [I2] = 0.20 M.
5. Kc = (1.20)² / (0.20 × 0.20) = 1.44 / 0.04 = 36.
6. Change: −x, +2x. [NO2] = 2x = 0.40 → x = 0.20. [N2O4] = 1.00 − 0.20 = 0.80 M. Kc = (0.40)² / 0.80 = 0.16 / 0.80 = 0.20.
7. ICE: A = 1.00 − x, B = x. Kc = x / (1.00 − x) = 4.0 → x = 4.0(1.00 − x) = 4.0 − 4.0x → 5.0x = 4.0 → x = 0.80. So [A] = 1.00 − 0.80 = 0.20 M and [B] = 0.80 M. (Check: 0.80 / 0.20 = 4.0 ✓.)
▶  Check your reasoning — model response to Problem 8
The classmate is wrong. A catalyst speeds up the forward and the reverse reactions by the same amount, so the system reaches equilibrium faster but arrives at the exact same balance point — the same equilibrium concentrations and the same value of Kc. Because it does not favor one direction over the other, a catalyst never changes how much product forms; it only changes how soon you get there. To actually increase NO2 at equilibrium you would need a real stress, such as raising the temperature (this reaction is endothermic) or lowering the pressure. Your wording can differ — look for these ideas: (1) catalyst speeds both directions equally, (2) equilibrium position / K is unchanged, (3) only the rate of reaching equilibrium changes.
