📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You're handed a beaker of acid and told only that it is hydrochloric acid — but no one knows how concentrated it is. With nothing but a base of known concentration, a buret, and a single drop of indicator, how could you pin down the unknown concentration to three significant figures?
This is the question that closes Module 7 and ties the whole acid-base story together. The answer is the titration — one of the oldest and most exact tools in chemistry. By adding a measured volume of a known solution until the reaction is exactly complete, a chemist converts an invisible quantity into a number on a buret. This lesson defines the equivalence point, gives you the equation that solves a strong-acid / strong-base titration, shows how an indicator and the titration curve announce when you've arrived, and walks through a full worked calculation. It is chemistry as careful accounting — the discipline of the quantitative.

📚 PART 1 · THE TITRATION AND THE EQUIVALENCE POINT
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
You drip a base, drop by drop, into an acid that has one drop of indicator in it. For a long time nothing seems to happen — then, suddenly, a single extra drop turns the whole flask pink and it stays. What does that sudden, lasting color change mark? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

A titration is a method for finding the unknown concentration of a solution by reacting it with a solution of known concentration, called the titrant, added in carefully measured volumes from a buret. You add titrant until the reaction is exactly complete — the moment when the moles of added base equal the moles of acid present (or vice versa). That stoichiometric meeting point is the equivalence point. Because you know the titrant's concentration and can read the exact volume delivered, you can work backward to the moles — and then to the concentration — of the unknown.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW · WHERE “TITRATION” COMES FROM
The word titration traces back to the French titre — a term that once described the fineness or standard of gold, the proportion of pure metal in an alloy. It carried the idea of measuring a substance against a known standard, which is exactly what a titration does with concentration. The technique grew up in the late 1700s and 1800s as chemists developed volumetric analysis: instead of weighing a product, they measured the volume of a standard solution needed to complete a reaction. The French chemist Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac was among those who refined these volumetric methods in the early nineteenth century, building the careful glassware and standard solutions that made a buret reading trustworthy. Long before pH meters and sensors existed, a chemist armed with a standard solution, a graduated tube, and a color-changing indicator could pin down an unknown concentration to remarkable precision — the same disciplined accounting you will practice this week.

For the simplest case — a monoprotic strong acid titrated with a monoprotic strong base — the acid and base react one-to-one, so at the equivalence point the moles are equal. Since moles = molarity × volume (M × V), this gives a compact working equation:

THE EQUIVALENCE-POINT EQUATION (1:1 STRONG–STRONG)
MaVa = MbVb
moles of acid = moles of base at the equivalence point
▶  Think first, then check: At the equivalence point, is the acid “gone”? What exactly is equal there?
At the equivalence point, the moles of added base exactly equal the moles of acid that were originally present — they have reacted in stoichiometric proportion. The acid hasn't vanished into nothing; it has been converted to water and a salt. Whether the resulting solution is neutral, basic, or acidic depends on that salt (Lesson 7.10!), which is why the equivalence-point pH isn't always 7.
🧠 HOW SCIENTISTS KNOW · NATURE OF SCIENCE
You cannot see the exact instant when moles of base equal moles of acid — the equivalence point is not observed directly, it is inferred from evidence. The color flip of an indicator and the steep jump on a titration curve are the observable signals; the equivalence point is the conclusion a chemist draws from them. That is why careful scientists distinguish the endpoint (where the indicator actually changes) from the true equivalence point, and why they choose an indicator whose color change falls as close to it as possible. Think it through: if two students got slightly different volumes because their indicator flipped at slightly different moments, does that mean chemistry is imprecise — or that every measurement carries uncertainty that good technique works to shrink?
📚 PART 2 · A WORKED TITRATION CALCULATION

Return to the opening puzzle. You have 25.0 mL of HCl of unknown concentration, and you titrate it with 0.100 M NaOH. The indicator turns at the equivalence point after you have added 30.0 mL of the base. Find the concentration of the HCl.

Step 1 — moles of base added: mol NaOH = M × V = 0.100 mol/L × 0.0300 L = 0.00300 mol.
Step 2 — use the 1:1 ratio: HCl + NaOH react one-to-one, so mol HCl = mol NaOH = 0.00300 mol.
Step 3 — divide by the acid's volume: MHCl = mol / V = 0.00300 mol / 0.0250 L = 0.120 M.

Check with MaVa = MbVb: Ma = (MbVb) / Va = (0.100 × 30.0) / 25.0 = 3.00 / 25.0 = 0.120 M. ✓ Same answer.

Notice the unit care: convert milliliters to liters before multiplying, and keep three significant figures throughout. The whole result — 0.120 M — rests on a single accurately read buret volume. This is why titration glassware is calibrated so finely.

🤖 AI AS AN INSTRUMENT · NOW
Hand an AI tool a titration problem and it will usually set it up well — but watch for two classic slips: forgetting to convert milliliters to liters (giving an answer off by a factor of 1000), and ignoring reaction stoichiometry when the acid or base is not 1:1 (for a diprotic acid like H2SO4, you need twice as much base per mole of acid). Treat the AI like any instrument: have it show every step, then recompute by hand and confirm the units cancel to mol/L. The buret reading is the evidence; the AI is a second opinion to verify, not the judge.
⚡ FLIP CARDS · WHAT'S THE pH AT THE EQUIVALENCE POINT?
Predict the equivalence-point pH for each titration type, then tap to flip and check. (Hint: think about the salt that forms — Lesson 7.10.)
⚖️ strong acid + strong base — tap to reveal
pH = 7. The salt formed (e.g., NaCl) has only spectator ions, so the solution is neutral at the equivalence point.
🧪 weak acid + strong base — tap to reveal
pH > 7 (basic). The salt's anion is the conjugate base of a weak acid, so it hydrolyzes and leaves the solution basic.
🧊 strong acid + weak base — tap to reveal
pH < 7 (acidic). The salt's cation is the conjugate acid of a weak base, so it hydrolyzes and leaves the solution acidic.
🌹 phenolphthalein — tap to reveal
Colorless in acid, pink in base (turns around pH 8.2–10). A common indicator, well suited to titrations ending near or above pH 7.
📚 PART 3 · READING THE TITRATION CURVE

If you record the pH after every addition of titrant and plot pH (vertical axis) against volume of titrant added (horizontal axis), you get the titration curve. For a strong acid titrated with a strong base, the curve starts low (acidic), rises only gently at first, then shoots up almost vertically through a narrow band of added volume, and finally levels off high (basic). That steep vertical region is the signature of the equivalence point: a single drop near the end changes the pH by several units, because so little acid remains to absorb the added base. The midpoint of the steep jump brackets the equivalence point — for a strong–strong titration it crosses pH 7. Study the curve below before the questions.

A strong-acid / strong-base titration curve. The blue curve is gentle at first, then jumps steeply; the red dot is the equivalence point, here at pH 7. (Generic shape, not drawn to scale for any one titration.)

A well-chosen indicator changes color within that steep region, so its color change pinpoints the equivalence point. Phenolphthalein — colorless in acid, pink in base, switching around pH 8.2–10 — is a classic choice for titrations that end near or above neutral, which is why a strong–strong or weak-acid / strong-base titration so often ends with a faint, lasting pink. (For a weak acid titrated with a strong base, the equivalence point lands above 7, and the steep region centers higher; phenolphthalein still works well.)

☀️ THEN → NOW → FUTURE · TITRATION IN MEDICINE AND MEDICINE-MAKING
Then: nineteenth-century chemists used titration and the buffer chemistry of the last two lessons to measure the acidity of blood, water, and foods — some of the first exact chemical tests of the human body and its environment. Now: the same techniques are woven through modern pharmaceutical quality control. Before a batch of medicine reaches a pharmacy, analysts commonly run titrations to confirm the concentration of an active ingredient, and they formulate many drugs as buffered solutions so the product holds a safe, stable pH — an eye drop or an IV fluid must sit near the pH of the tissue it touches. Clinicians, in turn, monitor a patient's blood pH, which the carbonic-acid / bicarbonate buffer holds near 7.4, as a window into health. Future: researchers are working to make this measurement faster and more automated — miniaturized sensors, lab-on-a-chip devices, and continuous pH monitoring that could one day track a patient's or a reaction's acid-base balance in real time. The details will keep changing, but the core idea you're learning — find the equivalence point, hold the pH steady — is likely to remain at the heart of it.

▶  Think first, then check: It takes 20.0 mL of 0.150 M NaOH to titrate 40.0 mL of HCl to the equivalence point. What is the HCl concentration?
0.0750 M. Using MaVa = MbVb: Ma = (MbVb)/Va = (0.150 × 20.0)/40.0 = 3.00/40.0 = 0.0750 M. (Or: mol NaOH = 0.150 × 0.0200 = 0.00300 mol = mol HCl; M = 0.00300/0.0400 = 0.0750 M.) The acid is more dilute than the base because it took less base to reach equivalence.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A titration measures an unknown concentration by adding a known titrant to the equivalence point, where moles of acid equal moles of base — and for a 1:1 strong–strong titration, MaVa = MbVb. The equivalence-point pH is 7 for strong–strong, above 7 for weak acid + strong base, and below 7 for strong acid + weak base, set by the salt that forms. An indicator like phenolphthalein and the steep region of the titration curve both announce the equivalence point. Hold onto this: this single technique unites stoichiometry, molarity, equilibrium, and salt hydrolysis — the whole module — into one precise, repeatable measurement.
