Hydrolysis of Salts

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Dissolve ordinary table salt (NaCl) in water and the pH stays right around 7. Dissolve baking-soda's cousin, sodium acetate, and the water turns slightly basic. Dissolve ammonium chloride and it turns slightly acidic. All three are “just salts” — so why do they leave the water at three different pH values?
You learned in Lesson 7.09 that some acids and bases are strong and some are weak. That single distinction turns out to settle a puzzle that would otherwise look like magic: a salt is the product of an acid reacting with a base, and the strength of the acid and base it came from decides whether its solution ends up neutral, basic, or acidic. The hidden actor is a reaction called hydrolysis — certain dissolved ions react with water itself. This lesson gives you a clean three-case rule for predicting a salt solution's pH just by tracing the salt back to its parent acid and base.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to trace a salt back to the acid and base that formed it and identify each as strong or weak.
2

3
I will be able to explain why only the conjugate of a weak acid or weak base reacts with water, while the conjugate of a strong acid or strong base is a spectator.

📚 PART 1 · A SALT IS NOT ALWAYS NEUTRAL
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
Table salt, NaCl, is the salt left behind when hydrochloric acid (HCl) reacts with sodium hydroxide (NaOH). Both of those are strong — HCl a strong acid, NaOH a strong base. When you dissolve the leftover NaCl in pure water, what do you predict the pH will be? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

When an acid neutralizes a base, the products are water and a salt — an ionic compound made of the base's cation and the acid's anion. It is tempting to assume that, because neutralization “cancels out” the acid and base, every salt solution must be neutral. Measure the pH, though, and you find this is often false. Sodium acetate makes a slightly basic solution; ammonium chloride makes a slightly acidic one. The salt itself is not an acid or a base in the usual sense, yet its solution shifts the pH. The explanation is a quiet reaction between certain dissolved ions and the water around them.

That reaction is called salt hydrolysis (the word means “splitting by water”). When a salt dissolves, it separates into its ions. Some of those ions are conjugates of weak acids or weak bases — and a conjugate of something weak is itself reactive enough to pull a proton from water, or donate one to it, nudging the pH. The key, which you already hold from Lesson 7.09, is this: the stronger an acid, the weaker its conjugate base — and vice versa. An ion that is the conjugate of a strong acid or base is so feeble it does nothing; an ion that is the conjugate of a weak acid or base is strong enough to react.

THE CENTRAL IDEA
Only the conjugate of a WEAK acid or WEAK base hydrolyzes
conjugates of strong acids/bases are spectators — they leave the pH alone
▶  Think first, then check: HCl is a strong acid. Is its conjugate base, Cl⁻, a strong base or a hopelessly weak one?
Hopelessly weak. Because HCl is a strong acid (it gives up its proton almost completely), its conjugate base Cl⁻ has essentially no pull to grab a proton back from water. So Cl⁻ just floats in solution as a spectator and does not affect the pH. This inverse relationship — strong acid → negligibly weak conjugate base — is the engine behind the whole three-case rule.
💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW · HYDROLYSIS IS ALL AROUND YOU
Salt hydrolysis is not a textbook curiosity — it quietly shapes everyday life. Reach for baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) and you are using a salt whose anion is the conjugate base of a weak acid; dissolved in water it turns mildly basic, which is exactly why a spoonful can soothe an acidic stomach or lift the pH of a cake batter. The ammonium salts in many fertilizers do the opposite: their cation is the conjugate acid of the weak base ammonia, so as they dissolve they release H3O+ and slowly turn soil more acidic. Even the fluid inside your own cells is kept in a narrow pH window partly by the hydrolysis of dissolved salts — a preview of the biological buffering you'll study in the next lesson. The same three-case rule you're learning here lets a chemist, a gardener, or a physiologist predict which way a dissolved salt will nudge the pH before ever reaching for a pH meter.
📚 PART 2 · THE THREE-CASE RULE

To predict a salt solution's pH, trace the salt back to the acid and base that would form it, label each as strong or weak, and apply one of three cases. The strong-from-strong combination is neutral; a weak parent on either side tips the balance.

Case 1. Strong acid + strong base → neutral

Take NaCl, from the strong acid HCl and the strong base NaOH. Both ions — Na⁺ (conjugate of the strong base) and Cl⁻ (conjugate of the strong acid) — are far too weak to react with water. They are pure spectators. With no ion pulling a proton from water or donating one to it, the solution stays at pH ≈ 7, neutral. Potassium nitrate, KNO3 (from HNO3 + KOH), behaves the same way.

Case 2. Strong base + weak acid → basic

Take sodium acetate, NaC2H3O2, from the strong base NaOH and the weak acid acetic acid. The Na⁺ is a spectator, but the acetate ion (C2H3O2−) is the conjugate base of a weak acid — so it is a meaningful base. It grabs a proton from water:

C2H3O2− + H2O  ⇌  HC2H3O2 + OH−

By stealing a proton it leaves extra OH− behind, so the solution turns basic, pH > 7.

Case 3. Strong acid + weak base → acidic

Take ammonium chloride, NH4Cl, from the strong acid HCl and the weak base ammonia. Now the Cl⁻ is the spectator, but the ammonium ion (NH4+) is the conjugate acid of a weak base — so it is a meaningful acid. It donates a proton to water:

NH4+ + H2O  ⇌  NH3 + H3O+

By releasing a proton it makes extra H3O+, so the solution turns acidic, pH < 7.

The whole rule in one line:
strong acid + strong base → neutral  ·  strong base + weak acid → basic  ·  strong acid + weak base → acidic.
Memory hook: the strong parent wins. Two strong parents → a stand-off (neutral); one weak parent → the strong parent's character (acidic or basic) shows through.
💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW · THE USUAL SPECTATORS
A short list of ions almost always behave as spectators because they are conjugates of strong acids or strong bases: the cations of strong bases — Na+, K+ (and the other group-1 metals) — and the anions of strong acids — Cl−, Br−, I−, NO3−. When you spot one of these in a salt, cross it off: it will not change the pH. Whatever decides the pH must be the other ion.

📚 PART 3 · A WORKED WALK-THROUGH

Here is the routine you'll use every time. Suppose you're asked whether a solution of NH4Cl is neutral, basic, or acidic.

Step 1 — split the salt: NH4Cl → NH4+ + Cl−.
Step 2 — trace each ion to a parent: NH4+ is the conjugate acid of ammonia (a weak base); Cl− is the conjugate base of HCl (a strong acid).
Step 3 — cross off spectators: Cl− comes from a strong acid, so it's a spectator. The decider is NH4+.
Step 4 — ask what the active ion does to water: NH4+ donates a proton to water, producing H3O+.
Conclusion: extra H3O+ means acidic, pH < 7. ✓
🔗 CONNECT IT · HYDROLYSIS IS EQUILIBRIUM IN DISGUISE
Notice the double arrow (⇌) in every hydrolysis equation. Hydrolysis is just the equilibrium chemistry from earlier this module applied to water: a weak conjugate sets up a small equilibrium with H2O, and its tiny extra OH− or H3O+ is what tips the pH. The shift is usually slight — these are weak players — which is why salt solutions drift only a little off neutral rather than becoming as harsh as a strong acid or base.
🧠 HOW SCIENTISTS KNOW · NATURE OF SCIENCE
Notice that no one sees a proton jump from water to an acetate ion. The three-case rule works because chemists trust a model — equilibrium between weak conjugates and water — that they built from repeated, measurable evidence: dissolve the salt, measure the pH, and check whether the prediction holds. The model earns its authority not because it is obvious but because it keeps predicting the measured pH correctly, salt after salt. Think it through: if you dissolved a brand-new salt and the measured pH disagreed with the three-case rule, which would you trust — the rule or the measurement — and what would a careful scientist do next?

▶  Think first, then check: In a solution of NaC2H3O2, which ion is the spectator and which one decides the pH?
Na+ is the spectator (it's the conjugate of the strong base NaOH, so it ignores water). Always find the ion that came from the weak parent — that's your decider.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A salt is not automatically neutral. Trace it to its parent acid and base: two strong parents leave the solution neutral; a strong base with a weak acid leaves it basic; a strong acid with a weak base leaves it acidic. The reason is hydrolysis — only the conjugate of a weak acid or base reacts with water, while spectators like Na+, K+, Cl−, and NO3− leave the pH alone. Hold onto this: the salt left at a titration's equivalence point obeys this exact rule, which is why not every “neutralization” ends at pH 7.
