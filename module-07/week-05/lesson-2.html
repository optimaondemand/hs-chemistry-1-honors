Lesson 7.11
Buffers & Polyprotic Acids
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
When you sprint up a flight of stairs, your muscles dump acid into your bloodstream by the second. Add that much acid to a glass of pure water and the pH would crash. Yet your blood pH barely moves — it holds near 7.4. What in your blood is quietly absorbing the shock?
Pure water is defenseless: a single drop of strong acid sends its pH plummeting. But many real solutions — your blood, ocean water, the inside of a living cell — resist pH change remarkably well. The trick is a special partnership called a buffer: a weak acid kept side-by-side with its conjugate base, so the mixture can swallow added acid or added base without lurching. This lesson shows how a buffer works, how the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation predicts its pH, and how acids that carry more than one proton (polyprotic acids) give them up one careful step at a time. The same chemistry runs through your veins and through the world's oceans.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to describe what a buffer is — a weak acid and its conjugate base together — and explain how it resists pH change when acid or base is added.
2

3
I will be able to explain that a polyprotic acid donates its protons in successive steps with decreasing ease (Ka1 > Ka2 > Ka3).

📚 PART 1 · WHAT A BUFFER IS AND WHY IT WORKS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
During hard exercise your muscles release acid into your blood, yet your blood pH barely moves from 7.4. What do you think keeps it so steady? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

A buffer is a solution that resists changes in pH when a small amount of acid or base is added. It is built from a weak acid together with its conjugate base (for example acetic acid, HC2H3O2, mixed with acetate, C2H3O2−) — or, equivalently, a weak base together with its conjugate acid. The two partners share the work. The conjugate base stands ready to absorb any added acid, and the weak acid stands ready to neutralize any added base. Because a reservoir of each is present at the same time, the solution has a defense against attacks from both directions.

Picture the acetic-acid / acetate buffer absorbing a splash of strong acid. The flood of H3O+ would normally crash the pH — but here the acetate ions intercept it, converting the strong acid into more weak acetic acid (which barely ionizes), so the free H3O+ hardly rises. If instead a splash of strong base arrives, the acetic acid donates protons to neutralize the added OH−. Either way, the buffer trades a dangerous strong acid or base for a tame weak one, and the pH holds nearly steady. The defense lasts only until one partner is used up — that limit is the buffer's capacity — but within it, the solution is remarkably stable.

THE CENTRAL IDEA
A buffer = a weak acid + its conjugate base, working together
one partner soaks up added acid, the other soaks up added base — so the pH barely moves
▶  Think first, then check: Could a mixture of HCl (a strong acid) and NaCl make a good buffer?
No. A buffer needs a weak acid and its conjugate base. HCl is a strong acid, so it ionizes completely — there is no reservoir of intact HCl waiting to neutralize added base, and Cl− is too weak a conjugate to absorb added acid. Buffers are built from weak acid/base pairs precisely because a weak acid keeps a standing reserve of un-ionized molecules and its conjugate is reactive enough to help.
📚 PART 2 · PREDICTING A BUFFER'S pH

A buffer's pH depends on two things: the strength of its weak acid (captured by its pKa) and the ratio of conjugate base to weak acid present. One tidy equation ties them together — the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation:

pH = pKa + log( [A−] / [HA] )

Here HA is the weak acid and A− is its conjugate base. The most useful single fact falls right out of it: when the amounts of conjugate base and weak acid are equal, [A−] = [HA], the ratio is 1, and log(1) = 0 — so pH = pKa. A chemist who wants a buffer to hold a particular pH simply chooses a weak acid whose pKa is near that target and mixes it roughly half-and-half with its conjugate base.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW · A BUFFER EQUATION BORN IN THE BLOODSTREAM
The equation on this page did not begin in a chemistry lab — it began with a question about the human body. In the early 1900s the American physiologist Lawrence Henderson was studying how blood manages to hold such a steady pH despite the acid our cells constantly produce. Working from the carbonic-acid / bicarbonate equilibrium, he wrote down the relationship between a buffer's pH and the ratio of its two partners. A few years later the Danish chemist Karl Hasselbalch rewrote Henderson's expression using the then-new logarithmic pH scale, giving us the compact form — pH = pKa + log([A−]/[HA]) — that still carries both their names. It is a small reminder that much of acid-base chemistry was worked out by people trying to understand living things: the same math a pharmacist uses to design a stable medicine was first assembled to explain why blood does not turn to acid when you run.

A worked example

Acetic acid has Ka ≈ 1.8 × 10−5, so pKa = −log(1.8 × 10−5) ≈ 4.74.

Mix it half-and-half with acetate so [A−] = [HA]:
pH = 4.74 + log(1) = 4.74 + 0 = 4.74 — the buffer holds at its pKa.

Now use ten times as much acetate as acetic acid ([A−]/[HA] = 10):
pH = 4.74 + log(10) = 4.74 + 1 = 5.74 — more conjugate base nudges the pH up by one unit.

⬇️ more HA than A− — tap to reveal

Best buffer. A buffer works best near its pKa, so pick a weak acid whose pKa is close to the pH you need to hold.
📚 PART 3 · POLYPROTIC ACIDS — PROTONS IN STEPS

Most acids you've met donate a single proton. But some carry two or three ionizable hydrogens; these are polyprotic acids, and they don't release all their protons at once. They give them up in successive steps, and each step is harder than the last — so the ionization constants fall in order: Ka1 > Ka2 > Ka3. The reason is intuitive: pulling a positive proton off a neutral molecule is easier than pulling the next proton off an ion that is already negatively charged, and harder still off a doubly negative ion. The negative charge holds the remaining protons more tightly.

Phosphoric acid (H3PO4), triprotic — three steps:
Step 1: H3PO4 ⇌ H+ + H2PO4−  (Ka1, easiest)
Step 2: H2PO4− ⇌ H+ + HPO42−  (Ka2, harder)
Step 3: HPO42− ⇌ H+ + PO43−  (Ka3, hardest)
Each successive proton is pulled from an increasingly negative ion, so Ka1 > Ka2 > Ka3.

Three polyprotic acids worth knowing: sulfuric acid (H2SO4) and carbonic acid (H2CO3) are diprotic (two protons), and phosphoric acid (H3PO4) is triprotic (three). Notice that the intermediate species — H2PO4−, HCO3−, and the like — are conjugate partners that can act as either an acid or a base. That is exactly why polyprotic systems make such natural buffers: each step provides a ready weak-acid / conjugate-base pair.

☀️ NOW · BUFFERS AT WORK IN THE WORLD
Your blood is buffered by the carbonic-acid / bicarbonate pair, H2CO3 / HCO3−, which holds it near pH 7.4 — the same kind of weak-acid / conjugate-base partnership you studied today, drawn from diprotic carbonic acid. The very same chemistry governs the ocean. Scientists are currently studying ocean acidification: as the atmosphere's carbon dioxide rises, more CO2 dissolves into seawater and forms carbonic acid, slowly lowering the ocean's pH. The ocean's carbonate / bicarbonate buffer system softens the blow, but it can be overwhelmed — and as more carbonate is consumed buffering the added acid, less is left for corals and shellfish to build their calcium-carbonate shells and skeletons. Researchers are working to measure how fast this is happening and what it means for marine life. It is a striking reminder that a buffer is powerful but not unlimited: the same equilibrium that protects your blood and the sea can be pushed past its capacity.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A buffer is a weak acid paired with its conjugate base; one partner absorbs added acid, the other absorbs added base, so the pH barely moves. The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, pH = pKa + log([A−]/[HA]), predicts the pH — and when the two are equal, pH = pKa. Polyprotic acids release protons in steps with Ka1 > Ka2 > Ka3, supplying natural buffer pairs. Hold onto this: the carbonic-acid / bicarbonate buffer holds your blood at 7.4 and softens ocean acidification — the same equilibrium chemistry, at two very different scales, both powerful and both limited.
