📖 WHAT THIS BUILDS ON
From this week's lessons: the titration and its equivalence point (7.12) and the equation MaVa = MbVb for a 1:1 strong–strong titration. Here you take a real set of (volume, pH) readings, plot the titration curve in Excel, read the equivalence-point volume off the steep region, and use it to compute an unknown acid concentration — exactly what a chemist does at the bench.
This is your week's “doing chemistry” work, and it rehearses the titration reasoning you'll narrate in your Week-5 VR Titration Station recording.
📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
A student titrated 25.0 mL of hydrochloric acid (HCl) of unknown concentration with 0.100 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH), recording the pH after each addition. In Excel, you'll enter their data, plot pH versus volume of NaOH added as a Scatter (XY) chart, identify the equivalence-point volume from the steep vertical region of the curve, and then use MaVa = MbVb to compute the unknown HCl concentration. The spreadsheet draws the curve; your job is to read it correctly and finish the calculation.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
The pH barely budges for the first 25 mL, then leaps several units over a single milliliter. How does that sudden steep jump tell you exactly how much acid was in the flask — and therefore how concentrated it was?
🔢 WHAT YOU'RE GIVEN
Volume of HCl titrated: Va = 25.0 mL = 0.0250 L
Concentration of NaOH titrant: Mb = 0.100 M
Reaction is 1:1 (HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H2O), so at equivalence MaVa = MbVb.

📝 WHAT TO DO IN EXCEL
1
Enter the two-column table with headers in row 1: Volume NaOH (mL) and pH. Put your givens off to the side, e.g. Va = 25.0 mL and Mb = 0.100.
2
Plot the titration curve. Select both columns, insert a Scatter (XY) chart (use the “Scatter with Smooth Lines and Markers” style), and title it “Titration Curve: HCl with 0.100 M NaOH.” Label the x-axis Volume NaOH added (mL) and the y-axis pH.
3
Find the equivalence-point volume. Locate the steep, nearly vertical jump on your curve. The equivalence point is at its midpoint — the volume where pH passes through 7. In a cell labeled V_eq, type that volume (in mL).
4
Compute moles of NaOH at equivalence in a labeled cell: =Mb*(V_eq/1000) (divide mL by 1000 to get liters). Because the reaction is 1:1, this equals the moles of HCl.
5
Compute the HCl concentration in a labeled cell: =mol_HCl/(Va/1000) — moles of HCl divided by the acid's volume in liters. This is Ma.
6
In cells or a text box below the table, answer the three Analysis Questions below.
❓ ANALYSIS QUESTIONS (answer in your spreadsheet)
A. From your curve, what is the equivalence-point volume (in mL), and how did the shape of the curve tell you where it is?
B. Show your MaVa = MbVb work and report the HCl concentration to three significant figures.
C. This was a strong acid titrated with a strong base, and the equivalence-point pH is 7.00. In one or two sentences, explain (using Lesson 7.10) why the salt formed (NaCl) leaves the solution neutral — and what would have been different if the acid had been weak instead.
🧠 REASON IT THROUGH (WRITE 2–3 SENTENCES, THEN SELF-CHECK)
D. Suppose you had titrated the same 25.0 mL of this HCl with 0.200 M NaOH — twice as concentrated — instead of 0.100 M. Would the equivalence-point volume be larger, smaller, or unchanged? Explain your reasoning in terms of moles, then predict the new volume. Write your answer in the spreadsheet before opening the model answer below.
▶  Check your reasoning — model answer for D
Smaller — about 15.0 mL. The amount of HCl in the flask has not changed, so you still need the same number of moles of NaOH to reach the equivalence point: 0.00300 mol. But now each milliliter of titrant carries twice as much NaOH, so it takes only half the volume to deliver those moles. Check: Vb = mol / Mb = 0.00300 mol / 0.200 mol/L = 0.0150 L = 15.0 mL. The equivalence point is set by moles, not volume — a more concentrated titrant simply reaches it sooner. (Notice the computed HCl concentration would still come out to 0.120 M, exactly as before.)
▶  Check your work — answer key
Equivalence-point volume: V_eq = 30.0 mL of NaOH. It is the midpoint of the steep vertical jump (pH leaps from about 2.7 at 29.0 mL to about 11.3 at 31.0 mL), passing through pH 7.00 at 30.0 mL.

Moles of NaOH at equivalence: 0.100 mol/L × 0.0300 L = 0.00300 mol = moles of HCl (1:1 reaction).

HCl concentration: Ma = mol HCl / Va = 0.00300 mol / 0.0250 L = 0.120 M.
Check with MaVa = MbVb: Ma = (0.100 × 30.0) / 25.0 = 3.00 / 25.0 = 0.120 M. ✓

A. 30.0 mL. The curve is nearly flat (gentle) until about 28 mL, then jumps almost vertically over roughly one milliliter; the equivalence point is the middle of that jump, where pH = 7.
B. Ma = (MbVb)/Va = (0.100 M × 30.0 mL)/(25.0 mL) = 0.120 M HCl.
C. NaCl comes from the strong acid HCl and the strong base NaOH, so both Na+ and Cl− are spectator ions that don't react with water — the solution stays neutral (pH 7) at equivalence. If the acid had been weak, the salt's anion would be the conjugate base of a weak acid and would hydrolyze, so the equivalence-point pH would be above 7 (basic) and the steep region would center higher.
