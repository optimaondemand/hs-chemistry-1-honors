Entropy, Free Energy & the ΔG Calculation

📖 WHAT THIS BUILDS ON
From this week's lessons: the sign rules for ΔS (7.01), the Second-Law idea that the total entropy decides spontaneity (7.02), and a first look ahead at the single number that fuses energy and entropy — the Gibbs free energy, ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. Here you'll predict signs, read the spontaneity sign chart, and run the ΔG calculation by hand — including the unit trap that snares almost everyone.
This is your week's “doing chemistry” work. Do it on paper or in your notebook — no spreadsheet needed this week.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
Energy (ΔH) and entropy (ΔS) sometimes pull in the same direction and sometimes fight each other. How does a single number, ΔG, settle the argument — and when does temperature get the deciding vote?
🧰 YOUR TOOLBOX
ΔG = ΔH − TΔS  (T in kelvin) ·  ΔG < 0 spontaneous ·  ΔG > 0 nonspontaneous ·  ΔG = 0 at equilibrium.
Kelvin: T(K) = T(°C) + 273  (so 25 °C = 298 K).
⚠️ THE UNIT TRAP — READ THIS TWICE
ΔH is usually given in kJ, but ΔS is given in J/K. Before you subtract, you MUST put them in the same unit — convert ΔS from J/K to kJ/K by dividing by 1000 (e.g. 50 J/K = 0.050 kJ/K). Forgetting this makes your TΔS term 1000× too big and your answer nonsense. Convert first, subtract second.
📝 PART A · PREDICT THE SIGN OF ΔS (items 1–3)

For each process, state whether the entropy of the system increases (ΔS > 0) or decreases (ΔS < 0), and give a one-line reason.

1. Dry ice sublimes: CO2(s) → CO2(g).
2. Steam condenses on a mirror: H2O(g) → H2O(l).
3. 2 SO2(g) + O2(g) → 2 SO3(g).
📊 PART B · READ THE SPONTANEITY SIGN CHART (items 4–5)

Use the chart below: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS decides spontaneity. For each case, say whether it is spontaneous at all temperatures, no temperatures, only low T, or only high T.

ΔH ΔS Spontaneous when…
− (exothermic)+ (increase)at ALL temperatures
+ (endothermic)− (decrease)at NO temperatures
− (exothermic)− (decrease)only at LOW T
+ (endothermic)+ (increase)only at HIGH T
4. A reaction has ΔH = −120 kJ and ΔS = +150 J/K. Spontaneous at all, none, low, or high T?
5. A reaction has ΔH = +85 kJ and ΔS = −40 J/K. Spontaneous at all, none, low, or high T?
🧮 PART C · COMPUTE ΔG AND DECIDE (items 6–8)

Use ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. Convert ΔS to kJ/K first. Show TΔS, then ΔG, then state spontaneous or not.

6. ΔH = −100 kJ, ΔS = +50 J/K, T = 298 K. Find TΔS and ΔG. Spontaneous?
7. ΔH = +30 kJ, ΔS = +90 J/K, T = 500 K. Find TΔS and ΔG. Spontaneous? (Watch the units!)
8. ΔH = +30 kJ, ΔS = +90 J/K, T = 200 K. Find TΔS and ΔG. Spontaneous? Compare your answer to item 7 — what role did temperature play?
💡 WORKED EXAMPLE (the method for item 6)
ΔH = −100 kJ, ΔS = +50 J/K, T = 298 K.
Step 1 — fix the units: ΔS = 50 J/K = 0.050 kJ/K.
Step 2 — TΔS: 298 K × 0.050 kJ/K = 14.9 kJ.
Step 3 — ΔG: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS = −100 − 14.9 = −114.9 kJ.
Verdict: ΔG < 0, so the reaction is spontaneous. (Both ΔH < 0 and ΔS > 0, so the sign chart already promised “spontaneous at all T” — the calculation confirms it.)
✍️ PART D · EXPLAIN YOUR REASONING IN WORDS (item 9)

Numbers alone aren't understanding. Put the chemistry into a sentence or two of your own words — do this before you open the model answer.

9. Items 7 and 8 are the same reaction (ΔH = +30 kJ, ΔS = +90 J/K) at two different temperatures, yet one is spontaneous and the other is not. In 2–3 sentences, explain why temperature changed the verdict — referring to how the TΔS term competes with ΔH in ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. (Write your explanation first; then check it below.)
▶  Model answer — open only after you've written yours
Because ΔH is positive (endothermic), energy alone opposes the reaction; only the entropy term can make ΔG negative. Since ΔS is positive, the TΔS term grows as temperature rises — at 500 K, TΔS = 45 kJ is large enough to overcome the +30 kJ ΔH, so ΔG is negative and the reaction is spontaneous; at 200 K, TΔS is only 18 kJ, too small to overcome ΔH, so ΔG is positive and it is not. Temperature is the deciding vote whenever ΔH and ΔS push in opposite directions (here, both positive) — a higher T lets entropy win.
🤖 AI IS AN INSTRUMENT, NOT AN ORACLE
If you ask an AI tool to compute a ΔG, it will often forget to convert ΔS from J/K to kJ/K — returning an answer about 1000 times too large, or even the wrong sign. Treat any AI result as a claim to verify, not an answer: redo the J→kJ conversion and the subtraction by hand, and confirm the sign matches the sign chart. Reasoning from the evidence is the skill; the calculator is just an instrument.
🔑 CHECK YOUR WORK
▶  Answer key — open after you've tried all eight
Part A — sign of ΔS:
1. ΔS > 0 (increase). Solid → gas frees particles into a huge volume; sublimation is a big jump in disorder.
2. ΔS < 0 (decrease). Gas → liquid pulls free-flying molecules into a tight liquid — fewer arrangements.
3. ΔS < 0 (decrease). 3 moles of gas (2 SO2 + 1 O2) become 2 moles of gas (2 SO3) — fewer gas moles.

Part B — sign chart:
4. ΔH < 0 and ΔS > 0 → spontaneous at ALL temperatures (both factors favor it).
5. ΔH > 0 and ΔS < 0 → spontaneous at NO temperatures (both factors oppose it).

Part C — ΔG calculations (convert ΔS to kJ/K first!):
6. ΔS = 0.050 kJ/K; TΔS = 298 × 0.050 = 14.9 kJ; ΔG = −100 − 14.9 = −114.9 kJ → spontaneous (ΔG < 0).
7. ΔS = 0.090 kJ/K; TΔS = 500 × 0.090 = 45 kJ; ΔG = +30 − 45 = −15 kJ → spontaneous (ΔG < 0). The big TΔS term overpowered the positive ΔH.
8. ΔS = 0.090 kJ/K; TΔS = 200 × 0.090 = 18 kJ; ΔG = +30 − 18 = +12 kJ → not spontaneous (ΔG > 0). Same reaction as item 7, but the lower temperature shrank TΔS so it could no longer overcome ΔH. This is the “ΔH+, ΔS+ → spontaneous only at HIGH T” case — temperature cast the deciding vote.

Unit-trap check: if any answer came out in the thousands (like −14,900 kJ for item 6), you forgot to convert ΔS from J/K to kJ/K. Divide ΔS by 1000 and redo.
