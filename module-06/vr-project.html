📚 THE BIG PICTURE
Across this module you've built an Intermolecular Forces & States Model in OptimaXR, one week at a time, and used it to answer a single question: why does matter behave the way it does — why some things melt easily and others don't, why ice floats, why salt dissolves and oil won't, why road salt melts ice? Week 1 you built molecular-scale models of the three intermolecular forces — London dispersion, dipole-dipole, and a hydrogen bond between two water molecules; Week 2 you added a crystal lattice and a dissolution station showing water pulling ions off the lattice; Week 3 you added property-explanation annotations that link each model to something you can actually observe. Now you bring it all together.
Your task is to record a guided video walkthrough inside the headset — a short tour in which you narrate the chemistry of your model, connecting invisible forces between molecules to the visible, measurable properties of liquids, solids, and solutions. This recorded explanation, not a static screenshot, is what earns the grade. The model is the stage; your spoken reasoning is the performance.
⭐ WHY THIS MATTERS
This walkthrough is one of only two teacher-reviewed pieces in Module 6 (the other is your property-argument writing assignment). Your teacher will watch your recording and respond to your reasoning personally — so make it your best thinking, clearly explained.

🎤 WHAT YOUR RECORDING MUST COVER (aim for 3–5 minutes)
1
Open at the entrance. Introduce your model and state the big question it answers: how do the invisible forces between molecules decide the visible properties we can see and measure — melting and boiling, dissolving, floating, freezing?
2
The three intermolecular forces (Week 1). Walk through your models of London dispersion (in all molecules; the only force in nonpolar ones), dipole-dipole (between polar molecules), and hydrogen bonding (your two water molecules — H bonded to O reaching for a neighbor's lone pair). Rank them by strength and say the key rule: stronger intermolecular forces mean a higher boiling and melting point.
3
Solids & the crystal lattice (Week 2). Show your lattice and name what kind of solid it is (ionic, like NaCl). Explain why that structure gives the properties it does — an ionic lattice is hard but brittle, has a high melting point, and conducts only when melted or dissolved (the ions must be free to move).
4
Dissolving & electrolytes (Week 2). At your dissolution station, show water molecules surrounding and pulling ions off the lattice (ion–dipole hydration). Explain “like dissolves like,” and why salt water conducts electricity (a strong electrolyte — full of free ions) while sugar water does not (a nonelectrolyte — whole molecules, no ions).
5
From model to measurable property (Week 3). Read your property annotations and tie at least two of them to numbers: why ice floats (hydrogen bonds hold an open, less-dense lattice), and a colligative example — dissolved particles lower the freezing point, which is why 1.00 m of salt (NaCl) lowers water's freezing point by about 3.72 °C (ΔTf = i·Kf·m = 2 × 1.86 × 1.00), so the road no longer ices at 0 °C.
6
Close by explaining the evidence — then name the limits. Tie each claim back to what your model actually shows: your IMF models are the evidence for the observed macroscopic properties — the open hydrogen-bonded lattice is why ice floats, the ionic lattice is why salt is brittle and high-melting, and the dissolved particles are why salt lowers the freezing point (ΔTf = i·Kf·m). Then state one honest limit — “like dissolves like” and colligative ideality are models that break down for real solutions. End on the through-line: the macroscopic properties we measure are written into the forces between molecules — read the forces and you can predict, and put numbers on, the behavior.
How to record: Use OptimaXR's video recording feature to capture your narrated tour from inside the headset (start the recording from the menu/tablet, walk and talk, then stop and save). If your setup can't capture in-headset audio, record your screen with narration instead. Aim for one clean take; a second take is fine.

📊 HOW THIS IS GRADED (TEACHER RUBRIC — build natively as a Canvas Rubric)
Criterion Full marks Developing Pts
Evidence-based scientific reasoning The walkthrough leads with evidence and reasoning: the three intermolecular forces and their strength order are used to explain the observed properties (why stronger IMFs raise the boiling and melting point); the crystal lattice and the dissolution station are used to justify why an ionic solid is brittle and why salt water conducts while sugar water does not; and the model→property links are argued from the molecular models to the macroscopic observations (an open hydrogen-bonded lattice → ice floats; dissolved particles → freezing-point depression, ΔTf = i·Kf·m). Every claim is tied back to the molecular evidence the model shows. States conclusions without evidence; names forces and properties without explaining why the structure produces the property. 35
Accurate chemistry The chemistry is correct — the three IMFs (London dispersion in all molecules, dipole-dipole, hydrogen bonding requiring H bonded to N, O, or F), the ionic crystal-lattice solid type and its properties, ion–dipole hydration and the strong-vs-nonelectrolyte distinction, and the colligative calculation (1.00 m NaCl → ΔTf = 2 × 1.86 × 1.00 = 3.72 °C) — and the demonstrated values are right. Contains chemistry errors; IMFs mislabeled or demonstrated values wrong. 25
Clear narration & model The narrated tour is organized and easy to follow, and the build (the three IMF models, the crystal lattice, the dissolution station, the property annotations) is recognizable and correct at a high-school level; audio and visuals are usable. Disorganized or hard to follow; models confused, missing, or don't show the chemistry; key parts inaudible. 20
Week-by-week iteration The build clearly shows all three stages (IMF models, lattice + dissolution station, property annotations), and the comment lists the three weekly builds. Only some stages present; little sign of building over time. 12
Uncertainty & limits reflection Names an honest limit — “like dissolves like” and colligative ideality are models that break down (real solutions deviate; a strong electrolyte's measured van’t Hoff factor runs slightly below the ideal whole number because of ion pairing) — showing the honesty of the quantitative. No acknowledgment of the model's limits or of real-solution deviations. 8
