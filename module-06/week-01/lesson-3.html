📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A puddle on the sidewalk slowly disappears on a cool day, even though it never gets anywhere near 100 °C. Where does the water go — and why doesn't it have to boil to leave?
You now know what holds a liquid's molecules together. This lesson is about what happens when energy and pressure push a substance from one state to another. We'll name the six possible phase transitions and track the energy each one absorbs or releases, follow a heating curve as it climbs and pauses, pin down what boiling really is, and finally learn to read a phase diagram — the single map that shows which state a substance is in at any temperature and pressure. By the end you'll be able to look at a point on that map and say at once whether you're looking at a solid, a liquid, or a gas.

📚 PART 1 · THE SIX PHASE TRANSITIONS & THE HEATING CURVE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
A shallow puddle dries up over an afternoon even though it never gets hot enough to boil. Where do you think the water goes, and how does it leave without boiling? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

Matter can move among the three states — solid, liquid, gas — in six ways, and they come in three pairs of opposites:

Transition Change Energy
Melting (fusion)solid → liquidabsorbs (endothermic)
Freezingliquid → solidreleases (exothermic)
Vaporizationliquid → gasabsorbs (endothermic)
Condensationgas → liquidreleases (exothermic)
Sublimationsolid → gasabsorbs (endothermic)
Depositiongas → solidreleases (exothermic)

The pattern is clean: the three transitions that move toward more freedom (melting, vaporization, sublimation) absorb energy to break intermolecular forces; their reverses release it. The energy to melt a solid is its heat of fusion; the energy to boil a liquid is its heat of vaporization. The heat of vaporization is always the larger of the two, because vaporizing pulls molecules completely apart, while melting only loosens them.

This shows up vividly in a heating curve — a plot of temperature versus heat added. As you heat a single phase (say, ice), the temperature climbs steadily. But the moment a phase change begins, the curve goes flat — it plateaus. During that plateau the added energy is being spent breaking intermolecular forces, not speeding up the molecules, so the temperature holds constant until the change is complete. Then it climbs again. Those flat steps are the visual signature of melting and boiling.

▶  Think first, then check: You keep adding heat to a beaker of boiling water, yet a thermometer in it reads a steady 100 °C. Where is all that energy going?
Into breaking the intermolecular forces that hold the liquid together — pulling water molecules apart into vapor. During a phase change, added energy goes into separating molecules rather than raising their kinetic energy, so the temperature stays flat (the plateau on a heating curve) until all the liquid has become gas. Only then will the temperature climb again.
📚 PART 2 · VAPOR PRESSURE & WHAT BOILING REALLY IS

Recall the puddle from the start of the lesson. Even far below the boiling point, the fastest-moving molecules at a liquid's surface can break free into the gas phase — that is evaporation. In a closed container, escaping molecules build up above the liquid until just as many return as leave; the pressure of that vapor is the liquid's vapor pressure. Vapor pressure rises with temperature, because hotter molecules escape more readily.

Now the key definition: boiling happens when a liquid's vapor pressure rises to equal the external (atmospheric) pressure pushing down on it. At that point vapor bubbles can form throughout the liquid, not just at the surface, and it boils. This is why the boiling point depends on pressure. High on a mountain, the atmosphere pushes down less (you measured exactly this with the barometer in Module 5), so the vapor pressure reaches the lower external pressure sooner — and water boils below 100 °C. That's why mountain cooking takes longer: the water is genuinely cooler even though it's boiling.

The definition to keep: a liquid boils when its vapor pressure = the external pressure.
Lower the external pressure (altitude) → the liquid boils at a lower temperature.

📚 PART 3 · READING A PHASE DIAGRAM

A phase diagram gathers all of this into one map. It plots pressure (P) on the vertical axis against temperature (T) on the horizontal axis, and divides the plane into three regions — solid, liquid, and gas. The lines between regions mark the conditions where two phases coexist in equilibrium: the fusion (melting) curve between solid and liquid, the vaporization curve between liquid and gas, and the sublimation curve between solid and gas. Two special points stand out: the triple point, where all three lines meet and solid, liquid, and gas coexist at once; and the critical point, beyond which liquid and gas are no longer distinct — the substance becomes a single “supercritical fluid.” Study the map below before the questions.

A generic phase diagram. Blue lines are the boundaries where two phases coexist; the red dot is the triple point, the gold dot the critical point. (Not drawn to scale for any one substance.)

To use the map, just locate a point by its pressure and temperature and read off the region it lands in. High pressure and low temperature (upper-left) is the solid region; high temperature (right side) is the gas region; the liquid sits between. Move across a boundary line and you cross a phase transition — for instance, moving right along a horizontal line out of the solid region, you cross the fusion curve into the liquid.

Two real diagrams worth knowing

Most substances follow the generic map, but water has a famous twist. Its solid–liquid (fusion) line slopes slightly to the left — a negative slope — rather than to the right. This is the phase-diagram fingerprint of the same oddity from Lesson 6.02: because ice is less dense than liquid water, squeezing ice harder can actually melt it. Water's triple point sits at 0.01 °C and 0.006 atm, and its critical point at 374 °C and 218 atm.

Contrast that with carbon dioxide. Its triple point is at 5.1 atm and −56.6 °C — above ordinary atmospheric pressure. The consequence is everyday: at the normal 1 atm of the open air, solid CO2 (dry ice) never passes through a liquid stage. Instead it sublimes straight to gas, which is why dry ice gives off a cold fog and leaves no puddle behind.

💡 THEN · WHERE THE CRITICAL POINT CAME FROM
The tidy map you just read was won by patient measurement. In the 1860s the Irish chemist Thomas Andrews set out to answer a stubborn question: could every gas be squeezed into a liquid if you simply pressed hard enough? Working with carbon dioxide, he compressed it at temperature after temperature and watched the boundary between liquid and gas. He found the deciding fact: above a certain temperature — the critical temperature — no pressure, however great, would produce a distinct liquid. Above that point the two states simply merged into one. His 1869 report put the critical point on the map (literally, it anchors the top end of the vaporization curve) and handed later theorists like van der Waals the evidence they needed to explain why. The phase diagram is the descendant of experiments like his.
🤖 AI AS AN INSTRUMENT · NOW
Ask an AI tool to “describe the phase diagram of water” and it will produce a confident paragraph — but AI tools sometimes get the water diagram backwards, describing the fusion line with the usual positive slope instead of water's unusual negative one. Treat the tool like any instrument: read the actual diagram, find the regions and the triple and critical points yourself, and check whether the solid–liquid line leans left (water) or right (most substances). The diagram is the evidence; the AI is a second opinion to verify, not the judge.
🔬 NATURE OF SCIENCE · WHAT A PHASE DIAGRAM DOES vs WHAT EXPLAINS IT
A phase diagram is built from measured transition temperatures and pressures. Kinetic-molecular theory — molecules gaining or losing enough energy to overcome their attractions — is the model that explains those transitions. Water's fusion line even leans the “wrong” way. 💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Matter changes state in six transitions, three pairs of opposites; the changes toward greater freedom absorb energy, their reverses release it. A heating curve plateaus during a phase change because the energy goes into breaking forces, not raising temperature. Boiling is simply the moment a liquid's vapor pressure equals the external pressure — so lower pressure means a lower boiling point. And a phase diagram maps all of it: regions for each state, lines where phases coexist, a triple point where all three meet, and a critical point beyond which liquid and gas merge. Hold onto this: with a phase diagram, the state of any substance becomes a measurable, readable point — and water's leftward-leaning fusion line is the quiet signature of its floating ice.
