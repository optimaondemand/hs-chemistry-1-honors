Week 1 · OptimaXR
Model the Three Forces — Begin Your Intermolecular Forces & States Model
VR Assignment · 20 pts · one headset session (~35–45 min)
🧘 PREFER NOT TO USE A HEADSET?
A full non-VR alternate — “Model the Three Forces (No-Headset Pathway)” — meets the exact same objectives and earns the same credit. Do one pathway, not both.
📚 WHAT YOU'RE DOING
You're starting the project you'll build across all three weeks of this module: an Intermolecular Forces & States Model — a molecular-scale exhibit that you'll narrate on camera at the end of the module. This week you build the foundation: model-scale molecules showing all three intermolecular forces. You'll set up a London dispersion attraction between two nonpolar molecules, a dipole-dipole attraction between two polar molecules, and a hydrogen bond between two water molecules — each clearly labeled, with a note connecting “stronger IMF → higher boiling point.” In later weeks you'll add a crystal lattice and a dissolution station (Week 2), then property-explanation annotations (Week 3) — and finally record the full walkthrough.
🥽 WHAT YOU'LL NEED
▶  A VR headset with the OptimaXR app (or the OptimaXR desktop app).
▶  Your notes from Lessons 6.01–6.02 (the three intermolecular forces; the N/O/F rule for hydrogen bonding).
▶  About 35–45 minutes in one sitting.
📍 HOW TO JOIN
Device: VR headset (or OptimaXR desktop app)
Session type: persistent (your placed objects stay between visits all module)
Session ID: see the pinned OptimaXR Sessions list in this module
Location: States & Solutions Lab
Spawn point: Lab Bench A
When you arrive: a Whiteboard IFX facing the bench shows the three forces — London dispersion < dipole-dipole < hydrogen bonding — and this week's Step 1, beside three empty labeled zones marked LDF, Dipole-Dipole, and Hydrogen Bond. A sticky note at your feet reads “Start here.” You won't have to guess what to do.
📝 WHAT TO DO, IN ORDER
These steps follow the way a scientist actually investigates: Observe → Question → Predict → Investigate → Analyze → Defend → Extend. Don't skip the predict step — committing to a ranking before you build the models is the point.
1
Observe. At the bench, read the Whiteboard IFX and its three empty labeled zones. Recall the puzzle from Lesson 6.01: water boils at 100 °C, but methane — a molecule of similar size — boils at −161 °C. Leave open space along the bench to the right for the crystal lattice and dissolution station (Week 2) and property-explanation annotations (Week 3).
2
Question. On a sticky note, write the question you are investigating: “Why should water need so much more heat to boil than methane, when both are small molecules?” Place it on the bench beside the labeled zones.
3
Predict — before you build anything. On a second sticky note, commit a prediction: rank CH4, HCl, and H2O from lowest to highest boiling point, and name the strongest force you expect in each. Write the actual order down. This is your committed prediction — do not change it after you build the models.
4
Investigate. In the LDF zone, spawn two nonpolar molecules (for example, two CH4 or two Ne atoms) and place them near each other. Add a dashed-line connector and a sticky note: “London dispersion — weakest; present in all substances.”
5
Investigate. In the dipole-dipole zone, spawn two polar molecules (for example, two HCl). Mark the slightly positive (δ+) and slightly negative (δ−) ends, line up the + of one with the − of the other, and label: “Dipole-dipole — between permanent dipoles.”
6
Investigate. In the hydrogen-bond zone, spawn two water molecules (H2O). Draw the hydrogen bond from an H of one molecule to the O (lone pair) of the other, and label: “Hydrogen bond — strongest; H bonded to N, O, or F.”
7
Analyze. Step back and compare your three models. Rank them by strength of attraction and match each to the strength ladder (London dispersion < dipole-dipole < hydrogen bonding). Now check that ranking against the committed prediction from your Predict note — was your order right? Off anywhere? By how much?
8
Defend. Place a signed summary sticky note stating a claim with its evidence and reasoning: “Claim: among CH4, HCl, and H2O, water boils highest. Evidence: only water hydrogen-bonds (H on O); HCl is dipole-dipole; CH4 has only dispersion. Reasoning: stronger IMF → more energy to pull molecules apart → higher boiling point.” Sign your name.
9
Extend. On one last sticky note, predict a wrinkle: could a large nonpolar molecule with many electrons ever out-boil a small polar one — and which force would be doing that work? (Hint: dispersion forces add up.) You'll test ideas like this as the model grows in Weeks 2–3.
💾
Save & capture. Lock your three models and labels so they aren't bumped, save a snapshot named IMF_Wk1_YourName (your project's saved progress for next week), and take one in-headset screenshot showing all three labeled force models and your signed claim note. That screenshot is what you'll submit.
Need to stop partway? Because the Session is persistent, your placed and locked models will be waiting when you return. Save the IMF_Wk1_YourName snapshot before you log out so nothing is lost.
📤 WHAT TO SUBMIT
1. Upload your in-headset screenshot showing the three labeled force models (LDF, dipole-dipole, hydrogen bond) and your “stronger IMF → higher boiling point” note to this Canvas assignment.
2. In the comment box, write a short CER reflection (2–4 sentences): a Claim about which of CH4, HCl, and H2O boils highest, the Evidence (the strongest force acting in each), and your Reasoning (why stronger intermolecular forces mean a higher boiling point). Add one sentence comparing the result to the ranking you committed in your Predict note. (This reflection is used when your work appears in the class gallery walk later this module.)
