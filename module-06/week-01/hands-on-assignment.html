Intermolecular Forces & Boiling-Point Prediction
Assignment · 20 pts · Completion + self-check
📖 WHAT THIS BUILDS ON
From this week's lessons: the three intermolecular forces and boiling-point reasoning (6.01), surface tension / viscosity / capillary action and water's special properties (6.02), and the phase transitions and heating curves (6.03).
This is your week's “doing chemistry” work. Nine items: identify forces, rank boiling points with justification, explain properties from the forces, read a phase transition, and — last — a short data-analysis writing step where you predict in words before you read a boiling-point data table, then interpret the trend.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
Given only a substance's structure, can I reason out the forces between its molecules — and from those forces, predict the physical properties I'd actually measure?
🧮 YOUR TOOLKIT (USE THESE EXACT IDEAS)
The three IMFs (weakest → strongest, comparable size): London dispersion < dipole-dipole < hydrogen bonding. (Full ionic/covalent bonds are stronger still.)
Hydrogen bonding requires: H bonded to N, O, or F.
London dispersion: present in all substances; stronger with more electrons / larger molar mass.
Key rule: stronger forces → higher melting and boiling points.
Cohesion = pull of a liquid for itself · Adhesion = pull of a liquid for a surface.
Heating curve: temperature plateaus during a phase change (energy breaks forces, doesn't raise temperature).

🧪 PART A · IDENTIFY THE INTERMOLECULAR FORCES (problems 1–3)
1. Name the strongest intermolecular force present in each: (a) O2 (nonpolar), (b) NH3 (polar, H bonded to N), (c) HCl (polar, H bonded to Cl).
2. A classmate says water (H2O) and methane (CH4) should have similar boiling points because both are small molecules. Name the strongest force in each, and explain why this reasoning is wrong.
3. Which intermolecular force is present in every one of the substances above — and why is it found in all of them?
▶  Check your thinking — Part A answer key
1. (a) O2 → London dispersion only (nonpolar). (b) NH3 → hydrogen bonding (H on N). (c) HCl → dipole-dipole (polar, but H is on Cl, not N/O/F, so no hydrogen bonding).
2. Water's strongest force is hydrogen bonding; methane's is London dispersion only. Size alone doesn't set boiling point — the type of force does. Water's hydrogen bonds are far stronger, so it takes much more energy to separate water molecules. (Water boils at 100 °C; methane at about −161 °C.)
3. London dispersion forces. They come from the motion of electrons, and every substance has electrons — so dispersion is present in all of them (it is the only force in nonpolar O2, and acts alongside the stronger forces in NH3 and HCl).
🌡️ PART B · RANK THE BOILING POINTS, WITH JUSTIFICATION (problems 4–5)
4. Rank helium (He), methane (CH4), and water (H2O) from lowest to highest boiling point. State the strongest force in each and justify the order.
5. Ethane (CH3CH3) and ethanol (CH3CH2OH) have nearly the same molar mass, yet ethanol boils far higher (about 78 °C) than ethane (about −89 °C). Which boils higher, and what single structural feature explains the huge difference?
▶  Check your thinking — Part B answer key
4. He < CH4 < H2O. Helium — a tiny nonpolar atom with the weakest possible London dispersion — is lowest. Methane is also nonpolar (dispersion only) but larger, with more electrons, so its dispersion forces are stronger — middle. Water hydrogen-bonds, the strongest of the three forces — highest. (You can rank these from the forces alone, with no temperatures.)
5. Ethanol boils higher. The one difference is ethanol's –OH (hydroxyl) group, which lets it form hydrogen bonds; ethane (only C and H) has just weak London dispersion. The functional group sets the physical property through the force it enables.
💧 PART C · EXPLAIN A PROPERTY FROM THE FORCES (problems 6–7)
6. A water strider rests on a pond's surface, and a gently placed paper clip floats on water in a glass. Name the property responsible and explain it in terms of the forces on a surface molecule.
7. Explain, at the molecular level, why ice floats on liquid water — and why that is unusual compared with most substances.
▶  Check your thinking — Part C answer key
6. Surface tension. A molecule deep in the liquid is pulled equally in all directions, but a surface molecule has no neighbors above, so the net pull is inward. This makes the surface behave like a stretched skin that resists being broken — strong enough (thanks to water's hydrogen bonding) to support a light, gently placed object like a strider or a paper clip.
7. When water freezes, its hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in an open, spacious lattice with gaps, so solid ice is less dense than liquid water and floats. This is unusual: most substances pack closer when they freeze, so their solids are denser and sink. The floating ice insulates the water below.
🔥 PART D · READ A PHASE TRANSITION (problem 8)
8. A sample of ice is heated steadily. On the heating curve, the temperature climbs, then goes flat at 0 °C for a while, climbs again, then goes flat at 100 °C. (a) What is happening during each flat plateau? (b) Why does the temperature hold constant even though heat is still being added? (c) Name the two phase transitions occurring (in order), and say whether each absorbs or releases energy.
▶  Check your thinking — Part D worked answer
(a) The first plateau (0 °C) is the ice melting to liquid water; the second plateau (100 °C) is the liquid water boiling (vaporizing) to steam.
(b) During a phase change the added energy goes into breaking intermolecular forces (pulling the molecules apart) rather than raising their kinetic energy, so the temperature stays constant until the change is complete.
(c) In order: melting (fusion), then vaporization — both absorb energy (endothermic).

Check yourself: did you reason from the forces in every item, and name the strongest force present (remembering dispersion is in everything)?
✍️ PART E · DATA-ANALYSIS WRITING: PREDICT IN WORDS, THEN READ THE DATA (problem 9)
9. Below are the measured boiling points of five molecules of similar or increasing size.

(a) Before you look hard at the numbers — in words (no calculating, no sorting yet) — predict which molecule will boil lowest and which will boil highest, and name the one force that decides it. Write one or two sentences.

(b) Now study the data table and write 3–4 sentences analyzing the trend: which molecules rely only on London dispersion, which add dipole-dipole, and which can hydrogen-bond — and describe how the measured boiling points line up (or don't) with the strength order. Point out one place where molar mass (dispersion) and force type both matter.
Substance Formula Boiling point (°C)
methaneCH4−161
ethaneC2H6−89
formaldehydeCH2O−19
methanolCH3OH+65
ethanolC2H5OH+78
▶  Check your thinking — Part E model answer
(a) Prediction (in words): Methane should boil lowest — it is small and nonpolar, so only weak London dispersion acts. Ethanol should boil highest — its –OH group lets it hydrogen-bond, the strongest of the three forces. The deciding force is hydrogen bonding (present in the molecules with an –OH).

(b) Analysis of the data: The table confirms the prediction. Methane (CH4) and ethane (C2H6) are nonpolar, so they feel only London dispersion — and they boil lowest (−161 and −89 °C). Formaldehyde (CH2O) is polar, so it adds dipole-dipole attraction and boils higher (−19 °C). Methanol and ethanol both carry an –OH group, so they hydrogen-bond — and they boil far higher (+65 and +78 °C). The measured points climb exactly with the strength order: dispersion < dipole-dipole < hydrogen bonding.

Where both molar mass AND force type matter: Methane vs. ethane shows dispersion alone — same force type, but ethane is larger (more electrons), so it boils ~70 degrees higher purely from stronger dispersion. Ethanol vs. methanol shows the same idea among the hydrogen-bonders: both hydrogen-bond, but the larger ethanol boils a bit higher. So within one force type, size (dispersion) breaks the tie; across force types, the stronger type wins outright.

Check yourself: did you write your word-prediction in (a) before sorting the numbers, and does your analysis name the force type for each molecule rather than just re-listing the temperatures?
