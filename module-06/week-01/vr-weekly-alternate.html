Model the Three Forces
Assignment · 20 pts · ~35–45 min

📚 WHAT YOU'RE DOING
You'll build an Intermolecular Forces & States Model — a set of molecular diagrams, built up across three weeks, that you'll explain at the end of the module — using a PowerPoint deck or a hand-drawn poster. This week you diagram all three intermolecular forces: a London dispersion attraction between two nonpolar molecules, a dipole-dipole attraction between two polar molecules, and a hydrogen bond between two water molecules — each labeled, with a note connecting “stronger IMF → higher boiling point.” In later weeks you'll add a crystal lattice and a dissolution diagram (Week 2), then property-explanation annotations (Week 3), then record a short narrated walkthrough.
🎨 CHOOSE ONE FORMAT
Option A — Digital (PowerPoint)
Make a 4-slide deck: a title slide, then one slide each for the three forces (LDF between two nonpolar molecules; dipole-dipole between two polar molecules; a hydrogen bond between two water molecules), each labeled, with a summary line tying strength to boiling point.
Option B — Hands-on (poster)
Draw all three force diagrams on paper, each labeled, with the “stronger IMF → higher boiling point” summary underneath, and photograph the poster into one document.
📝 WHAT TO DO, IN ORDER
These steps follow the way a scientist actually investigates: Observe → Question → Predict → Investigate → Analyze → Defend → Extend. Don't skip the predict step — committing to a ranking before you draw the diagrams is the point.
1
Observe. Title your project Intermolecular Forces & States Model and note the strength order: London dispersion < dipole-dipole < hydrogen bonding. Recall the puzzle from Lesson 6.01: water boils at 100 °C, but methane — a molecule of similar size — boils at −161 °C. Leave room — you'll add more exhibits in Weeks 2–3.
2
Question. Write the question you're investigating on your title slide (or the top of the poster): “Why should water need so much more heat to boil than methane, when both are small molecules?”
3
Predict — before you draw anything. Write a committed prediction: rank CH4, HCl, and H2O from lowest to highest boiling point, and name the strongest force you expect in each. Don't change it after you've drawn the diagrams.
4
Investigate. Draw the London dispersion diagram: two nonpolar molecules (e.g., two CH4) near each other with a dashed attraction between them. Label: “weakest; present in all substances.”
5
Investigate. Draw the dipole-dipole diagram: two polar molecules (e.g., two HCl) with the δ+ end of one facing the δ− end of the other. Label: “between permanent dipoles.”
6
Investigate. Draw the hydrogen bond diagram: two water molecules, with a dashed bond from an H of one to the O of the other. Label: “strongest; H bonded to N, O, or F.”
7
Analyze. Compare your three diagrams: rank them by strength of attraction and match each to the strength ladder (London dispersion < dipole-dipole < hydrogen bonding). Check that ranking against the committed prediction you wrote — was your order right? Off anywhere?
8
Defend. Add a signed summary stating a claim with its evidence and reasoning: “Claim: among CH4, HCl, and H2O, water boils highest. Evidence: only water hydrogen-bonds (H on O); HCl is dipole-dipole; CH4 has only dispersion. Reasoning: stronger IMF → more energy to pull molecules apart → higher boiling point.” Sign your name.
9
Extend. Add one last note predicting a wrinkle: could a large nonpolar molecule with many electrons ever out-boil a small polar one — and which force would be doing that work? (Hint: dispersion forces add up.) You'll test ideas like this in Weeks 2–3.
💾
Save your file. Save the finished deck or photo document. Keep it — you'll add the crystal lattice / dissolution diagram and the property-explanation annotations in Weeks 2–3.
