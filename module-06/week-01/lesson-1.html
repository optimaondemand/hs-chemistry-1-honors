📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Water boils at 100 °C, but methane — a molecule of almost the same size — boils at −161 °C. Both are held together by ordinary covalent bonds, so why does one need so much more heat to break free into a gas?
For two quarters you have studied the forces inside molecules — the ionic and covalent bonds that hold atoms together. Those bonds explain a molecule's identity. But they do not, by themselves, explain whether a substance is a gas, a liquid, or a solid at room temperature; why some liquids are syrupy and others runny; or why water beads on a waxed car. Those everyday, measurable physical properties come from a second, much weaker set of attractions — the forces between molecules, called intermolecular forces. This lesson sorts them into three kinds, ranks their strengths, and uses them to predict the single most diagnostic property of all: the boiling point. By the end you'll be able to look at a molecule and reason out how strongly it clings to its neighbors.

2

3

📚 PART 1 · FORCES BETWEEN MOLECULES, NOT WITHIN THEM
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
Splash water onto a freshly waxed car and it pulls into tight round beads; splash the same water onto a clean glass window and it spreads into a flat sheet. Same water both times — so what must be different about how strongly the water is pulled? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

It helps to be careful with two words that look almost identical. The bonds you studied in Module 3 — ionic and covalent — are intramolecular forces: they act within a molecule, holding its atoms together and giving it its chemical identity. The attractions we meet today are intermolecular forces (IMFs): they act between separate molecules. The prefixes carry the whole distinction — intra means "inside," inter means "between."

Intermolecular forces are far weaker than the bonds inside a molecule — often less than a tenth as strong. Yet they are the forces that decide a substance's physical properties: whether it is a solid, liquid, or gas at room temperature; how high its melting and boiling points are; how thick it is to pour; whether its surface beads or spreads. To melt or boil a substance you do not break its chemical bonds — you only have to pull its molecules apart from one another, overcoming the IMFs that hold them close. The stronger those forces, the more energy it takes, and the higher the melting and boiling points climb.

THE CENTRAL IDEA
Stronger intermolecular forces → higher melting and boiling points
it takes more energy to pull molecules apart when they cling more tightly
▶  Think first, then check: When liquid water boils into steam, are the O–H bonds inside each water molecule breaking?
No. Boiling pulls whole water molecules away from one another — it overcomes the intermolecular forces between molecules. The strong covalent O–H bonds inside each molecule stay intact; the steam is still made of H2O molecules. That is the heart of the intra- vs. inter- distinction: melting and boiling are about forces between molecules, not the bonds within them.
📚 PART 2 · THE THREE KINDS OF INTERMOLECULAR FORCE

There are three intermolecular forces to know, from weakest to strongest. The trick to telling them apart is to ask two questions about a molecule: Does it have a permanent positive-and-negative side (is it polar)? and Does it have a hydrogen atom bonded to nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine?

1. London dispersion forces — present in everything

Even a molecule with no permanent charge separation has electrons in constant motion. At any instant those electrons can pile up slightly on one side, creating a fleeting instantaneous dipole. That momentary lopsidedness tugs on a neighbor's electron cloud and induces a matching dipole, and the two briefly attract. These flickering attractions are London dispersion forces, and because every substance has electrons, every substance has them — they are the only intermolecular force acting in nonpolar substances such as the noble gases, N2, O2, methane (CH4), and the hydrocarbons. They grow stronger as a molecule has more electrons (larger molar mass) and more surface area, because a bigger, softer electron cloud is easier to distort.

2. Dipole-dipole forces — between polar molecules

A polar molecule has a permanent dipole — a slightly positive end and a slightly negative end — because of an uneven sharing of electrons (you predicted these from shape and electronegativity back in Module 3). When many polar molecules crowd together, the positive end of one is attracted to the negative end of the next. These dipole-dipole forces are present in polar substances such as hydrogen chloride (HCl) and acetone, and for molecules of similar size they are stronger than dispersion forces alone.

3. Hydrogen bonding — the strong special case

When a hydrogen atom is bonded to one of the three small, highly electronegative atoms — nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine — that hydrogen is left very exposed and strongly positive, and it is powerfully attracted to a lone pair on the N, O, or F of a neighboring molecule. This especially strong dipole-dipole attraction is called hydrogen bonding. Why only N, O, and F? They are small and pull electrons hard, so the bonded hydrogen is stripped unusually bare; a hydrogen bonded to a larger, less greedy atom (like carbon) simply isn't exposed enough. Hydrogen bonding is why water, hydrogen fluoride (HF), and ammonia (NH3) boil at surprisingly high temperatures compared with heavier molecules that can't hydrogen-bond.

The strength ladder (for molecules of comparable size):
ionic & covalent BONDS  »»  hydrogen bonding  >  dipole-dipole  >  London dispersion.
One caution: dispersion forces add up. In a very large molecule, many dispersion contacts can outweigh a single dipole — size can override type.
💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY
In 1873, the Dutch physicist Johannes Diderik van der Waals proposed in his doctoral thesis that real gases stray from ideal behavior for two reasons: their molecules take up actual space, and — the radical part — they attract one another. This was the first quantitative account of an attractive force between separate molecules, at a time when many doubted such forces existed at all. (You met the consequence last module, when real gases deviated from the ideal gas law.) His work earned the 1910 Nobel Prize in Physics, and to this day the family of weak attractions between molecules is collectively called the van der Waals forces in his honor.
⚡ FLIP CARDS · NAME THE DOMINANT FORCE
Predict the strongest intermolecular force at work in each substance, then tap to flip and check.
🍲 CH4 (methane) — tap to reveal
London dispersion only. Methane is nonpolar, so dispersion is its sole IMF — and a weak one, which is why methane is a gas down to −161 °C.
🧊 HCl — tap to reveal
Dipole-dipole (plus dispersion). HCl is polar but its H is bonded to chlorine, not N/O/F, so it does not hydrogen-bond.
💧 H2O (water) — tap to reveal
Hydrogen bonding. H bonded to O → strong hydrogen bonds, which is why water is a liquid at room temperature and boils all the way up at 100 °C.
⚛️ Ne (neon) — tap to reveal
London dispersion only. A lone noble-gas atom has no dipole, so only fleeting dispersion forces act — very weak, so neon is a gas except at extreme cold.
📚 PART 3 · READING BOILING POINTS FROM THE FORCES

Here is the payoff. Because boiling means pulling molecules apart, the boiling point is a direct readout of intermolecular-force strength: the stronger the forces, the more heat is needed, the higher the boiling point. This lets you predict and rank boiling points without memorizing them — you reason from structure.

A worked comparison

Rank these three by boiling point, lowest to highest: methane (CH4), methanol (CH3OH), and sodium chloride (NaCl).

Reason it out:
• CH4 is nonpolar — only weak London dispersion. Lowest. (boils at −161 °C)
• CH3OH has an –OH group, so it hydrogen-bonds — much stronger. Middle. (boils at 65 °C)
• NaCl is held by full ionic bonds, not mere IMFs — far stronger than any intermolecular force. Highest. (boils at 1413 °C)

Order: CH4 < CH3OH < NaCl. The huge jumps between them mirror the strength ladder exactly.
🔗 CONNECT IT · FUNCTIONAL GROUPS SET PHYSICAL PROPERTIES
Remember the –OH (hydroxyl) group from organic chemistry in Module 3? Now you can see what it does. Ethanol (CH3CH2OH) and ethane (CH3CH3) have almost the same molar mass, yet ethanol boils at about 78 °C while ethane boils at about −89 °C — a difference of more than 160 degrees. The only structural change is ethanol's –OH, which lets it hydrogen-bond. An organic functional group, in other words, sets a substance's physical properties through the intermolecular forces it enables.

⚡ TRY IT · TRUE OR FALSE ABOUT INTERMOLECULAR FORCES
Decide whether each statement is true or false. You'll get instant feedback on each.
1. London dispersion forces are found only in polar molecules.
2. Hydrogen bonding occurs only when a hydrogen atom is bonded to N, O, or F.
3. A substance with stronger intermolecular forces will, in general, have a higher boiling point.
4. HCl forms hydrogen bonds because it contains a hydrogen atom.
▶  Think first, then check: Rank helium (He), methane (CH4), and water (H2O) from lowest to highest boiling point, and say why.
He < CH4 < H2O. Helium is a tiny nonpolar atom with the weakest possible dispersion forces — lowest. Methane is also nonpolar (dispersion only) but larger, with more electrons, so its dispersion forces are stronger — middle. Water hydrogen-bonds, the strongest of the three intermolecular forces — highest. Notice you never needed the actual temperatures: you ranked them purely from the forces.
⭐ MODERN RESEARCH · THEN → NOW → FUTURE
Then. In 1873 van der Waals argued that molecules attract one another at all — a faint force many chemists doubted was real.
Now. Those “weak” forces are engineered on purpose. Researchers traced a gecko's ability to walk up smooth glass to millions of tiny van der Waals contacts on the hairs of its toes, and are designing gecko-inspired adhesives from that insight; materials scientists and nanotechnologists tune intermolecular forces to make self-assembling materials and molecular machines that snap together on their own.
Future. An open question drives the field: can we design a material's bulk behavior — its strength, stickiness, or the way it flows — by programming the weak forces between its molecules? Predicting and controlling intermolecular forces in crowded, complex systems is still hard, and it is where a lot of today's materials research lives.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

The bonds inside a molecule give it its identity; the forces between molecules give it its physical properties. There are three, weakest to strongest: London dispersion (in everything), dipole-dipole (in polar molecules), and hydrogen bonding (the strong special case, requiring H bonded to N, O, or F). Stronger forces mean higher melting and boiling points — so you can rank a set of substances by reasoning from structure alone. Hold onto this: nearly every physical property you'll measure in this module — surface tension, melting point, solubility, the very state a substance is in — traces back to the strength of these intermolecular forces.
