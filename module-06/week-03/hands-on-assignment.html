📖 WHAT THIS BUILDS ON
From this week's lessons: the two colligative equations — ΔTf = i · Kf · m and ΔTb = i · Kb · m (6.07) — and the van 't Hoff factor i (sugar 1, NaCl 2, CaCl₂ 3). Here you'll run them across several solutions in Excel, where the spreadsheet does the repeating arithmetic so you can watch how the freezing- and boiling-point shifts grow with molality and with the particle count.
This is your week's “doing chemistry” work, and it rehearses the colligative reasoning your module property-argument writing assignment can draw on.
📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
In Excel, you'll build a table that computes the freezing-point depression (ΔTf) and boiling-point elevation (ΔTb) for six water solutions that vary in both molality and van 't Hoff factor i. You'll then find each solution's new freezing point and boiling point, graph ΔTf versus molality, and answer a few questions about the pattern you see. The spreadsheet handles the repeated multiplication — your job is to set up the formulas correctly and read what the numbers reveal.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
Two things change how far a freezing point drops — how concentrated the solution is, and how many particles each solute releases. Which one can you control by choosing a different salt, and what does the graph reveal about the other?
✏️ EXPLAIN YOUR REASONING FIRST — BEFORE YOU CALCULATE
Before you build a single formula, write two or three sentences in your notebook (or a text box in the sheet): looking only at the six solutions in the table, which one do you predict will have the lowest freezing point, and why? Reason in words from ΔTf = i · Kf · m — talk about both the molality and the van 't Hoff factor — before the spreadsheet does any arithmetic. You'll test your prediction against your computed numbers at the end.
▶  Check your reasoning (open only after you've committed a prediction)
The lowest freezing point should be Solution 4 (2.00 m NaCl). Its particle molality i · m = 2 × 2.00 = 4.0 is the highest in the table — higher even than Solution 6 (CaCl₂, i · m = 3 × 1.00 = 3.0), because the large molality outweighs CaCl₂'s larger i. So ΔTf is largest for Solution 4 (7.44 °C, freezing at −7.44 °C). If you predicted CaCl₂, that's a reasonable instinct — it shows exactly why you must compare i · m, not i alone.
🔢 CONSTANTS & REFERENCE YOU'LL NEED
Kf(water) = 1.86 °C/m  ·  Kb(water) = 0.512 °C/m
New freezing point = 0 °C − ΔTf  ·  New boiling point = 100 °C + ΔTb
van 't Hoff factor i: sugar/glucose = 1, NaCl = 2, CaCl₂ = 3.

📝 WHAT TO DO IN EXCEL
1
Enter the six-row table with headers in row 1: Solution, Solute, Molality m, i. Put your two constants in cells off to the side, e.g. Kf = 1.86 and Kb = 0.512.
2
Add a ΔTf (°C) column: i × Kf × m, e.g. =D2*$G$1*C2 (point at your i, your Kf constant with $ locks, and your molality). Fill down all six rows.
3
Add a ΔTb (°C) column: i × Kb × m, e.g. =D2*$G$2*C2. Fill down all six rows.
4
Add a Freezing point (°C) column: =0-E2 (zero minus your ΔTf). Add a Boiling point (°C) column: =100+F2.
5
Graph ΔTf vs molality. Select your Molality column and your ΔTf column, insert a Scatter (XY) chart, and title it “Freezing-point depression vs molality.” Label the x-axis molality (mol/kg) and the y-axis ΔTf (°C).
6
In cells or a text box below the table, answer the three Analysis Questions below.
❓ ANALYSIS QUESTIONS (answer in your spreadsheet)
A. Compare Solution 3 (1.00 m NaCl) and Solution 6 (1.00 m CaCl₂). They have the same molality, yet different ΔTf values. Which one depresses the freezing point more, and which factor in ΔTf = i · Kf · m explains the difference?
B. Look at the two NaCl rows (Solutions 3 and 4). When you doubled the molality from 1.00 m to 2.00 m, what happened to ΔTf? What does that tell you about the shape of your ΔTf-vs-molality graph (for a fixed solute)?
C. Every freezing point you computed is below 0 °C and every boiling point is above 100 °C. In one or two sentences, explain in molecular terms why dissolving any of these solutes pushes the freezing point down and the boiling point up.
D. (Data-analysis write-up.) Write a short paragraph — three or four sentences — interpreting your ΔTf-vs-molality graph in words. Describe the shape of the line, say what its slope represents in the equation ΔTf = i · Kf · m, and explain how a line drawn for CaCl₂ would compare to the line for NaCl. Finally, state whether the numbers confirmed the prediction you wrote at the start.
▶  Check your work — answer key
Computed results (Kf = 1.86, Kb = 0.512):
• Sol 1 — sugar, 0.50 m, i = 1: ΔTf = 1 × 1.86 × 0.50 = 0.93 °C (FP −0.93 °C); ΔTb = 1 × 0.512 × 0.50 = 0.256 °C (BP 100.256 °C).
• Sol 2 — glucose, 1.00 m, i = 1: ΔTf = 1 × 1.86 × 1.00 = 1.86 °C (FP −1.86 °C); ΔTb = 1 × 0.512 × 1.00 = 0.512 °C (BP 100.512 °C).
• Sol 3 — NaCl, 1.00 m, i = 2: ΔTf = 2 × 1.86 × 1.00 = 3.72 °C (FP −3.72 °C); ΔTb = 2 × 0.512 × 1.00 = 1.024 °C (BP 101.024 °C).
• Sol 4 — NaCl, 2.00 m, i = 2: ΔTf = 2 × 1.86 × 2.00 = 7.44 °C (FP −7.44 °C); ΔTb = 2 × 0.512 × 2.00 = 2.048 °C (BP 102.048 °C).
• Sol 5 — CaCl₂, 0.50 m, i = 3: ΔTf = 3 × 1.86 × 0.50 = 2.79 °C (FP −2.79 °C); ΔTb = 3 × 0.512 × 0.50 = 0.768 °C (BP 100.768 °C).
• Sol 6 — CaCl₂, 1.00 m, i = 3: ΔTf = 3 × 1.86 × 1.00 = 5.58 °C (FP −5.58 °C); ΔTb = 3 × 0.512 × 1.00 = 1.536 °C (BP 101.536 °C).

A. CaCl₂ (Solution 6, ΔTf = 5.58 °C) depresses the freezing point more than NaCl (Solution 3, ΔTf = 3.72 °C), even though both are 1.00 m. The difference is the van 't Hoff factor i: CaCl₂ releases 3 particles per formula unit (i = 3) versus NaCl's 2 (i = 2), and colligative properties count particles. The ratio is exactly 3/2: 5.58 / 3.72 = 1.5.
B. Doubling the molality (1.00 m → 2.00 m) doubled ΔTf (3.72 °C → 7.44 °C). For a fixed solute, ΔTf is directly proportional to molality, so the graph of ΔTf vs molality is a straight line through the origin — its slope is i · Kf.
C. Dissolved particles get in the way of the solvent molecules organizing into a solid lattice, so the solution must be cooled below 0 °C before it can freeze (freezing-point depression). The same particles lower the solvent's vapor pressure, so the solution must be heated above 100 °C before its vapor pressure matches the surrounding air and it boils (boiling-point elevation).

D. The ΔTf-vs-molality graph is a straight line through the origin — for a fixed solute, ΔTf is directly proportional to molality. The line's slope is i · Kf: for NaCl the slope is 2 × 1.86 = 3.72 °C per unit molality, and a CaCl₂ line would be steeper (slope 3 × 1.86 = 5.58) because its larger van 't Hoff factor raises the slope. The computed numbers confirm the opening prediction: the lowest freezing point belongs to 2.00 m NaCl (ΔTf = 7.44 °C), because its particle molality i · m = 4.0 beats every other row.
