📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Stir a spoonful of sugar into iced tea and it vanishes. Add a second, a third, a tenth — and at some point the sugar simply stops dissolving and settles to the bottom. What sets that limit, and why does the same tea swallow far more sugar when it's hot?
Last lesson you saw why things dissolve — the molecular tug-of-war of “like dissolves like” and the hydration of ions. Now we ask how much. Dissolving is not unlimited: every solvent has a ceiling for every solute, and that ceiling is a measurable number called solubility. This lesson defines the saturation states a solution can be in, teaches you to read a solubility curve — the graph that turns “how much dissolves” into something you can look up — and shows how temperature and pressure shift the ceiling for solids and for gases. By the end you'll be able to look at a point on a solubility curve and say whether a solution is hungry for more solute, exactly full, or dangerously overfull.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define solubility and distinguish an unsaturated, saturated, and supersaturated solution.
2
I will be able to read a solubility curve to find how much solute dissolves at a given temperature and to classify a point as unsaturated, saturated, or supersaturated.
3
I will be able to explain how temperature and pressure affect the solubility of solids and of gases (including Henry's law for gases).

📚 PART 1 · HOW MUCH WILL DISSOLVE? SATURATION
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
Heat a pot of water on the stove and, long before it ever boils, small bubbles cling to the bottom and sides. The water isn't boiling yet — so what are those early bubbles? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

You can dissolve a great deal of sugar in a glass of water — but not an unlimited amount. Keep adding, and you reach a point where no more will dissolve and the extra sits undissolved at the bottom. That ceiling is the heart of today's lesson. Solubility is the maximum amount of a solute that dissolves in a given amount of solvent at a given temperature — usually reported as grams of solute per 100 grams of water. It is a measurable property of a particular solute–solvent pair, and it depends strongly on temperature.

Depending on how much solute is dissolved relative to that ceiling, a solution is in one of three states:

Unsaturated — less solute is dissolved than the ceiling allows. More can still dissolve.
Saturated — exactly at the ceiling. Dissolved and undissolved solute sit in a dynamic balance (dissolving ⇌ crystallizing at equal rates). Add more and it just piles up at the bottom.
Supersaturated — more than the ceiling is dissolved. This is an unstable, “overfull” state usually made by dissolving a lot of solute in hot solvent and cooling it carefully. One disturbance — a dust speck or a tiny seed crystal — triggers the excess to crystallize out all at once.

Supersaturation is not just a curiosity. A chemical hand-warmer is a sealed pouch of supersaturated sodium acetate; click the little metal disk inside and crystallization races through the pouch, releasing heat. Rock candy grows the same way: a hot, nearly supersaturated sugar solution is cooled, and over days the excess sugar crystallizes onto a string. The unstable, overfull solution is exactly what makes both work.

▶  Think first, then check: You stir 5 g of salt into water and it all dissolves, with no salt left at the bottom and room for more. Which of the three states is this solution in?
Unsaturated. All the solute dissolved and more could still dissolve, so the solution is below its ceiling. It would become saturated only when you reach the point where the next pinch of salt refuses to dissolve and settles out.
📚 PART 2 · READING A SOLUBILITY CURVE

Because solubility depends on temperature, chemists summarize it in a graph called a solubility curve: solubility (grams of solute per 100 g of water) on the vertical axis, temperature on the horizontal axis. Each solute gets its own curve. The curve is the saturation ceiling at every temperature — and that makes it a map of all three states at once.

HOW TO READ A POINT ON A SOLUBILITY CURVE
ON the line = saturated · BELOW the line = unsaturated · ABOVE the line = supersaturated
find your temperature on the x-axis, go straight up, and compare your amount of solute to the curve's height there

Most solids become more soluble as temperature rises — their curves climb. Potassium nitrate (KNO₃), for example, rises steeply: cold water holds little, but hot water dissolves a great deal. A few solutes are nearly flat: table salt (NaCl) is only slightly more soluble in hot water than in cold, so its curve is almost level. This is why you can dissolve so much sugar in hot tea, then watch crystals form as it cools — the cooling solution's ceiling has dropped below what's dissolved.

Worked once: reading KNO₃

Suppose KNO₃'s curve passes through 32 g per 100 g water at 20 °C and 110 g per 100 g water at 60 °C.

• A beaker holding 20 g of KNO₃ in 100 g water at 20 °C → 20 is below 32, so the point is below the line: unsaturated (more can dissolve).
• A beaker holding 32 g in 100 g water at 20 °C → right on the line: saturated.
• Cool a saturated 60 °C solution (110 g) down to 20 °C without disturbing it, and 110 g is now far above the 32 g ceiling: supersaturated, and a seed crystal would dump out about 110 − 32 = 78 g of solid.

📚 PART 3 · GASES: TEMPERATURE AND PRESSURE WORK DIFFERENTLY

Dissolved gases behave almost backwards from solids, and two everyday observations tell the whole story.

Temperature. Unlike most solids, a gas becomes less soluble as temperature rises. A glass of soda left to warm goes flat because the warming liquid can no longer hold its dissolved carbon dioxide. The same effect matters in nature: warm water holds less dissolved oxygen than cold water, so when a power plant or factory releases warm water into a river — thermal pollution — the oxygen level can drop low enough to harm fish. (This is also why those early bubbles appeared in the heating pot in Part 1.)

Pressure. A gas becomes more soluble as the pressure of that gas above the liquid increases. This is Henry's law: the solubility of a gas is proportional to the partial pressure of that gas above the solution. A sealed can of soda is carbonated under high CO₂ pressure, which forces a great deal of CO₂ into solution. Crack the can open, the pressure drops to ordinary atmospheric pressure, the gas is suddenly far above its new, lower ceiling — and it fizzes out.

THE TWO RULES FOR DISSOLVED GAS
Higher temperature → less gas dissolves  ·  Higher pressure → more gas dissolves
warm soda goes flat · sealed (pressurized) soda stays fizzy
📚 HOW WE FOUND OUT · THE PRESSURE PUZZLE
Why should pressing a gas harder against a liquid change how much of it dissolves? In the early 1800s the English chemist William Henry set out to measure the effect rather than guess at it. He sealed a measured amount of water beneath a gas, varied the pressure above it, and recorded how much gas the water took up each time. The numbers settled the question: the amount dissolved rose in direct step with the pressure of the gas above the liquid — roughly double the pressure, double the dissolved gas. That measured regularity is what we now call Henry's law. Notice what decided it — not a clever argument, but a table of measurements that came out the same way every time the experiment was repeated. It is exactly why a soda can is charged with CO₂ under high pressure, and why it fizzes the moment that pressure is released.
🧠 NATURE OF SCIENCE · A LAW DESCRIBES; A MODEL EXPLAINS
Henry's law describes what happens — dissolved gas is proportional to pressure — but it never says why. The molecular model supplies the "why": gas molecules are always entering and leaving the liquid's surface, and raising the pressure above the liquid pushes more molecules in than escape, until a new balance is reached. Keep the two ideas separate, because the model has limits. The clean proportionality holds for a gas that simply dissolves, but it bends for a gas like CO₂ that partly reacts with water, and it weakens at very high pressures. In your notebook: a law that fits the data and a model that explains the data are different kinds of knowledge — if a gas someday disobeyed Henry's proportionality, which one would have to change, and how would you tell?
💡 NOW · WHERE THIS IS HEADED
Solubility sits at the center of one of this century's hardest practical problems: turning seawater and wastewater into fresh drinking water. The dominant method today is reverse osmosis, which pushes water under high pressure through a membrane whose pores let water molecules through but block the dissolved salt ions. An older approach, distillation, simply boils the water and recondenses the pure vapor, leaving the salts behind. As fresh water grows scarce in many regions, chemists and engineers are currently working to design better membranes and cut the large amount of energy desalination still demands — so that pulling salt out of water becomes cheap enough to use widely.
▶  Think first, then check: A bottle of soda goes flat fastest when it is left open AND warm. Using both gas rules, explain why those two conditions together are the worst case.
Opening the bottle drops the CO₂ pressure above the liquid to ordinary atmospheric pressure, and by Henry's law lower pressure means a lower ceiling for dissolved gas — so CO₂ escapes. Letting it warm also lowers the ceiling, because gas solubility falls as temperature rises. Both changes push the dissolved CO₂ above its new, lower limit, so an open warm soda loses its fizz fastest of all.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Dissolving has a ceiling, and that ceiling is a measurable number — solubility — that a solubility curve maps at every temperature. A solution sits below the curve (unsaturated), on it (saturated), or above it (supersaturated, and unstable). Most solids dissolve more in hot water; gases do the opposite, dissolving less as temperature rises and more under pressure (Henry's law). Hold onto this: solubility turns “will it dissolve?” into “exactly how much?” — another place where chemistry becomes something you can measure and predict.
