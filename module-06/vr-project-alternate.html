✔️ THIS IS THE ALTERNATE
This pathway earns the same credit, meets the same objectives, and is graded on the same rubric as the OptimaXR version. Complete this OR the VR project — not both.
📚 THE BIG PICTURE
Across this module you've assembled an Intermolecular Forces & States Model — on slides or as physical models — one week at a time: Week 1 the three intermolecular forces (London dispersion, dipole-dipole, and a hydrogen bond between two water molecules); Week 2 a crystal lattice and a dissolution station showing water pulling ions off the lattice; Week 3 property-explanation annotations linking the models to observable behavior.
Now you record a narrated walkthrough of your model. The graded work is your spoken explanation of the chemistry, not the artwork — the model is the stage; your reasoning is the performance.
⭐ WHY THIS MATTERS
This walkthrough is one of only two teacher-reviewed pieces in Module 6 (the other is your property-argument writing assignment). Your teacher will watch your recording and respond to your reasoning personally.
🎨 CHOOSE ONE FORMAT
Option A — Digital (screen-recorded slides)
Use your three weekly PowerPoint builds as the visual. Record your screen while you narrate a tour: the three IMF models, the crystal lattice + dissolution station, and the property annotations.
Option B — Hands-on (physical models)
Use balls-and-sticks, gumdrops/toothpicks, or drawings for the molecules and lattice; a cup of salt water (and one of sugar water) for the dissolution/electrolyte point; ice floating in water for the density point. Record a video narrating the same tour.
🎤 WHAT YOUR RECORDING MUST COVER (aim for 3–5 minutes)
1
Open with the big question: how do the invisible forces between molecules decide the visible properties we can see and measure — melting and boiling, dissolving, floating, freezing?
2
The three intermolecular forces (Week 1). Walk through London dispersion (in all molecules), dipole-dipole (polar molecules), and hydrogen bonding (your two water molecules). Rank them by strength and state the rule: stronger intermolecular forces mean a higher boiling and melting point.
3
Solids & the crystal lattice (Week 2). Show your lattice, name the solid type (ionic), and explain why that structure is hard but brittle, high-melting, and conducts only when melted or dissolved.
4
Dissolving & electrolytes (Week 2). Show water surrounding and pulling ions off the lattice (ion–dipole hydration). Explain “like dissolves like,” and why salt water conducts (a strong electrolyte) while sugar water does not (a nonelectrolyte).
5
From model to measurable property (Week 3). Tie at least two annotations to numbers: why ice floats (hydrogen bonds hold an open, less-dense lattice), and a colligative example — 1.00 m of salt (NaCl) lowers water's freezing point by about 3.72 °C (ΔTf = i·Kf·m = 2 × 1.86 × 1.00), which is why salt melts ice on winter roads.
6
Close by explaining the evidence — then name the limits. Tie each claim back to what your model actually shows: your IMF models are the evidence for the observed macroscopic properties — the open hydrogen-bonded lattice is why ice floats, the ionic lattice is why salt is brittle and high-melting, and the dissolved particles are why salt lowers the freezing point (ΔTf = i·Kf·m). Then state one honest limit — “like dissolves like” and colligative ideality are models that break down for real solutions. End on the through-line: the macroscopic properties we measure are written into the forces between molecules — read the forces and you can predict, and put numbers on, the behavior.

📊 HOW THIS IS GRADED (SAME RUBRIC AS THE VR PROJECT — build natively as a Canvas Rubric)
Criterion Full marks Developing Pts
Evidence-based scientific reasoning The walkthrough leads with evidence and reasoning: the three intermolecular forces and their strength order are used to explain the observed properties (why stronger IMFs raise the boiling and melting point); the crystal lattice and the dissolution station are used to justify why an ionic solid is brittle and why salt water conducts while sugar water does not; and the model→property links are argued from the molecular models to the macroscopic observations (an open hydrogen-bonded lattice → ice floats; dissolved particles → freezing-point depression, ΔTf = i·Kf·m). Every claim is tied back to the molecular evidence the model shows. States conclusions without evidence; names forces and properties without explaining why the structure produces the property. 35
Accurate chemistry The chemistry is correct — the three IMFs (London dispersion in all molecules, dipole-dipole, hydrogen bonding requiring H bonded to N, O, or F), the ionic crystal-lattice solid type and its properties, ion–dipole hydration and the strong-vs-nonelectrolyte distinction, and the colligative calculation (1.00 m NaCl → ΔTf = 2 × 1.86 × 1.00 = 3.72 °C) — and the demonstrated values are right. Contains chemistry errors; IMFs mislabeled or demonstrated values wrong. 25
Clear narration & model The narrated tour is organized and easy to follow, and the build (the three IMF models, the crystal lattice, the dissolution station, the property annotations) is recognizable and correct at a high-school level; audio and visuals are usable. Disorganized or hard to follow; models confused, missing, or don't show the chemistry; key parts inaudible. 20
Week-by-week iteration The build clearly shows all three stages (IMF models, lattice + dissolution station, property annotations), and the comment lists the three weekly builds. Only some stages present; little sign of building over time. 12
Uncertainty & limits reflection Names an honest limit — “like dissolves like” and colligative ideality are models that break down (real solutions deviate; a strong electrolyte's measured van’t Hoff factor runs slightly below the ideal whole number because of ion pairing) — showing the honesty of the quantitative. No acknowledgment of the model's limits or of real-solution deviations. 8
