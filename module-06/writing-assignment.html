Module 6 Writing Assignment
Why Matter Behaves — A Scientific Argument
Word (.docx) · 100 pts · teacher-reviewed
📚 THE TASK
All module you have argued one idea from many angles: the properties we can see and measure are written into the forces between particles. Why does one liquid boil long before another? Why does ice float? Why does salt thrown on a winter road melt the ice? None of these are arbitrary — each follows from intermolecular forces or from what dissolved particles do to a solvent.
In this assignment you will write a scientific argument: make a claim about an observed property, then defend it with molecular-level evidence and clear reasoning. Choose one of the two prompts below.
PROMPT A · BOILING-POINT ORDERING
Three substances of similar molar mass — ethane (C2H6), formaldehyde (CH2O), and ethanol (C2H5OH) — have very different boiling points (−89 °C, −19 °C, and +78 °C). Argue why they line up in that order, from the intermolecular forces each one has.
PROMPT B · WHY ROAD SALT MELTS ICE
Cities scatter salt (NaCl, and sometimes CaCl2) on icy roads in winter. Argue why dissolved salt lowers the freezing point of water — and why CaCl2 works at even lower temperatures than NaCl — using colligative behavior. Support it with a freezing-point-depression calculation.
You'll write it in Microsoft Word, organized into the argument sections listed further down.
⭐ WHY THIS MATTERS
This argument is one of only two teacher-reviewed pieces in Module 6 (the other is your Intermolecular Forces & States Model VR project). Your teacher reads it personally and responds to your reasoning — so make it your careful best, and connect every claim to the molecular level.
🧮 YOUR TOOLKIT (USE THESE EXACT IDEAS & VALUES)
IMF strength (similar-size molecules): hydrogen bonding > dipole-dipole > London dispersion. Stronger forces → higher boiling/melting point.
Hydrogen bonding requires: H bonded to N, O, or F (so an –OH or –NH group enables it).
Freezing-point depression: ΔTf = i · Kf · m, with Kf(water) = 1.86 °C/m and the van’t Hoff factor i = particles per formula unit (NaCl → 2, CaCl2 → 3, sugar/glucose → 1). New freezing point = 0 °C − ΔTf.
Colligative properties depend on the number of dissolved particles, not their identity.
🧠 YOUR REASONING TOOLKIT (how to build the argument)
Use these steps in your own words — don't just copy the headings.
Step 1 — Identify the forces (Prompt A) or the particles (Prompt B).
A: for each molecule, name every intermolecular force it has (every molecule has London dispersion; polar ones add dipole-dipole; an –OH group adds hydrogen bonding). B: state how many particles each formula unit releases when it dissolves (NaCl → Na+ + Cl− = 2; CaCl2 → Ca2+ + 2 Cl− = 3).
Step 2 — Connect force/particles to the property.
A: stronger intermolecular forces take more energy to overcome, so they raise the boiling point. B: more dissolved particles lower the freezing point more (and raise the boiling point) — that's colligative behavior.
Step 3 — Put a number on it.
A: use the given boiling points to confirm your ordering matches the force strength. B: compute ΔTf for 1.00 m NaCl and for 1.00 m CaCl2 and report each solution's freezing point — show the math.
📝 STRUCTURE: WRITE THESE ARGUMENT SECTIONS
1
Claim. One or two clear sentences stating your answer (e.g., “Ethanol boils highest because it is the only one of the three that can hydrogen-bond,” or “Salt lowers water's freezing point because dissolved ions are a colligative effect, and CaCl2 works better because it releases more particles.”).
2
Molecular-level Evidence. Identify the intermolecular forces (A) or the dissolved particles (B) for each substance. A small labeled sketch or structure helps — show where the –OH is, or how each formula unit breaks into ions.
3
Reasoning. Tie the evidence to the property with the correct rule (stronger IMF → higher boiling point; more dissolved particles → lower freezing point). Include your calculation: confirm the boiling-point order against the given values (A), or compute ΔTf = i·Kf·m and the freezing point for each salt (B). Show every step with units.
4
Real-world link. Connect your argument to something observable: why a fuel like ethane is a gas at room temperature while ethanol is a pourable liquid (A), or why a city might switch from NaCl to CaCl2 on the coldest nights (B).
5
Address a counter-case. Name one reasonable objection to your claim and answer it with evidence — a real argument anticipates the other side. A: for example, “Doesn't the heaviest molecule always boil highest?” Here the three have similar molar masses, so it is the type of intermolecular force, not mass, that sets the order — formaldehyde is polar, yet ethanol still boils higher because hydrogen bonding outranks dipole-dipole. B: for example, “Wouldn't sugar melt ice just as well, since it dissolves too?” No — colligative effects depend on the number of dissolved particles, and one sugar molecule (i = 1) releases fewer particles than NaCl (i = 2) or CaCl2 (i = 3), so sugar depresses the freezing point far less.
6
Conclusion. Restate your claim in light of the evidence and your answer to the counter-case, and name the big idea: the macroscopic property you can measure is set by what happens between particles.
✎ LENGTH & STYLE
Aim for roughly 500–700 words across the five sections, plus your worked calculation (handwritten and photographed in, or typed). Use correct terminology — London dispersion, dipole-dipole, hydrogen bonding, colligative property, freezing-point depression, van’t Hoff factor, molality. Carry units through your math and keep a sensible number of significant figures. The chemistry and the reasoning are what's graded — not polished prose.
⚠️ A NOTE ON USING AI TOOLS
An AI tool will gladly rank boiling points or compute a freezing point for you — and it can quietly invent a force a molecule doesn't have, forget that London dispersion is in every molecule, or drop the van’t Hoff factor and still sound confident. If you consult one, treat it as a draft to check, never an answer to paste: re-identify the forces yourself from each structure, re-run ΔTf = i·Kf·m by hand, and make sure its reasoning matches the molecular evidence before you trust it. The instrument proposes; your argument verifies. That is exactly the skill this assignment is built to grow.

📊 HOW THIS IS GRADED (TEACHER RUBRIC — build natively as a Canvas Rubric)
Criterion Full marks Developing Pts
Claim A clear, specific, scientifically defensible claim that directly answers the chosen prompt. Claim vague, missing, or only restates the question. 12
Molecular-level evidence Correctly identifies the intermolecular forces in each molecule (A) or the dissolved particles per formula unit (B); structures/sketches accurate. Forces or particle counts partly wrong (e.g., London dispersion omitted; i miscounted). 25
Reasoning & calculation Links evidence to the property with the correct rule, and the calculation (boiling-point order confirmed, or ΔTf = i·Kf·m and freezing points) is correct with units and shown work. Reasoning asserted without the rule, or calculation wrong / missing / unshown. 28
Counter-case addressed Names a reasonable objection and answers it with molecular-level evidence (mass vs IMF type; particle count vs mere dissolving), strengthening the argument. No counter-case, or the objection is dismissed without evidence. 12
Real-world link Connects the argument to a genuine, correct observation in the everyday world. Link missing, generic, or scientifically off. 10
Conclusion & communication Conclusion ties back to the claim and names the big idea; correct terminology, clear organized argument form. Conclusion thin or just restates numbers; terms misused; disorganized. 13
Total: 100 pts
The molecular-level evidence and the reasoning/calculation carry the most weight, and answering a counter-case with evidence earns real credit — a correct ordering or freezing point with no molecular explanation earns little. Argue from the particles up.
