📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A grain of table salt and a chip of diamond are both hard, clear, orderly solids — yet one dissolves in water and crumbles under a hammer, while the other shrugs off both. What inside a solid decides how it behaves?
For three lessons you have watched intermolecular forces set the behavior of liquids — their surface tension, their boiling points, the shapes of their phase diagrams. Now we cool matter all the way down to its most ordered state: the solid. In a solid the particles are locked in place, but how they are held — by ionic attraction, by weak intermolecular forces, by an unbroken web of covalent bonds, or by a shared sea of electrons — turns out to predict almost everything you can observe about it: whether it melts at a few degrees or a few thousand, whether it conducts electricity, whether it shatters or bends. This lesson sorts crystalline solids into four families by what holds them together, then looks at the repeating pattern — the crystal lattice — that gives a crystal its shape.

📚 PART 1 · FOUR FAMILIES OF SOLIDS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
Strike a salt crystal with a hammer and it shatters into smaller crystals. Strike a copper coin with the same hammer and it just dents and flattens. Both are solids made of orderly particles — so why does one break apart and the other bend? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

A crystalline solid is matter at its most orderly: its particles — atoms, ions, or molecules — sit in a regular, repeating pattern that extends in every direction. But not all solids are held together the same way, and that single fact — what kind of particle sits at each point, and what force pins it there — sorts solids into four families. Once you know which family a solid belongs to, you can predict how it will behave before you ever touch it.

Solid type What sits at each point & what holds it Typical properties Examples
Ionic Positive and negative ions in a lattice, held by strong ionic attraction High melting point; hard but brittle; conduct only when molten or dissolved NaCl (table salt)
Molecular Discrete molecules held by intermolecular forces (LDF, dipole–dipole, H-bonding) Low melting point; soft; do not conduct Ice, dry ice (CO2), sugar
Covalent network Atoms bonded in one continuous covalent web throughout the whole crystal Very high melting point; very hard; usually nonconducting Diamond, quartz (SiO2), silicon carbide
Metallic Cations in a shared “sea” of delocalized electrons Variable melting point; malleable/ductile; conduct heat and electricity Copper, iron

Read the table as a chain of cause and effect. The strength of the force holding particles in place sets the melting point: weak intermolecular forces in a molecular solid let it melt near room temperature (ice at 0 °C, dry ice subliming below room temperature), while the unbroken covalent web of diamond must have actual chemical bonds broken to melt — which takes thousands of degrees. The type of particle sets the electrical behavior: charges can carry a current only if they are free to move, so an ionic solid stays an insulator until it melts or dissolves and its ions break loose, while a metal conducts at once because its electron sea is already mobile.

The one question to ask: what is held, and how strongly?  Ions held by charge → hard, brittle, high-melting, conduct only when free.  Molecules held by IMFs → soft, low-melting, insulating.  Atoms in a covalent web → extremely hard and high-melting.  Metal atoms sharing electrons → bendable and conducting.

📚 PART 2 · THE CRYSTAL LATTICE: ORDER YOU CAN COUNT

What makes a crystal a crystal is not just that its particles are packed together, but that they are packed in a pattern that repeats. Picture floor tiles: one tile, copied and laid edge to edge, covers the whole floor. A crystal works the same way in three dimensions. The smallest repeating unit — the “tile” that, stacked in every direction, builds the entire crystal — is called the unit cell, and the full, extended pattern of points is the crystal lattice.

For many common solids the unit cell is a cube, and there are three cubic patterns worth naming, differing only in where the particles sit:

Simple cubic
A particle at each of the eight corners of the cube — the most basic repeating box.
Body-centered cubic (bcc)
Corners plus one particle in the very center of the cube.
Face-centered cubic (fcc)
Corners plus one particle in the center of each of the six faces.

A useful number that comes out of the pattern is the coordination number — how many nearest neighbors touch a given particle. It is a way of counting the order: a more tightly packed lattice gives each particle more neighbors. This is the quantitative habit Q3 keeps returning to — even something as abstract as a crystal's arrangement can be reduced to numbers you can compare.

Because the repeating pattern is so exact, a crystalline solid has long-range order, and that order shows up in a sharp, definite melting point: heat it, and the whole lattice gives way at essentially one temperature. Not every solid is built this way. An amorphous solid — ordinary glass, or many plastics — has its particles frozen in a jumble, with no repeating long-range pattern. Lacking a single regular structure, an amorphous solid does not have one sharp melting point; instead it softens over a range of temperatures as it is heated. A windowpane and a salt crystal are both “solid,” but only one is a crystal.

Crystalline vs amorphous: a crystalline solid has a repeating long-range pattern (a lattice built of unit cells) and a sharp melting point; an amorphous solid (glass, many plastics) has no long-range order and softens over a range instead of melting at one temperature.
▶  Think first, then check: A clear quartz crystal has a single sharp melting point, while a clear pane of window glass just softens gradually as it is heated — even though both are mostly silicon and oxygen. What does that difference tell you about their structures?
Quartz is crystalline: its atoms sit in a repeating, long-range lattice, so the whole structure gives way at one definite temperature — a sharp melting point. Window glass is amorphous: the same kinds of atoms are frozen in a disordered jumble with no long-range pattern, so there is no single temperature at which everything releases at once — it softens over a range instead. The sharp-versus-gradual behavior is a direct read-out of order versus disorder inside the solid.
🔬 NATURE OF SCIENCE · A MODEL, NOT THE WHOLE TRUTH
Sorting every solid into four tidy families — and picturing a crystal as a single unit cell copied endlessly — is a model: a deliberately simplified picture that earns its keep by letting you predict melting point, hardness, and conductivity before you ever touch a sample. But a model is not the thing itself. The categories blur at the edges (graphite is a covalent solid, yet it is soft, slippery, and conducts), and no real crystal is flawless — every one carries defects the perfect-lattice picture leaves out. That is how scientific models work: they are judged by how well they predict and explain, and knowing where a model breaks down matters as much as knowing where it holds. In your notebook: name one property this four-family model lets you predict well, and one situation where you would expect it to fall short.
📚 PART 3 · ONE ELEMENT, TWO SOLIDS: DIAMOND AND GRAPHITE

The clearest proof that arrangement sets properties comes from a single element doing two completely different things. Diamond and graphite are both made of nothing but carbon atoms — yet one is the hardest natural material known and an electrical insulator, while the other is soft, slippery, and conducts electricity. The only difference is how the carbon atoms are bonded together. Forms of the same element with different structures are called allotropes.

Diamond
Each carbon bonds to four neighbors in a rigid, three-dimensional covalent network. There are no weak points and no free electrons, so diamond is the hardest natural material and does not conduct.
Graphite
The carbon atoms form flat sheets that stack loosely on top of one another. The sheets slide easily (that is why graphite is slippery and writes on paper), and loose electrons within the sheets let graphite conduct — the conductive exception among covalent solids.

Same atoms, same element — but rearrange the bonding from a rigid 3-D web into stacked sheets, and hardness becomes slipperiness and an insulator becomes a conductor. There is no better illustration of this whole lesson's claim: in a solid, the structure is the property. If you can name how the particles are held and how they are arranged, you can predict what the solid will do.

💡 THEN · SEEING THE INVISIBLE LATTICE
For most of history the orderly inside of a crystal could only be guessed at from its outer shape — no one could see how the atoms were actually arranged. The deciding evidence arrived in 1912, when Max von Laue reasoned that if a crystal truly were a regular repeating lattice, a beam of X-rays sent through it should scatter into a pattern of sharp, orderly spots — and the experiment produced exactly that pattern, proof that the lattice was real. William Henry Bragg and his son William Lawrence Bragg then turned those spots into a method: from the angles and spacing of the scattered beams they worked backward to the actual positions of the atoms, and solved the structure of ordinary table salt itself. For the first time the arrangement inside a solid could be measured rather than imagined — the invisible lattice read directly from the evidence it left in the scattered rays.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Crystalline solids come in four families — ionic, molecular, covalent-network, and metallic — and which family a solid belongs to predicts its melting point, hardness, and electrical behavior. The pattern repeats through a unit cell to build the full crystal lattice, giving a crystal its sharp melting point, while amorphous solids like glass lack that long-range order and merely soften. Diamond and graphite, both pure carbon, prove the point: rearrange the bonding and you change everything observable. Hold onto this: in a solid, the structure is the property — name how the particles are held and arranged, and you can predict what the solid will do.
