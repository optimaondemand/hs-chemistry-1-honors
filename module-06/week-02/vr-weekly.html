Week 2 · OptimaXR
Build a Crystal Lattice & a Dissolution Station — Continue Your States Model
VR Assignment · 20 pts · one headset session (~35–45 min)
🧘 PREFER NOT TO USE A HEADSET?
A full non-VR alternate — “Build a Crystal Lattice & Dissolution Station (No-Headset Pathway)” — meets the exact same objectives and earns the same credit. Do one pathway, not both.
📚 WHAT YOU'RE DOING
You're continuing the project you build across all three weeks of this module: the Intermolecular Forces & States Model — a molecular-scale exhibit you'll narrate on camera at the end. This is Build 2 of 3. Beside last week's three intermolecular-force models, you'll add two new exhibits. First, a crystal lattice: a small repeating grid of alternating positive and negative ions, like the inside of a grain of salt, labeled as an ionic solid with its giveaway properties. Then a dissolution station: a few water molecules crowding around the edge of that lattice and pulling ions off into solution — ion–dipole hydration caught in the act — with a small contrast jar showing a nonelectrolyte dissolving as whole molecules. Next week you'll add property-explanation annotations and then record the full walkthrough.
🥽 WHAT YOU'LL NEED
▶  A VR headset with the OptimaXR app (or the OptimaXR desktop app).
▶  Your notes from Lessons 6.04–6.05 (the four solid types; ion–dipole hydration; strong electrolyte vs nonelectrolyte).
▶  Your IMF_Wk1_YourName snapshot from last week — you'll build right beside it.
▶  About 35–45 minutes in one sitting.
📍 HOW TO JOIN
Device: VR headset (or OptimaXR desktop app)
Session type: persistent (your placed objects stay between visits all module)
Session ID: see the pinned OptimaXR Sessions list in this module
Location: States & Solutions Lab
Spawn point: Lab Bench A
When you arrive: your three IMF models from Week 1 are still on the bench. To their right, a Whiteboard IFX shows this week's Step 1 beside an empty grid frame labeled Crystal Lattice and an empty tray labeled Dissolution Station. A sticky note reads “Start here.” You won't have to guess what to do.
📝 WHAT TO DO, IN ORDER
These steps follow the way a scientist actually investigates: Observe → Question → Predict → Investigate → Analyze → Defend → Extend. Don't skip the predict step — committing a prediction before the water touches the lattice is the point.
1
Observe. Confirm your Week 1 IMF models are still on the bench and read the Whiteboard IFX. From the IFX object library, spawn spheres and build a crystal lattice — a small repeating grid of alternating positive and negative ions (an NaCl-style ionic lattice, e.g., a 3×3×3 block of alternating Na+ and Cl−), using two colors so the ions are easy to tell apart. Label it an ionic solid with a sticky note listing its giveaway properties: high melting point, hard but brittle, conducts only when molten or dissolved. Leave open space to the right for the Week 3 annotations.
2
Question. On a sticky note, write the question you are about to investigate: “When water meets this ionic lattice, what happens to its ions — and will the result carry an electric current?” Place it on the bench beside the lattice.
3
Predict — before you build the dissolution. On a second sticky note, commit a prediction: when water reaches the lattice, will the ions break free? Will the salt solution conduct? And will a jar of a molecular solute like sugar conduct? Write down what you expect and why. This note is your committed prediction — do not change it after you see what happens.
4
Investigate. Build the dissolution station: place a few water molecules at the lattice edge with their negative (oxygen) ends toward a positive ion and their positive (hydrogen) ends toward a negative ion. Pull one Na+ and one Cl− off the lattice and surround each with water — show them as hydrated, free ions drifting away.
5
Analyze. Label the freed ions Na+(aq) and Cl−(aq) and the attraction as ion–dipole (hydration). Then place a small contrast jar showing a few whole, neutral molecules dissolving without breaking apart, labeled nonelectrolyte (e.g., sugar). Compare the two: the salt released free ions, the sugar did not. Check both against your committed prediction from step 3 — were you right?
6
Defend. Place a signed sticky note stating a claim with its evidence and reasoning: “Claim: salt water is a strong electrolyte and sugar water is not. Evidence: the lattice released free Na+(aq) and Cl−(aq), while the sugar stayed as whole molecules. Reasoning: only free charges can carry a current, so only the salt solution conducts.” Sign your name.
7
Extend. On one last sticky note, predict what a weak electrolyte (such as vinegar / acetic acid) would look like at this scale — how would its picture differ from both the salt (fully ionized) and the sugar (no ions)? You'll build toward property-explanation annotations next week.
💾
Save & capture. Lock the lattice, the water molecules, and your notes so they aren't bumped, save a snapshot named IMF_Wk2_YourName (your project's saved progress for next week), and take one in-headset screenshot showing the labeled crystal lattice, the dissolution station (water pulling ions off, the hydrated Na+(aq)/Cl−(aq) labels, and the nonelectrolyte contrast jar), and your claim note. That screenshot is what you'll submit.
Need to stop partway? Because the Session is persistent, your placed and locked exhibits will be waiting when you return. Save the IMF_Wk2_YourName snapshot before you log out so nothing is lost.

2. Add one sentence comparing the result to the prediction you committed in step 3. (This reflection is used when your work appears in the class gallery walk later this module.)
