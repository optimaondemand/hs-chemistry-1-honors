📖 WHAT THIS BUILDS ON
From this week's lessons: classifying solids by type and predicting their properties (6.04), and explaining dissolution, “like dissolves like,” and sorting electrolytes (6.05). You'll also practice reading a solubility curve — given as a small data table here — to decide whether a solution is unsaturated, saturated, or supersaturated, and to compare how solids and gases respond to temperature.
This is your week's “doing chemistry” work — reasoning and data-reading, no calculator required for most items.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
How does the way a substance is put together — the bonding in a solid, the polarity of a molecule, whether it breaks into ions — let you predict, before any experiment, how it will melt, dissolve, and conduct?
📋 HOW TO WORK THIS ASSIGNMENT

📊 PART A · CLASSIFY THE SOLID & PREDICT PROPERTIES (6.04)

For each substance, name its solid type (ionic, molecular, covalent network, or metallic) and give the property described.

1. Iron — can be hammered into shape and conducts electricity as a solid. Name its solid type, and state why it bends instead of shattering.
2. Quartz (SiO2) — extremely hard, melts only at a very high temperature, does not conduct. Name its solid type.
3. Sugar (sucrose) — a soft solid that melts at a low temperature and does not conduct. Name its solid type and the kind of force holding its particles together.
4. Potassium iodide (KI) — high-melting, hard but brittle. Name its solid type, and predict whether it will conduct electricity (a) as a solid and (b) when dissolved in water.
▶  Check your thinking — Part A answer key
1. Metallic. Iron is a metal — cations in a sea of delocalized electrons. It bends instead of shattering because layers of cations can slide past one another while the electron sea keeps holding them together; nothing snaps.
2. Covalent network. Quartz is a continuous covalent web of silicon and oxygen atoms, so melting it means breaking real covalent bonds — hence very hard and very high-melting, and nonconducting.
3. Molecular solid, held together by intermolecular forces (for sucrose, including hydrogen bonding). Weak forces between separate molecules give a low melting point, softness, and no conduction.
4. Ionic. KI is a soluble ionic salt. (a) As a solid it does not conduct (ions are locked in the lattice). (b) When dissolved it conducts, because the freed K⁺ and I⁻ ions can move.
📊 PART B · READ THE SOLUBILITY CURVE (data provided)

The table below is a solubility curve written out as numbers: the maximum mass of solute that dissolves in 100 g of water at each temperature. A point on the curve is exactly saturated; below the line is unsaturated (more can still dissolve); above the line is supersaturated (more is dissolved than equilibrium allows — unstable). Use it to answer the questions.

Solute 0 °C 20 °C 40 °C 60 °C 80 °C
KNO3 (g / 100 g water)133264110169
NaCl (g / 100 g water)3636373738
5. Which solute's solubility changes far more steeply as temperature rises — KNO3 or NaCl? What does that tell you about choosing a temperature to dissolve a lot of KNO3?
6. A beaker holds 60 g of KNO3 dissolved in 100 g of water at 40 °C. Using the table, is this solution unsaturated, saturated, or supersaturated? Explain.
7. You have 100 g of KNO3 dissolved in 100 g of water at 60 °C (still fully dissolved). You cool it slowly to 40 °C with no disturbance, and it all stays in solution. What kind of solution is it now, and roughly how many grams of KNO3 would crystallize out if a seed crystal were dropped in?
8. Gases vs solids: a sealed bottle of soda goes flat faster when left out warm, and a warm pond holds less dissolved oxygen than a cold one. Does gas solubility increase or decrease as temperature rises — and is that the same direction as KNO3 or the opposite?
✍️ REASON IN WORDS FIRST — THEN WRITE A SHORT DATA-ANALYSIS PARAGRAPH
9. Explain before you calculate. Before doing any arithmetic for question 7, write one or two sentences in plain words: what does it mean that the cooled solution is “supersaturated,” and how will you use the numbers at 60 °C and 40 °C to find how much KNO3 crystallizes out? Describe what the calculation will do and why — do not compute yet.
10. Data-analysis paragraph. In 3–4 sentences, describe the pattern in the solubility table: compare how KNO3 and NaCl each respond to rising temperature, then explain what that difference means for someone trying to purify a solid by dissolving it in hot water and cooling it — which of the two solids would this technique work well for, and why?
▶  Check your thinking — Part B answer key
5. KNO3 changes far more steeply (13 → 169 g across the range), while NaCl is nearly flat (36 → 38 g). To dissolve a lot of KNO3, use hot water — raising the temperature dramatically increases how much will dissolve. (Heating barely helps with NaCl.)
6. Unsaturated. At 40 °C the saturation limit is 64 g per 100 g water; 60 g is below the line, so more KNO3 could still dissolve.
7. Supersaturated. At 40 °C the limit is 64 g, but 100 g is still dissolved — more than equilibrium allows, an unstable state. A seed crystal would trigger crystallization of about 100 − 64 = 36 g of KNO3, leaving a saturated solution (~64 g dissolved).
8. Gas solubility decreases as temperature rises (warm soda goes flat; warm water holds less O2). This is the opposite direction from KNO3, whose solubility increases with temperature.
9. (reason-first, sample): “Supersaturated means the water is holding more KNO3 than it can normally hold at 40 °C, so it is unstable and the extra will drop out as crystals once something triggers it. To find how much, I’ll take the amount dissolved (what fit at 60 °C) and subtract the most that can stay dissolved at 40 °C — the difference must crystallize.” (The arithmetic in #7 then gives 100 − 64 = 36 g.) Any answer that states the subtraction in words before computing earns credit.
10. (data-analysis paragraph, sample): KNO3’s solubility climbs steeply with temperature (13 g at 0 °C up to 169 g at 80 °C), while NaCl barely moves (36–38 g across the whole range). Purifying by dissolving hot and then cooling (recrystallization) works well only when a lot of solid comes back out on cooling — so it works well for KNO3: dissolve a large amount in hot water, cool it, and most of the KNO3 crystallizes back as pure solid while impurities stay behind in solution. It would not work for NaCl, because cooling barely changes how much stays dissolved, so almost nothing would crystallize out.
📊 PART C · “LIKE DISSOLVES LIKE” PREDICTIONS (6.05)

Predict whether each pair will mix/dissolve well, and give the one-line reason. (Water and table salt are polar/ionic; oil and grease are nonpolar.)

9. Table salt (NaCl) in water. Will it dissolve well? Why?
10. Cooking oil in water. Will it dissolve well? Why?
11. Grease (nonpolar) in a nonpolar degreasing solvent. Will it dissolve well? Why?
▶  Check your thinking — Part C answer key
9. Yes. NaCl is ionic and water is polar — “like dissolves like.” Water's charged ends form strong ion–dipole attractions that pull the ions out of the lattice (hydration).
10. No. Oil is nonpolar and water is polar — not “like.” Water's hydrogen bonds cling to each other and exclude the nonpolar oil, so the two separate into layers.
11. Yes. Both grease and the solvent are nonpolar — “like dissolves like.” Comparable (London dispersion) attractions let the grease mix in. (This is why nonpolar solvents clean grease that water can't.)
📊 PART D · CLASSIFY THE ELECTROLYTE (6.05)

For each substance dissolved in water, classify it as a strong electrolyte, weak electrolyte, or nonelectrolyte, and say whether a bulb-and-battery tester would glow brightly, dimly, or not at all.

12. Hydrochloric acid (HCl), a strong acid.
13. Ethanol (a molecular alcohol).
14. Ammonia (NH3), a weak base.
15. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), a soluble strong base.
16. Tie it together: two clear glasses both contain a dissolved solid and look identical, but one lights the bulb and the other doesn't. What single thing must be different about what happened when each solid dissolved?
▶  Check your thinking — Part D answer key
12. Strong electrolyte — HCl is a strong acid that ionizes essentially completely — bulb glows brightly.
13. Nonelectrolyte — ethanol dissolves as whole, neutral molecules and forms no ions — bulb does not glow.
14. Weak electrolyte — ammonia only partially ionizes — bulb glows dimly.
15. Strong electrolyte — NaOH is a soluble strong base that dissociates completely into Na⁺ and OH⁻ — bulb glows brightly.
16. One solid broke into ions when it dissolved (an electrolyte — free charges can carry a current, so the bulb lights), while the other dissolved as whole, neutral molecules (a nonelectrolyte — no free charges, so the bulb stays dark). Dissolving and conducting are two different questions.
