📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Stir a spoonful of salt into a glass of water and it vanishes; stir in a spoonful of cooking oil and it stubbornly refuses to mix. Stir in sugar and it disappears like the salt — yet only the salt water can carry an electric current. What is the water actually doing to each one?
Last lesson you saw how a salt crystal is built — charged ions locked in a rigid lattice. Now you'll watch that lattice come apart. Dissolving looks like a disappearing act, but at the molecular level it is a tug-of-war of the very intermolecular forces you studied this module. This lesson explains why some things dissolve and others don't (the rule like dissolves like), follows water as it pulls an ionic solid apart ion by ion, and then sorts dissolved substances by a measurable test: whether their solution conducts electricity. That single test — does the bulb light? — turns out to reveal whether a substance broke into ions or stayed whole.

📚 PART 1 · WHAT DISSOLVING REALLY IS — AND “LIKE DISSOLVES LIKE”
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
Two clear glasses sit on a counter. One holds salt water, one holds sugar water — both perfectly dissolved, both look identical. Dip a battery-and-bulb tester into each, and the salt water lights the bulb while the sugar water leaves it dark. Both dissolved the same way, didn't they? So why does only one carry a current? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

A solution is a homogeneous mixture — one substance spread so evenly through another that it looks like a single thing. The substance present in the smaller amount, the thing being dissolved, is the solute; the substance doing the dissolving, present in the larger amount, is the solvent. In salt water, salt is the solute and water is the solvent.

Dissolving is not magic — it is a competition between attractions. To dissolve a solute, you must pull its particles apart from each other (breaking solute–solute attractions) and push apart some solvent particles to make room (breaking solvent–solvent attractions). What pays for all that is the new solute–solvent attraction that forms. A substance dissolves readily when the solute–solvent attractions are comparable to the attractions that had to be broken. When they're not — when the solute and solvent simply don't attract each other well — the solute stays put.

This balance gives chemistry one of its most quoted rules of thumb: like dissolves like. Polar and ionic solutes dissolve in polar solvents (water is the great example); nonpolar solutes dissolve in nonpolar solvents (oil, hexane). The reason oil and water refuse to mix is right here: water molecules cling to one another through strong hydrogen bonds, and a nonpolar oil molecule offers nothing comparable to break into that network — so the water simply excludes the oil, and the two separate into layers.

THE RULE OF THUMB
Like dissolves like
polar/ionic solutes → polar solvents (water)  ·  nonpolar solutes → nonpolar solvents (oil, hexane)
▶  Think first, then check: Why does oil refuse to mix with water, no matter how hard you stir?
Water molecules are strongly attracted to one another by hydrogen bonding. To dissolve oil, those water–water attractions would have to be broken and replaced by water–oil attractions — but oil is nonpolar and offers only weak London dispersion forces, which are no match for water's hydrogen bonds. There's nothing in it for the water, so it stays clustered with itself and excludes the oil. Polar water and nonpolar oil are not “like,” so they don't dissolve — they separate into layers. Stirring just breaks them into droplets that quickly recombine.
🔬 NATURE OF SCIENCE · A RULE OF THUMB, NOT A LAW
“Like dissolves like” is exactly that — a rule of thumb, a compact model rather than an exact law. It captures a real pattern (polar with polar, nonpolar with nonpolar) and predicts a great many cases correctly, which is why chemists keep it close at hand. But like every model it has edges: it tells you nothing about how much will dissolve, temperature and pressure can shift the result, and many molecules are partly polar and partly nonpolar, dissolving only so far. A true law would give you an exact answer; a rule of thumb points you the right way and still expects your judgment. That difference — a law that describes versus a model that explains but simplifies — runs through all of chemistry. In your notebook: name one prediction “like dissolves like” makes well, and one question it cannot answer on its own.
📚 PART 2 · HOW WATER TAKES A LATTICE APART: HYDRATION

Now follow the salt crystal from Lesson 6.04 as it meets water. A water molecule is polar: its oxygen end carries a slight negative charge and its hydrogen ends a slight positive charge. When a crystal of NaCl touches water, those charged ends go to work. The negative oxygen ends of water molecules crowd around each positive Na+ ion at the crystal's surface, while the positive hydrogen ends crowd around each negative Cl− ion. These attractions between an ion and the polar water molecules are called ion–dipole forces.

Tug by tug, the surrounding water molecules pull each ion out of the lattice and wrap it in a shell of water — a process called hydration (or, in any solvent, solvation). Freed from the crystal and cushioned by water, the ions drift off into solution. We write the whole event compactly:

NaCl(s) → Na+(aq) + Cl−(aq)
the solid lattice separates into hydrated ions spread through the water (“aq” = aqueous, surrounded by water)

This is the molecular picture behind “like dissolves like”: polar water can dissolve an ionic solid precisely because its charged ends form strong ion–dipole attractions that compete with — and win against — the ionic bonds holding the lattice together. A nonpolar solvent has no such charged ends, so it leaves the salt crystal untouched.

💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY
In 1884, a young Swedish chemist named Svante Arrhenius proposed a startling idea in his doctoral dissertation: that when salts and acids dissolve in water, they split apart into separate, electrically charged particles — ions — and that this is why such solutions can carry an electric current. At the time the notion that a stable compound would simply break into charged fragments in water seemed so far-fetched that his examiners barely passed his thesis. But the idea explained the conductivity measurements beautifully, and over the following years it became the foundation of how we understand solutions of acids, bases, and salts. Nearly two decades later, the same work earned Arrhenius the 1903 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. It is a quiet lesson in honesty — following the evidence even when it points somewhere uncomfortable: the conductivity data demanded ions, and Arrhenius trusted the data over the consensus of his day.

📚 PART 3 · ELECTROLYTES: STRONG, WEAK, AND NONE

Arrhenius's insight gives us a clean way to sort dissolved substances using one measurable test — does the solution conduct electricity? A current needs charged particles free to move, so a solution conducts only if the solute produced ions when it dissolved. Substances whose solutions conduct are called electrolytes, and they come in two strengths; substances whose solutions don't conduct are nonelectrolytes.

Category What happens in water Conducts? Examples
Strong electrolyte Fully dissociates / ionizes — nearly all of it becomes ions Strongly (bulb bright) Soluble salts (NaCl); strong acids (HCl); strong bases (NaOH)
Weak electrolyte Partially ionizes — only a small fraction becomes ions; the rest stays as whole molecules Weakly (bulb dim) Weak acids (acetic acid / vinegar); weak bases (ammonia)
Nonelectrolyte Dissolves but forms no ions — stays as whole, neutral molecules No (bulb dark) Sugar (sucrose); ethanol

This is the answer to the opening puzzle. Salt is a strong electrolyte: it dissociates completely into Na+ and Cl− ions, so salt water conducts strongly and the bulb burns bright. Sugar is a nonelectrolyte: it dissolves perfectly well, but as whole, neutral sucrose molecules — no ions form, no charge can move, and the bulb stays dark. Two solutions that look identical can be told apart by a single quantitative reading: how brightly the bulb lights measures how many ions are free to carry the current.

Notice the “strong vs weak” distinction is about the fraction that becomes ions, not how much dissolves. Acetic acid (the acid in vinegar) dissolves easily in water, but only a small fraction of its molecules break into ions at any moment — so vinegar conducts, but weakly. You'll return to this strong/weak idea in a big way when you study acids and bases next quarter.

🤖 AI AS AN INSTRUMENT · NOW
Ask an AI tool whether a substance is a strong, weak, or nonelectrolyte and it will usually answer instantly — but the reasoning is what you're being graded on, and a tool can get the label right for the wrong reason (or simply get an unusual case wrong). The reliable move is to reason it through yourself: does this substance form ions in water, and if so, fully or only partly? Soluble salts and strong acids → strong; partly-ionizing weak acids/bases → weak; molecular substances that stay whole (sugar, ethanol) → none. Use the tool to check your classification, then make sure your reason — ions or no ions — matches the evidence. The conductivity logic is the evidence; the tool is a second opinion, not the judge.
🔆 THEN → NOW → FUTURE · FROM DISSOLVING SALT TO CLEAN WATER AND BETTER BATTERIES
Then: nineteenth-century chemists worked out the two ideas behind this week — that a crystal is a repeating lattice, and that dissolving is ions and molecules pulling free into a solvent. Now: those same ideas run modern technology. Desalination plants force seawater through membranes that hold back the hydrated Na+ and Cl− ions while letting water through, turning ocean water into drinking water for millions of people; battery engineers design crystal-lattice materials whose structures let ions slip in and out as the cell charges and discharges. Future: the open questions are live ones — can we desalinate seawater with far less energy, and can we build lattice materials that store more ions without breaking down over thousands of charge cycles? Scientists are actively exploring both, and the chemistry you are learning now is where that work begins.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Dissolving is a tug-of-war of intermolecular forces: a solute dissolves when its attraction to the solvent is comparable to the attractions broken — which is why like dissolves like, and why polar water dissolves ionic salts but excludes nonpolar oil. Water takes an ionic lattice apart through ion–dipole hydration, sending free Na+(aq) and Cl−(aq) into solution. A single measurable test — does the solution conduct? — sorts solutes into strong electrolytes (fully ionize), weak electrolytes (partly ionize), and nonelectrolytes (no ions). Hold onto this: dissolving and conducting are two different questions — only substances that break into ions can carry a current.
