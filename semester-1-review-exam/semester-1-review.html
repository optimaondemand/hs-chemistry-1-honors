📚 SECTION A · MODULE 1 — MATTER, MEASUREMENT & DENSITY

Key ideas to carry in

Module 1 set the rules of the game: how chemists know and how they measure. Science moves from a tested hypothesis (a testable proposed explanation) to a law (a pattern that describes what happens) to a theory (a well-tested explanation of why — strong, but still open to revision). Every idea can be viewed in three domains: the macroscopic (what we see and weigh), the microscopic (atoms and molecules), and the symbolic (formulas and equations).

Classifying matter
Pure substances = elements (one kind of atom) or compounds (atoms chemically bonded in a fixed ratio). Mixtures = homogeneous (uniform throughout, e.g. air) or heterogeneous (visibly non-uniform).
Properties
Physical (color, density, melting point) can be observed without changing identity. Chemical (flammability, reactivity) only show when the substance becomes something new.
Measuring well
SI units, significant figures (leading zeros never count), accuracy (close to true) vs. precision (repeatable), and dimensional analysis (cancel units to guide the math).

Worked refresher: identify a metal by density

A solid sample has a mass of 54.0 g and a volume of 20.0 cm³. Reference densities: aluminum 2.70, iron 7.87, copper 8.96 g/cm³. Which metal is it?

Use the density formula D = m / V:
D  =  54.0 g ÷ 20.0 cm³  =  2.70 g/cm³
2.70 g/cm³ matches the reference value for aluminum.

Density is an intensive property — it doesn't depend on sample size — so it identifies a material no matter how big the piece is.

Sig-fig reminder: How many significant figures are in 0.003080 m? Leading zeros don't count; the digits 3, 0, 8, and the trailing 0 after the decimal do → 4 significant figures.

📚 SECTION B · MODULE 2 — ATOMIC STRUCTURE & THE PERIODIC TABLE

Key ideas to carry in

Module 2 zoomed inside the atom — and showed a model that evolved as evidence arrived: Dalton (solid spheres) → Thomson (electrons in a positive cloud) → Rutherford (the gold-foil experiment revealed a tiny dense positive nucleus) → Bohr (electrons in fixed energy levels) → the quantum model (electrons in orbitals/probability regions). Each model kept what worked and fixed what didn't — the Nature of Science in action.

Counting the atom
Z (atomic number) = protons. A (mass number) = protons + neutrons. A neutral atom has electrons = protons. Isotopes share Z but differ in neutrons (so differ in A).
Light & electrons
Light: c = λν and E = hν. Because energy levels are quantized, atoms emit only certain photon energies — a sharp line spectrum, not a continuous rainbow.
Organizing matter
Electron configuration and valence electrons drive chemistry. Periodic trends: atomic radius and electronegativity. The table is ordered by increasing atomic number.

Worked refresher: average atomic mass (weighted)

An element has two isotopes: mass 35.0 u at 75.0% abundance and mass 37.0 u at 25.0% abundance. Find the average atomic mass.

Multiply each mass by its abundance (as a decimal), then add:
(0.750 × 35.0) + (0.250 × 37.0)  =  26.25 + 9.25  =  35.5 u
The average leans toward the more abundant, lighter isotope. These values match chlorine.
More to know cold: A neutral atom with 17 protons and 18 neutrons → Z = 17, A = 35, 17 electrons (chlorine). Its configuration is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁵ = 17 electrons, 7 valence. And c = λν for 5.0×10⁻⁷ m light: ν = (3.0×10⁸) ÷ (5.0×10⁻⁷) = 6.0×10¹⁴ Hz.

📚 SECTION C · MODULE 3 — CHEMICAL BONDS & MOLECULAR GEOMETRY

Key ideas to carry in

Module 3 turned atomic structure into bonding. In an ionic bond a metal transfers electrons to a nonmetal; the resulting oppositely charged ions lock into a lattice (which is why solid salt doesn't conduct, but molten or dissolved salt does — the freed ions can carry charge). In a covalent bond atoms share electrons. Whether the sharing is fair is set by the electronegativity difference — the Module 2 trend doing real work:

~0–0.4
Nonpolar covalent
~0.4–1.7
Polar covalent (δ+/δ−)
large
Trends ionic

Naming: ionic compounds use Roman numerals for transition metals (FeCl3 = iron(III) chloride); two nonmetals use Greek prefixes (N2O4 = dinitrogen tetroxide); polyatomic ions keep their names (Ca(NO3)2 = calcium nitrate). Lewis structures account for all valence electrons (CO2 = 4 + 2(6) = 16). Resonance (e.g. ozone's equal bonds) shows the single-drawing model has limits — the real molecule is one averaged hybrid. VSEPR predicts shape from electron domains.

Worked refresher: name a compound & count valence electrons

Name FeCl3: Iron is a transition metal with more than one possible charge, so a Roman numeral is required. Three Cl− demand Fe³+ → iron(III) chloride.

Total valence electrons in CO2: carbon contributes 4, each oxygen 6 → 4 + 2(6) = 16 valence electrons, drawn as O=C=O with two lone pairs on each oxygen.

Polarity twist: CO2 has polar bonds but is a nonpolar molecule — its linear shape makes the bond dipoles cancel. H2O is bent, so its dipoles don't cancel: it's polar.

📚 SECTION D · MODULE 4 — CHEMICAL REACTIONS & STOICHIOMETRY

Key ideas to carry in

Module 4 made chemistry quantitative. The mole is the chemist's counting unit: one mole = 6.022 × 1023 particles (Avogadro's number). Molar mass (g/mol) links the balance to the molecule, so you can move freely along the bridge grams ↔ moles ↔ particles. We balance equations by changing only coefficients (never subscripts — that would change the substance's identity), honoring conservation of mass.

Formulas & concentration
Empirical = simplest whole-number ratio; molecular = the actual count. Molarity M = mol ÷ L; dilution M₁V₁ = M₂V₂; plus mass percent / ppm.
The mole bridge
grams A → mol A → (coefficient ratio) mol B → grams B. The balanced equation's coefficients are the mole-to-mole bridge between substances.
Reaction types & yield
Precipitation, acid-base, redox, and combination / decomposition / replacement / combustion. The limiting reactant makes the least product; % yield = actual ÷ theoretical × 100.

Worked refresher: limiting reactant

For 2 H2 + O2 → 2 H2O, you combine 4 mol H2 with 1 mol O2. Which runs out first, and how much water forms?

Test each reactant against the coefficient ratios:
If all 4 mol H2 reacted (H2 : H2O = 2 : 2) → up to 4 mol H2O.
If all 1 mol O2 reacted (O2 : H2O = 1 : 2) → only 2 mol H2O.
O2 makes the least, so O2 is limiting → 2 mol H2O forms, with 2 mol H2 left over as excess.
Have these conversions ready: 88.02 g CO2 ÷ 44.01 g/mol = 2.00 mol; molarity 0.100 mol ÷ 0.250 L = 0.400 M; empirical formula from 40.0% C / 6.7% H / 53.3% O → ratio 1:2:1 = CH2O; percent yield 31.0 ÷ 35.7 × 100 = 86.8%.

💡 HOW TO STUDY THIS · A NATURE-OF-SCIENCE NOTE
Don't study these four modules as four separate piles of facts — study the spine that runs through them. Structure (M1–M2) explains bonding (M3), which explains reaction and measurement (M4). When a fact feels isolated, ask what it explains downstream. Two habits of mind recur all semester and are worth carrying into the exam: first, models evolve — Dalton to the quantum atom, the single Lewis structure to resonance — because science revises explanations when evidence demands it, and that is a strength, not a weakness. Second, honesty in measurement — significant figures, accuracy vs. precision, conservation of mass, and unit-canceling are not busywork; they are how a chemist tells what the evidence actually says from what they wish it said. To prepare: re-read the worked examples and redo each one on paper without looking, run every self-check widget until the feedback is green, and revisit any module's reading checks where you stumble.
