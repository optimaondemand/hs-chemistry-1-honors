📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
When a candle burns, where does the wax go — and how can chemistry promise that not a single atom was lost?
You can watch a log shrink to ash, a strip of magnesium flash into white powder, two clear liquids mix and turn solid. Matter is clearly changing. But for two hundred years one idea has held firm beneath every one of those changes: the atoms don't vanish and they don't appear from nowhere — they only rearrange. A chemical equation is how chemists write that promise down precisely, with every atom accounted for on both sides. In this lesson you learn to read an equation, to write one with the correct symbols, and to balance it so the books close perfectly — the bookkeeping discipline that the rest of this module is built on.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to read a chemical equation — identifying reactants, products, coefficients, and the state symbols (s), (l), (g), and (aq).
2
I will be able to explain conservation of mass and why a balanced equation must have equal atom counts on each side.
3
I will be able to balance a chemical equation by adjusting coefficients only — never changing subscripts — and verify it with an atom inventory.

📚 PART 1 · WHAT AN EQUATION SAYS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
Burn a 100-gram log in a sealed, airtight box and weigh the box before and after the fire. What do you predict the scale reads afterward? (No wrong answer — just commit to a stance.)

A chemical equation is a sentence written in the symbolic language of chemistry. On the left are the reactants — the substances you start with. On the right are the products — what they become. An arrow, read as “yields” or “produces,” points from reactants to products. The combustion of methane (natural gas) looks like this:

CH4(g)  +  2 O2(g)  →  CO2(g)  +  2 H2O(g)

Every part of that line carries information. The formulas (CH4, O2, CO2, H2O) name the substances. The large numbers in front — the coefficients — tell you how many of each: here, one methane and two oxygen molecules react to make one carbon dioxide and two water molecules. (A coefficient of 1 is never written; it is understood.) And the small italic tags after each formula are state symbols, telling you the physical state of each substance.

(s)
solid
(l)
liquid
(g)
gas
(aq)
aqueous — dissolved in water

That last one, (aq), will matter a great deal: it means the substance is dissolved in water — the molarity world you just left in Lesson 4.04. When silver nitrate solution meets table-salt solution, both dissolved, a solid suddenly appears: AgNO3(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO3(aq). The state symbols let the equation tell you, at a glance, that a solid dropped out of two clear solutions.

📚 PART 2 · CONSERVATION OF MASS — THE RULE BEHIND IT ALL

Here is the single most important idea in this lesson, and it is the reason equations must be balanced at all. In any ordinary chemical reaction, matter is neither created nor destroyed — atoms are only rearranged. This is the law of conservation of mass. Whatever atoms you start with on the reactant side, you must end with on the product side: the same number of carbon atoms, the same number of hydrogen atoms, the same of everything. They may be bonded differently, but they are all still there.

💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY
In the late 1700s the French chemist Antoine Lavoisier did something deceptively simple: he weighed everything. Burning and rusting were long thought to release or consume a mysterious substance, and an open flame really does leave less ash behind. Lavoisier's move was to run his reactions in sealed, closed vessels and weigh them before and after with a precise balance. The total mass never changed — not when metals were heated and gained weight, not when substances burned. When a metal gained mass on heating, that mass had come from a gas in the air (oxygen) combining with it, not from nothing. With the balance as his instrument, Lavoisier turned a vague intuition into a measurable law. It is a quiet model of scientific honesty: let the careful measurement, not the appearance, decide what is true.
🔬 HOW SCIENTISTS KNOW · TWO COMPETING EXPLANATIONS, AND WHAT DECIDED BETWEEN THEM
Conservation of mass did not arrive obvious and unopposed. For most of the 1700s the leading explanation of burning was phlogiston theory — a careful, widely accepted idea, not a foolish one. It held that flammable materials contained a fire-substance called phlogiston that was released as they burned. That theory explained a great deal: why a flame goes out in a sealed jar (the air becomes “full” of phlogiston), why ash weighs less than the wood. It was the reasonable prior view of its day. The two rival explanations lined up like this:
Phlogiston theory (the prior explanation)
Burning releases a hidden substance. Prediction: a burned material should weigh less afterward, because it lost its phlogiston.
Oxygen theory (Lavoisier's explanation)
Burning combines the material with a gas from the air (oxygen). Prediction: a metal that burns or rusts should weigh more afterward, because it gained that gas.
What evidence decided? When metals were heated in air, careful weighing showed they gained mass — exactly what oxygen theory predicts and exactly what phlogiston theory could not explain (losing a substance cannot make you heavier). In a sealed vessel the total mass held constant, because the gas that combined with the metal was still inside being weighed. The measurement, not the theory's elegance or its long acceptance, settled it. That is the lasting Nature-of-Science lesson here: a coherent, reasonable, widely believed theory can still be wrong — and evidence, not authority, is what reveals it.

Because of this law, a correct equation needs the same count of each kind of atom on both sides. We make that happen by choosing the right coefficients — the numbers in front of each formula. And here is the rule you must never break:

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · BALANCE WITH COEFFICIENTS, NEVER SUBSCRIPTS
To balance an equation you change only the coefficients (the big numbers in front). You may never change a subscript (the small numbers inside a formula). Why? The subscripts are the substance. H2O is water; rewrite it as H2O2 and you have hydrogen peroxide — a different chemical entirely. Changing a subscript doesn't balance the equation; it changes what reacted. Coefficients say how many molecules; subscripts say what the molecule is. Only the first is yours to adjust.
📚 PART 3 · BALANCING, STEP BY STEP
🧩 START WITH THE PARTICLES, NOT THE NUMBERS
Before you reach for a coefficient, do the one thing balancing is really about: count the atoms — the actual particles — on each side. Picture the molecules as clusters of atoms. If one kind of atom appears more often on the left than on the right, then atoms would have to vanish for the reaction to happen — and they can't, because mass is conserved. So an unequal particle count is the equation telling you it isn't finished yet. Coefficients are simply the tool you use to make the two particle counts agree. The habit is always the same three moves: count the particles → adjust a coefficient → re-count to prove they match. Balancing an equation is nothing more than writing conservation of mass down one atom at a time.

Worked once: the synthesis of water

Hydrogen gas and oxygen gas combine to form water. A first, unbalanced sketch — just the right substances — looks like this:

H2  +  O2  →  H2O   (not balanced yet)

Take an atom inventory. Left side: 2 H and 2 O. Right side: 2 H and only 1 O. The hydrogens match, but oxygen does not — 2 on the left, 1 on the right. Remember the rule: we cannot rewrite H2O as H2O2 to fix it. Instead we adjust coefficients. Put a 2 in front of H2O to supply a second oxygen:

H2  +  O2  →  2 H2O

Re-inventory. Right side now: 4 H and 2 O. Oxygen is fixed (2 = 2), but now hydrogen is off — only 2 on the left, 4 on the right. Add a 2 in front of H2 on the left:

2 H2(g)  +  O2(g)  →  2 H2O(g)
AtomLeft sideRight sideBalanced?
H2 × 2 = 42 × 2 = 4✓
O1 × 2 = 22 × 1 = 2✓

Both columns match: 4 H and 2 O on each side. The equation is balanced — and this very reaction, 2 H2 + O2 → 2 H2O, is the one you'll build to scale in your OptimaXR project this week.

Worked again: methane combustion (the equation from Part 1)

Look back at CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O. Do the inventory yourself before reading on. Carbon: 1 on the left (in CH4), 1 on the right (in CO2) — balanced. Hydrogen: 4 on the left (CH4), 4 on the right (2 × H2O = 4) — balanced. Oxygen: 4 on the left (2 × O2 = 4), and on the right 2 (in CO2) + 2 (in 2 H2O) = 4 — balanced. Every element checks. Always close the loop by re-counting every atom — that final inventory is your proof.

🤖 AI AS AN INSTRUMENT · VERIFY THE ATOM COUNT YOURSELF
Type “balance this equation” into an AI chatbot and it will hand you coefficients in a second. Useful — but an AI can confidently produce coefficients that don't actually conserve atoms, and it cannot know that you typed the formula correctly. So treat the AI's answer as a proposal, not a verdict: run your own atom inventory on its result. If carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen each tally equal on both sides, the answer earns your trust; if not, you've caught a mistake the machine couldn't. The balance-checker above models exactly this habit — the tool counts, but you confirm conservation of mass.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A chemical equation is a promise kept in atoms: reactants on the left, products on the right, and — because matter is conserved — every atom that starts must finish. You balance an equation by adjusting coefficients, never subscripts, and you prove it with an atom inventory that matches on both sides. Lavoisier earned that law with a balance and a sealed flask; you now wield it every time you write a reaction. Next, you'll sort the endless variety of reactions into a handful of recognizable families.
