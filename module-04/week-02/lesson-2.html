📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If molarity is so useful, why does a bottle of rubbing alcohol say “70%” and a water-quality report list contaminants in “parts per million” instead?
Molarity is the chemist's workhorse because reactions count moles. But molarity isn't always the most natural way to say “how much.” A pharmacist mixing a cream, an engineer monitoring a reservoir for a trace pollutant, and a manufacturer bottling alcohol each reach for a different unit — one that fits what they measure and what they care about. This lesson rounds out your concentration toolkit with three more units: mass percent, parts per million and per billion, and volume percent. The deeper skill isn't memorizing formulas — it's learning to pick the right unit for the job.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to calculate the mass percent of a solute from the mass of solute and the mass of solution.
2
I will be able to express the concentration of a trace solute in parts per million (ppm) or parts per billion (ppb), and interpret what those numbers mean for water quality.
3
I will be able to compute volume percent and choose the most appropriate concentration unit for a given situation.

📚 PART 1 · MASS PERCENT
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
A label reads “5% saline solution.” Before reading on, predict: when a label gives a plain percentage like this, what two amounts do you think are being compared — and why might that be easier to put on a bottle than molarity? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

The simplest way to state a concentration needs no moles at all — just a scale. Mass percent is the mass of solute as a percentage of the total mass of the solution:

mass percent = mass of solutemass of solution × 100%

Watch the denominator: it's the mass of the whole solution, which is solute plus solvent — the same “don't forget the solute takes up space” lesson you met with molarity, now in mass form.

Worked once: a salt solution

Dissolve 5.0 g of NaCl in 95 g of water. The mass of the solution is 5.0 g + 95 g = 100 g. So:

mass percent = (5.0 g ÷ 100 g) × 100% = 5.0%

A 5.0% solution — exactly what a label would read. Because it needs only a balance and no knowledge of what the substance even is, mass percent is the workhorse of consumer products, food labels, and medicine: hydrogen peroxide is sold as “3%,” saline as “0.9%,” and so on.

🔬 THEN · A UNIT FOR EVERY TRADE
Long before anyone spoke of moles, merchants, apothecaries, and metal assayers already needed to state “how much” in numbers a buyer could trust. The practical demands of commerce and medicine — certifying the strength of a spirit, the purity of a metal, the dose in a remedy — pushed people toward simple, checkable ratios that anyone with a balance could verify. Expressing one component as a percentage of the whole was the natural answer, and it stuck: it needs no theory of atoms, only a scale. That is why, long afterward, mass percent and volume percent still label the bottles on your shelf. The unit survived because it solved a real problem — making concentration a number ordinary people could check for themselves.
📚 PART 2 · PARTS PER MILLION AND PER BILLION — THE TRACE AMOUNTS

Now picture a different problem. A water utility must confirm that a contaminant sits below a safety limit — but the amount is so tiny that writing it as a percent would give an absurd number like 0.00025%. For these trace concentrations, chemists scale up the comparison. Parts per million (ppm) multiplies the solute-to-solution mass ratio by one million instead of one hundred; parts per billion (ppb) multiplies by one billion:

ppm = (mass of solute ÷ mass of solution) × 106
ppb = (mass of solute ÷ mass of solution) × 109

There's a handy shortcut for dilute water solutions. Because one liter of water has a mass very close to one kilogram (a million milligrams), 1 ppm is about 1 milligram of solute per liter of water (1 mg/L). That little equivalence turns a lot of water-quality math into something you can do in your head.

Worked once: fluoride in drinking water

A sample contains 0.0025 g of fluoride dissolved in 1.0 kg of water. Express this in ppm. Match the units — 1.0 kg of water is 1000 g, and 0.0025 g of fluoride is 2.5 mg:

ppm = (0.0025 g ÷ 1000 g) × 106 = 2.5 ppm
check with the shortcut: 2.5 mg in 1 L (≈ 1 kg) of water = 2.5 mg/L ≈ 2.5 ppm ✓

This is no abstract example. Drinking-water rules are written in exactly these units: fluoride is added to many water supplies at roughly the level above, and legal limits for contaminants like lead are set in ppb — because at those concentrations, the difference between 5 ppb and 50 ppb is the difference between safe and unsafe. The unit you choose has to match the smallness of what you're measuring.

📈 NOW · WHERE THIS IS HEADING
Trace analysis is a frontier of modern chemistry. Researchers today are pushing detection limits ever lower — from parts per billion down toward parts per trillion — to track substances such as so-called “forever chemicals” and microplastics that affect drinking water and ecosystems. Scientists are currently exploring more sensitive instruments and faster sensors so that contaminants can be caught at concentrations once thought impossible to measure. The arithmetic you just did — a mass ratio scaled up — is the same idea those frontier measurements rest on; only the instruments have grown sharper.
📚 PART 3 · VOLUME PERCENT & CHOOSING THE RIGHT UNIT

When both the solute and the solvent are liquids, it's often easiest to measure them by volume rather than mass. Volume percent (% v/v) is the volume of solute as a percentage of the total solution volume. Rubbing alcohol labeled 70% v/v means 70 mL of isopropyl alcohol in every 100 mL of solution — the rest is water. You'll see the same convention on beverages and disinfectants, because pouring a liquid is simpler than weighing it.

Tap each card to compare the three units and when each fits best:

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
Mass percent — (mass solute / mass solution) × 100%
Best when you can weigh things and don't need moles — food, medicine, consumer products. Example: 3% hydrogen peroxide. Needs only a balance.
⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
ppm / ppb — ratio × 10⁶ or 10⁹
Best for trace amounts too small for a sensible percent — pollutants, drinking-water limits, fluoride. Shortcut: 1 ppm ≈ 1 mg/L in water.
⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
Volume percent — (vol solute / vol solution) × 100%
Best for liquid-in-liquid mixtures you'd rather pour than weigh — rubbing alcohol (70% v/v), beverages, disinfectants.
▶  Think first, then check: A pool report lists chlorine at 3 ppm. Roughly how many milligrams of chlorine are in 1 liter of the pool water?
Use the water shortcut: 1 ppm ≈ 1 mg/L, so 3 ppm ≈ 3 mg/L — about 3 milligrams of chlorine in every liter. Notice how natural ppm is here: writing the same thing as a mass percent would be 0.0003%, an awkward string of zeros. The unit is chosen to fit the size of the quantity.

🔬 HOW SCIENTISTS KNOW · A NUMBER NEEDS ITS UNIT
Here is a quiet lesson in scientific honesty: “the fluoride level is 2.5” means nothing until you say 2.5 what. The same physical solution is “0.00025%” and “2.5 ppm” — same reality, different unit. Good measurement isn't only getting the digits right; it's reporting them in a unit that's honest about scale and lets another scientist reproduce your result. A chemist who quotes a contaminant in plain percent isn't wrong, exactly — just unhelpful. Choosing the right unit is part of telling the truth about what you measured.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Concentration can be reported many ways, each fitted to a purpose: molarity when a reaction counts moles, mass percent when a balance is all you have, ppm / ppb when the amount is vanishingly small, and volume percent when you'd rather pour than weigh. Every one of them is the same underlying idea — solute relative to solution — expressed at the right scale. Measuring well means choosing the unit that's honest about what you measured, and reporting the number with the unit that makes it reproducible.
