📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A bottle of saltwater and a glass of seawater both taste salty — but how would a chemist say exactly how salty each one is, in a number another chemist anywhere could reproduce?
Most chemistry doesn't happen with dry powders on a bench — it happens in solution, with substances dissolved in water. And the moment something dissolves, a new question appears: how much is dissolved in how much liquid? “A pinch” and “a splash” won't do; a reaction needs counted particles. Last week you learned to count particles with the mole. This lesson marries the mole to volume to give chemistry its most-used measure of concentration: molarity. By the end you'll be able to state precisely how concentrated a solution is, prepare one to a target concentration, and dilute a strong stock down to a weaker working solution — the everyday arithmetic of every laboratory.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to calculate the molarity of a solution from the moles (or grams) of solute and the volume of solution, and use molarity as a conversion factor.
2
I will be able to describe how to prepare a solution of a known molarity using a volumetric flask.
3
I will be able to use the dilution relationship M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ to find the volume of stock solution needed for a target concentration, and explain why moles of solute are conserved on dilution.

📚 PART 1 · CONCENTRATION AND MOLARITY
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
Two beakers each hold one spoonful of dissolved salt. The first beaker holds half a liter of water; the second holds two liters. Which solution is more concentrated — and what does that tell you about what “concentration” must depend on? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

A solution is a uniform mixture in which one substance — the solute — is dissolved in another — the solvent (very often water). To describe a solution quantitatively, we need its concentration: how much solute is present in a given amount of solution. As your prediction showed, this has to be a ratio; the bare amount of solute isn't enough, because the same salt spread through more water is more dilute.

Chemists reach for one concentration measure more than any other, because it speaks the language of reactions — counted particles. It is molarity (symbol M): the number of moles of solute per liter of solution.

molarity (M) = moles of soluteliters of solution

A 1.0 M solution holds one mole of solute in every liter of solution. Read the units out loud — “moles per liter” — and the formula tells you how to use it: amount of solute on top, volume of solution (not solvent) on the bottom.

Worked once: dissolving table salt

Suppose you dissolve 5.85 g of sodium chloride (NaCl) in enough water to make 0.250 L of solution. What is its molarity? First convert grams to moles using NaCl's molar mass, 58.44 g/mol:

moles NaCl = 5.85 g ÷ 58.44 g/mol = 0.100 mol
molarity = 0.100 mol ÷ 0.250 L = 0.400 M

The solution is 0.400 M — “0.400 molar” — meaning every liter would hold 0.400 mol of NaCl. Notice the two-step shape: grams → moles (the mole bridge from last week), then moles ÷ liters. Here's a second, simpler case to fix the pattern: dissolve 0.50 mol of NaCl in enough water to make 2.0 L of solution, and the molarity is just 0.50 mol ÷ 2.0 L = 0.25 M. Fewer moles spread through more liters — a dilute solution, exactly as your prediction anticipated.

🔬 WORTH KNOWING · MOLARITY IS A CONVERSION FACTOR
Once you know a solution's molarity, the ratio works in both directions, just like the mole bridge. A 0.400 M NaCl solution carries the conversion factor “0.400 mol per 1 L.” Need the moles in 0.500 L of it? Multiply: 0.500 L × 0.400 mol/L = 0.200 mol. Need the volume that contains 0.200 mol? Divide: 0.200 mol ÷ 0.400 mol/L = 0.500 L. Molarity is the bridge between a volume you can measure with a flask and the moles a reaction actually counts.
🔬 HOW SCIENTISTS KNOW · WHY THE NUMBER (AND ITS UNIT) MATTERS
Pause on something easy to overlook: why do chemists bother to define concentration this precisely? Because a measurement is only worth anything if another person, in another lab, can reproduce it. “I added a spoonful of salt” can't be checked by anyone else; “0.400 mol of solute per liter of solution” can be rebuilt exactly, anywhere, by anyone. That demand for reproducibility is the evidence standard that turned chemistry from recipes into a science — and it is the whole reason a careful unit like molarity exists. As you work, ask yourself: could someone else remake my solution from the number I wrote down? If yes, you have measured; if no, you have only guessed.
📚 PART 2 · PREPARING A SOLUTION OF KNOWN MOLARITY

Knowing the formula is one thing; making the real solution is another, and it hides a trap. Molarity is moles per liter of solution — the final mixture — not moles per liter of water you start with. The solute itself takes up space, so you can't simply add solute to a liter of water and expect a liter of solution. The fix is a piece of glassware built for exactly this: the volumetric flask, which has a single etched line marking one precise volume.

1 · Weigh the solute
Decide your target moles (molarity × volume), convert to grams with the molar mass, and weigh out that mass on a balance.
2 · Dissolve in some solvent
Add the solute to the volumetric flask with part of the water and swirl until it fully dissolves — not all the way to the line yet.
3 · Fill to the mark
Carefully add water until the bottom of the meniscus sits exactly on the etched line. Now the solution volume is exact — and so is the molarity.

Walk it through with numbers. To make 0.250 L of 0.400 M NaCl, you need 0.250 L × 0.400 mol/L = 0.100 mol of NaCl, which is 0.100 mol × 58.44 g/mol = 5.85 g. So you weigh 5.85 g of salt, dissolve it in a little water inside a 250 mL volumetric flask, then top up with water to the 250 mL line. That is precisely the solution from Part 1 — now you know how to build it, not just describe it.

🔬 THEN · MAKING “HOW CONCENTRATED” A MEASURABLE NUMBER
For most of history, describing a solution meant vague words — “strong,” “weak,” “a good pinch.” The problem that pushed chemists forward was practical and urgent: an apothecary measuring a dose, or an analyst testing the purity of a metal, needed to say exactly how much was dissolved, in a way anyone else could match. Out of that need grew volumetric analysis — the practice of preparing solutions of a known, standardized concentration and measuring precise volumes with purpose-built glassware like the volumetric flask you just met. Slowly, concentration stopped being an opinion and became a number. Molarity is the modern heir of that long effort to turn “how concentrated” into something you could measure and report rather than merely sense.

📚 PART 3 · DILUTION — MAKING WEAKER FROM STRONGER

Laboratories rarely make every solution from scratch. Instead they keep concentrated stock solutions and water them down to whatever working concentration a task needs. This is dilution: adding more solvent to lower the concentration. And dilution hides one beautifully simple idea — adding water does not add or remove solute. The number of moles of solute is exactly the same before and after; you've only spread those same moles through more liquid.

That conservation gives us the dilution equation. Since moles = molarity × volume, and the moles don't change, the moles before equals the moles after:

M₁V₁ = M₂V₂
(concentrated stock)  =  (diluted solution) — same moles on both sides

Worked once: diluting acid stock

A bottle of 6.0 M hydrochloric acid (HCl) sits on the shelf, but your procedure needs 0.500 L of 0.50 M HCl. How much of the stock should you measure out? Label what you know — the diluted side is M₂ = 0.50 M and V₂ = 0.500 L; the stock is M₁ = 6.0 M; you want V₁. Rearrange and solve:

V₁ = (M₂ × V₂) ÷ M₁
V₁ = (0.50 M × 0.500 L) ÷ 6.0 M = 0.0417 L = 41.7 mL of stock

So you carefully measure 41.7 mL of the 6.0 M stock into a 500 mL volumetric flask, then add water up to the 500 mL mark. (With acids, always add acid to water, never the reverse.) Sanity-check it: you took 41.7 mL of strong acid and spread it through twelve times the volume, so the concentration should drop by about twelve-fold — from 6.0 M to 0.50 M. It does. The moles of HCl never changed; only the volume they occupy did.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Molarity is the mole made measurable: moles of solute per liter of solution, the ratio that lets a volume you can pour stand in for a particle count a reaction can use. Prepare a solution by weighing solute and filling to the mark on a volumetric flask; dilute one by remembering that water changes the volume but never the moles, so M₁V₁ = M₂V₂. With concentration now under quantitative control, you're ready to ask what other units chemists use when molarity isn't the most natural fit.
