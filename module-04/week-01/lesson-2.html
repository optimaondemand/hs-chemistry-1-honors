📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
When a chemist isolates a brand-new compound, no label tells them its formula. So how do they figure out which atoms, and how many of each, are inside?
Yesterday you went from a formula to a mass. Today you reverse the arrow. A lab can measure what percent of a compound's mass comes from each element — and from those percentages you can reconstruct its formula. This is real detective work: it is how the formula of a new molecule is first established, and the same logic stands behind every formula in your textbook. You'll learn to read percent composition out of a known formula, then run it the other way: from percentages, to the simplest whole-number ratio (the empirical formula), to the true molecule (the molecular formula).

📚 PART 1 · PERCENT COMPOSITION: HOW MUCH IS EACH ELEMENT?
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
In water, H2O, there are two light hydrogen atoms and one much heavier oxygen atom. If you broke a sample of water down and weighed the hydrogen and the oxygen separately, which would account for more of the total mass? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

A compound's percent composition is the percentage of its total mass contributed by each element. To find it from a formula, you take the total mass of one element in the formula, divide by the formula mass, and multiply by 100%. The recipe is short:

% element = (mass of that element in the formula ÷ formula mass) × 100%

Worked once: water, H2O

From Lesson 4.01, the formula mass of water is 18.02. The hydrogen in it weighs 2 × 1.008 = 2.016; the oxygen weighs 16.00. So:

% H = (2.016 ÷ 18.02) × 100% = 11.19% hydrogen
% O = (16.00 ÷ 18.02) × 100% = 88.81% oxygen
(Check: 11.19% + 88.81% = 100% — the percentages always sum to 100.)

Just as you predicted: oxygen carries the lion's share of water's mass, even though hydrogen wins on atom count. That gap between how many and how heavy is exactly why percent composition is reported by mass — mass is what a lab can actually weigh.

📚 PART 2 · FROM PERCENTAGES TO AN EMPIRICAL FORMULA

Now run the arrow backward. A lab hands you percentages and asks for the formula. The trick is the empirical formula — the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in the compound. (The molecular formula may be a multiple of it; we get there in Part 3.) The method has four moves, and the first is a clever shortcut:

1 · Assume a 100-gram sample
In 100 g, each percent becomes grams directly: 40.0% C means 40.0 g of carbon. No data is lost — ratios don't depend on sample size.
2 · Grams → moles
Divide each element's grams by its atomic mass. Now you have moles of each atom — an atom count in the chemist's unit.
3 · Divide by the smallest
Divide every mole value by the smallest of them. This turns the ratio into one that starts at 1.
4 · Round to whole numbers
These become the subscripts. (If a value lands near 1.5 or 2.5, multiply all of them up to clear the fraction.)

Worked once: 40.0% C, 6.7% H, 53.3% O

A lab reports a compound is 40.0% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen, and 53.3% oxygen by mass. Find its empirical formula.

Step 1 — assume 100 g: 40.0 g C, 6.7 g H, 53.3 g O.
Step 2 — grams → moles:
  C: 40.0 ÷ 12.01 = 3.33 mol  ·  H: 6.7 ÷ 1.008 = 6.65 mol  ·  O: 53.3 ÷ 16.00 = 3.33 mol
Step 3 — divide by the smallest (3.33):
  C: 3.33 ÷ 3.33 = 1  ·  H: 6.65 ÷ 3.33 ≈ 2  ·  O: 3.33 ÷ 3.33 = 1
Step 4 — subscripts: C1H2O1 → empirical formula CH2O

The compound's atoms are in a 1:2:1 ratio of carbon to hydrogen to oxygen — CH2O. That is the simplest ratio, but it might not be the whole molecule. Notice how the mole did the heavy lifting again: percentages told us mass, mass gave us moles, and moles — an atom count — gave us the ratio. The mole is the hinge of every step.

💡 THEN · THE LAWS THAT MADE FORMULAS POSSIBLE
Why should a compound have a fixed formula at all? Around 1800 the French chemist Joseph Proust established the law of definite proportions: a given compound always contains the same elements in the same proportion by mass, no matter how it was made — water is always about 11% hydrogen and 89% oxygen. Soon after, the English chemist John Dalton noticed the law of multiple proportions: when two elements form more than one compound, the masses combine in small whole-number ratios. Together these two laws were the evidence that matter is built from discrete atoms combining in fixed counts — and they are exactly what makes an empirical formula a meaningful, repeatable answer rather than an accident of one sample.
🔍 NATURE OF SCIENCE · WHAT COUNTS AS EVIDENCE?
Before Proust and Dalton, it was not obvious that a compound must have a fixed formula at all. What changed scientists' minds was evidence: sample after sample of the same compound, however it was prepared, gave the same proportions by mass. Notice the two kinds of claim at work. Proust's definite proportions and Dalton's multiple proportions are laws — they describe a regularity measured again and again. Dalton's atomic theory is what explains that regularity: matter comes in discrete atoms that combine in whole-number counts. A law says what happens reliably; a theory says why. Ask yourself: because an empirical formula rests on measured mass, how could two different labs check each other's answer — and why does that reproducibility make the formula trustworthy?

📚 PART 3 · FROM EMPIRICAL TO MOLECULAR FORMULA

An empirical formula gives the ratio, but not the size. Two very different molecules can share the empirical formula CH2O: formaldehyde (the molecule is literally CH2O) and glucose, the sugar your body runs on. Glucose has the same 1:2:1 ratio — but six times as many of each atom. To tell them apart you need one more measurement: the compound's actual molar mass. Then a single division finishes the job:

n = molar mass ÷ empirical-formula mass   →   molecular formula = (empirical formula) × n

Worked once: CH2O with molar mass 180

Empirical-formula mass of CH2O: 12.01 + 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 30.03
Given molar mass: 180 g/mol
Find n: n = 180 ÷ 30.03 ≈ 6
Multiply the subscripts by 6: C(1×6)H(2×6)O(1×6) = C6H12O6 — glucose

The same percentages that gave CH2O, paired with a molar mass of 180, reveal the true molecule: glucose, C6H12O6. The empirical formula was honest but incomplete; the molar mass supplied the missing scale. This is the difference between “what's the ratio?” and “what's the molecule?”

Tap each card to test the empirical/molecular distinction:

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
Is CH2O the same as C6H12O6?
Same empirical formula (ratio 1:2:1), different molecular formula. CH2O is formaldehyde; C6H12O6 is glucose, with n = 6 times as many atoms. The ratio is identical; the molecules are not.
⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
What does n tell you?
n is how many empirical units make up one molecule: n = molar mass ÷ empirical mass. If n = 1, the empirical and molecular formulas are the same (as for water and CO2). If n = 6, multiply every subscript by 6.
⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
Why isn't the empirical formula enough?
Percent composition fixes the ratio but not the size — many molecules share a ratio. Only an independent molar-mass measurement reveals the true number of atoms. That's the one extra fact the lab must supply.

🔬 NOW · FROM A SLOW BURN TO A FAST INSTRUMENT
For a century, finding percent composition meant carefully burning a sample and weighing the products by hand — the same painstaking weighing that Proust and Dalton relied on. Today an instrument called a mass spectrometer can read a compound's molar mass in moments, and software (increasingly AI-assisted) proposes candidate formulas automatically. The chemistry hasn't changed — ratios from mass, scale from molar mass — only the speed has. And the discipline is the same as ever: when a tool proposes a formula, a chemist still verifies it against the measured composition. The instrument is fast; the human checks that the answer adds up.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Percent composition is the mass fraction of each element; run it backward through the mole and you recover the empirical formula, the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. One more fact — the measured molar mass — scales that ratio up to the true molecular formula. This is how the formula of a real compound is established from measurements alone, resting on Proust's and Dalton's laws that compounds combine in fixed, whole-number proportions. Once you can name the molecule, you're ready to write the reactions it takes part in.
