📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A scale reads “2.5 g.” A better scale reads “2.4783 g.” Why isn't the longer number always the more honest one?
Every measurement carries some uncertainty — there is always a last digit that is estimated, never perfectly known. A good scientist's job is not to pretend that uncertainty away but to report it honestly. Today you'll learn how a measurement's digits encode its uncertainty (significant figures), how to tell accuracy from precision, and why writing too many digits is just as dishonest as writing too few.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to count the significant figures in a measurement and round a result to a stated number of them.
2
I will be able to distinguish accuracy from precision and judge a set of measurements on both.
3
I will be able to apply the significant-figure rules for multiplication/division and addition/subtraction.

📚 PART 1 · EVERY MEASUREMENT HAS A LAST, UNCERTAIN DIGIT
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · OBSERVE FIRST
You measure a pencil with a ruler marked only in whole centimeters. The pencil ends a little past the 14 mark. What's the most honest way to record it?

No measuring instrument is perfect, so every measurement carries uncertainty. The convention scientists follow is simple and honest: record all the digits you know for certain, plus one final digit that you estimate. With a ruler marked in centimeters, you can be sure the pencil is past 14 and estimate the tenths place by eye — so “14.7 cm” is exactly right. The 14 is certain; the 7 is your best estimate; stopping there tells an honest reader precisely how good your tool was.

The digits in a measurement that carry real information — all the certain ones plus that one estimated one — are its significant figures (often “sig figs”). They are the measurement's way of announcing its own precision. “2.5 g” quietly says I was measured on a tool good to about a tenth of a gram. “2.4783 g” says I came from a far finer balance. Reporting five digits from a kitchen scale would be a lie about the instrument.

The rules for counting significant figures
●  Non-zero digits are always significant. (38.7 → 3 sig figs)
●  Zeros between non-zero digits count. (50.08 → 4 sig figs)
●  Leading zeros never count — they only place the decimal. (0.0042 → 2 sig figs)
●  Trailing zeros count only if there's a decimal point. (2.50 → 3 sig figs; but 250 → ambiguous, usually 2)
●  Exact / counted numbers (12 eggs, or 1000 mL = 1 L by definition) have infinite sig figs — they never limit a calculation.

🔬 SIG FIGS COME FROM THE TOOL, NOT A DIGIT-COUNTING TRICK
The rules above are a shortcut for reading a number someone already wrote down. But when you take the measurement, the number of sig figs is set by the instrument. Reason from the tool:
• A graduated cylinder marked every 1 mL. You can see it’s between 24 and 25 mL and estimate the tenths — so 24.0 mL is honest (3 sig figs). Writing 24.00 mL would claim a hundredths mark the cylinder doesn’t have.
• A digital balance that displays to 0.01 g. If it reads 5.00 g, all three digits are justified by the instrument — the trailing zeros are real, not decoration — so 5.00 g has 3 sig figs. The same object on a scale reading only whole grams would honestly be 5 g (1 sig fig).
Same object, different tools, different sig figs. Always ask: what is the smallest marking, and one estimate past it?
📚 PART 2 · ACCURACY vs. PRECISION

Two words that sound like synonyms in everyday speech mean very different things in the lab. Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the true value. Precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other — their reproducibility. A set of measurements can have one without the other, and the dartboard is the classic way to see it.

Low accuracy,
low precision
scattered and off-center
Low accuracy,
high precision
tight cluster, wrong spot
High accuracy,
high precision
tight cluster on target

The middle target is the dangerous one. Measurements that agree beautifully with each other but cluster around the wrong value are precise but not accurate — usually a sign of a systematic error, like a scale that wasn't zeroed. Precision can make a wrong answer look trustworthy, which is exactly why scientists check their instruments against known standards before trusting tight, repeatable data.

💡 THE QUICK TEST
Accurate? Ask: is it close to the true value? Precise? Ask: do my repeats agree with each other? You'll judge your own lab data on both in this module's density lab report — and you'll meet a third measure, percent error, that puts a number on accuracy.
📚 PART 3 · KEEPING SIG FIGS IN CALCULATIONS

Calculators don't know about uncertainty — divide 10 by 3 and you get 3.33333333. Your answer can never be more precise than the measurements you started with, so you must round the calculator's output back to the right number of sig figs. There are two rules, depending on the operation.

Multiply & divide
The answer keeps the fewest significant figures of any factor.

4.18 × 2.0 = 8.36 → round to 8.4 (2 sig figs, because 2.0 has only 2).
Add & subtract
The answer keeps the fewest decimal places of any term.

12.11 + 1.2 = 13.31 → round to 13.3 (one decimal place, because 1.2 has only one).
▶  Worked example: a density calculation, rounded honestly
A metal sample has mass 25.34 g (4 sig figs) and volume 3.0 mL (2 sig figs).
density = 25.34 ÷ 3.0 = 8.4466666… on the calculator.
This is a division, so the answer keeps the fewest sig figs of the inputs — that's 2 (from 3.0 mL).
Final answer: 8.4 g/mL. Reporting “8.4467 g/mL” would falsely claim five-digit precision you never measured.
🔧 EVERY TOOL HELPS — AND EVERY TOOL HAS A LIMIT
Doing chemistry means working through a chain of instruments, and each one extends what you can do and has a boundary you must respect:
• A ruler reads length — but only to one estimated digit past its finest mark.
• A balance reads mass — but only to the precision of its display, and only if it was zeroed.
• A calculator does the arithmetic — but it will happily print ten digits of false precision.
• An AI tool can organize and compute — but it never saw your sample or your instrument.
Knowing each tool’s limit is the scientist’s job. The tool never knows it for you.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI, NUMBERS, AND FALSE PRECISION
Ask an AI tool to crunch your lab data and it will gladly hand back an answer like “8.44667 g/mL” — clean, confident, and wrong about its own precision. An AI doesn't know your ruler had only centimeter marks; it can't see your instrument. This is exactly the moment the AI-as-instrument habit matters: use the tool to compute, but you are the one who knows the measurement was good to two sig figs, and you round it honestly. The judgment about what a number can truthfully claim stays human. Verify against the real measurement, every time.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

An honest measurement reports what you know plus one estimated digit — no more, no less. Significant figures carry that honesty into every calculation; accuracy and precision are two separate questions you must ask of any data. This is the empirical integrity — honesty about evidence — that the rest of chemistry rests on.
