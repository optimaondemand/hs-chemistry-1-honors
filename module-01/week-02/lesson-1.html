Lesson 1.04
Measurements — Units, Prefixes & the SI System
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If you told a chemist in Tokyo that a sample weighs “5,” they'd have no idea what you meant. Five what?
A number alone is almost useless in science. 5 grams, 5 kilometers, and 5 seconds describe completely different things. Chemistry runs on measurement, and measurement runs on a shared, worldwide language of units. Today you'll learn that language — the SI base units chemists actually use, the prefixes that let one unit stretch from the size of an atom to the size of a continent, and how to read a temperature on the scale that chemistry is built on.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name the SI base units chemists use for length, mass, time, temperature, and amount of substance, and state common derived units like the liter.
2
I will be able to interpret the common metric prefixes (kilo-, deci-, centi-, milli-, micro-, nano-) as powers of ten.
3
I will be able to convert between Celsius and Kelvin and explain why chemists favor the Kelvin scale.

📚 PART 1 · WHY A NUMBER NEEDS A UNIT
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A recipe says “add 2 of water.” Before reading on — what's the problem here? (No wrong answer; just notice it.)
⏳ THE PROBLEM THAT CAME FIRST
For most of history, the units themselves were unreliable. A “foot” might be the length of a particular king’s actual foot; a “pound” of goods in one town was a different amount of matter than a “pound” a day’s travel away. A careful measurement recorded in one place could not be trusted — or reproduced — somewhere else. That is a fatal problem for a science built on evidence anyone can check. The question this lesson answers is therefore not just “what units do we use?” but the deeper one: how can scientists compare evidence across time and place?

Every measurement in science is two things joined together: a number that tells you how much, and a unit that tells you of what. “36.0 g of water” is a measurement; “36.0” is just a number waiting for meaning. Strip the unit away and you can't compare it, reproduce it, or check it — and reproducibility, as you saw in Lesson 1.01, is the whole foundation of science.

For most of history this was a genuine mess. Towns and trades each kept their own “foot,” “pound,” and “cup,” and a measurement that meant one thing in one market meant something else a day's travel away. Science needed a single language. The one the world settled on is the International System of Units — abbreviated SI, from the French Système International.

💡 THEN · A MEASURE FOR ALL PEOPLE
The metric system was born out of the French Revolution in the 1790s, when scientists set out to replace a tangle of local units with one rational, decimal system “for all people, for all time.” Its great idea was to base everything on powers of ten, so converting between sizes meant simply moving a decimal point — no remembering that 12 inches make a foot and 3 feet make a yard. Refined and formalized as SI in 1960, it is now the shared measuring language of essentially every scientist on Earth.
🔬 NATURE OF SCIENCE · WHAT A MEASUREMENT REALLY IS
A measurement is not a fact handed to you by the universe — it is an observation made with an instrument. A ruler, a balance, a thermometer: each is a tool a human built, and every tool has limits to how finely and how truthfully it can read. That means every measurement carries a little uncertainty from the instrument that made it. Agreeing on units (SI) is the first half of trustworthy measurement; understanding the limits of your instruments is the second half — and it is exactly what the next lesson is about.
📚 PART 2 · THE SI BASE UNITS A CHEMIST USES

SI defines seven base units from which every other unit is built. Five of them come up constantly in chemistry. Learn these and you have the vocabulary for nearly every measurement this year.

Quantity SI base unit Symbol
Lengthmeterm
Masskilogramkg
Timeseconds
TemperaturekelvinK
Amount of substancemolemol

(SI also defines the ampere for electric current and the candela for light intensity, but those belong to physics; chemistry leans on the five above. The mole — the chemist's way of counting atoms — gets a whole lesson of its own in Module 4.)

From these base units we build derived units by combining them. Volume is length cubed: the SI unit is the cubic meter (m³), but it's far too big for a beaker, so chemists use the liter (L) and milliliter (mL). A crucial equality to memorize: 1 mL = 1 cm³ exactly. Density, which you'll meet in Lesson 1.07, is mass divided by volume — another derived unit (g/mL or g/cm³).

💡 A QUIRK WORTH KNOWING
The kilogram is the only base unit with a prefix (“kilo-”) built into its name — a historical accident. In the lab you'll most often weigh things in grams (g), where 1000 g = 1 kg. Just remember that when SI does the formal bookkeeping, mass is officially measured in kilograms.
📚 PART 3 · PREFIXES: ONE UNIT, EVERY SCALE

Here is the metric system's best trick. Instead of inventing a new word for every size — the way English has inch, foot, yard, mile — SI keeps one base unit and attaches a prefix that multiplies it by a power of ten. The meter becomes a kilometer (×1000) or a millimeter (×1/1000) just by swapping the prefix. Learn the prefixes once and they work on every unit: kilograms, milliliters, nanoseconds, all of it.

A sense of scale makes these stick. A kilometer is a brisk ten-minute walk; a centimeter is about the width of your fingernail; a millimeter is the thickness of a credit card; and a nanometer is the realm of single atoms and molecules — which is exactly why cutting-edge materials science is called nanotechnology. The same prefixes carry you from the everyday all the way down to the world you met in Lesson 1.02.

📜 WHERE THE WORDS COME FROM
The prefixes are ancient Greek and Latin, and the root usually tells you the size — so you can reason to the meaning instead of memorizing it. The larger units borrow Greek: kilo- from chilioi, “thousand.” The fractions borrow mostly Latin counting words: centi- from centum, “hundred” (as in century), and milli- from mille, “thousand” (as in millennium). The very smallest borrow Greek words for smallness: micro- from mikros, “small,” and nano- from nanos, “dwarf.” When you meet an unfamiliar prefix, the root is your first clue to its scale.

📚 PART 4 · TEMPERATURE: WHY CHEMISTS USE KELVIN

You already know the Celsius (°C) scale: water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C. It's convenient for everyday life, but it has a flaw for science — it has negative numbers. What would “twice as cold” even mean when temperatures can drop below zero? Chemistry needed a scale with a true bottom.

That scale is the Kelvin (K) scale, the SI unit for temperature. It starts at absolute zero — the coldest anything can possibly be, the point where particle motion is at its absolute minimum. There are no negative kelvins. The degrees are the exact same size as Celsius degrees; the whole scale is just shifted, so converting is simple addition:

K = °C + 273.15        °C = K − 273.15
Water freezes at 0 °C = 273.15 K · Water boils at 100 °C = 373.15 K · Absolute zero = 0 K = −273.15 °C

Notice that kelvin temperatures are written without a degree symbol — just 298 K, not 298 °K. Room temperature, about 25 °C, is roughly 298 K. You'll need Kelvin the moment you reach the gas laws in Module 5, where using Celsius would give nonsense answers; we introduce it now so it's familiar long before it's required.

▶  Worked example: convert 37 °C (human body temperature) to kelvin
Use K = °C + 273.15.
K = 37 + 273.15 = 310.15 K (often rounded to 310 K).

Going the other way: a sample at 350 K is 350 − 273.15 = 76.85 °C — hot enough to scald.
⭐ THEN → NOW → FUTURE · REDEFINING THE UNITS THEMSELVES
Then: for over a century the kilogram was defined by a single metal cylinder locked in a vault near Paris — and the world's entire mass standard depended on that one object not changing. Now: in 2019 scientists finished a quiet revolution, redefining the kilogram, the kelvin, the mole, and the ampere in terms of fixed constants of nature (for mass, the Planck constant) instead of physical objects, so any lab with the right equipment can realize the standards from the laws of physics themselves. Future: researchers are still pushing measurement to be more precise — building atomic clocks that would lose less than a second over the age of the universe and asking how finely we will ever be able to measure. Metrology, it turns out, is an open frontier.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A measurement is a number and a unit. SI gives chemists a shared, decimal language — five base units, a ladder of powers-of-ten prefixes, and the Kelvin scale that starts at absolute zero. Master this vocabulary now; every calculation you do for the rest of the year speaks it.
