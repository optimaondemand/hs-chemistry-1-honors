Lesson 1.06
Dimensional Analysis — The Factor-Label Method
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What if you could convert any unit into any other — grams to pounds, hours to seconds, even atoms to moles — with one reliable method that tells you when you've made a mistake?
You can. It's called dimensional analysis (or the factor-label method), and it is the single most useful problem-solving tool in all of chemistry. The idea is almost embarrassingly simple — multiply by cleverly chosen forms of the number 1 — but it will carry you from this lesson all the way through stoichiometry, gas laws, and beyond. Learn it well now and the math of the whole year gets easier.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to write a conversion factor from an equality and use it to cancel units.
2
I will be able to solve a single-step and a multi-step unit conversion by chaining conversion factors.
3
I will be able to use unit cancellation to check whether a setup is correct before I calculate.

📚 PART 1 · THE BIG IDEA: MULTIPLY BY 1
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
You know there are 12 inches in 1 foot. So what is the value of the fraction (12 inches) / (1 foot)?
🧠 THE REAL QUESTION BEHIND THE METHOD
Dimensional analysis looks like a calculation trick, but it is really a way of asking: how can the units reveal whether our reasoning is valid? Think of a conversion as a short logical argument. Each conversion factor is a true premise (a known equality). The units are the evidence that the premises were combined correctly — if they cancel down to the unit the question asks for, the argument is sound; if they don’t, the reasoning has a flaw no arithmetic can hide. You are not just converting numbers; you are checking your own logic.

Any true equality between two units can be written as a fraction that equals 1. Since 1 foot = 12 inches, both of these fractions equal 1:

12 in / 1 ft  =  1      and      1 ft / 12 in  =  1

These are called conversion factors. Multiplying any measurement by 1 leaves its real value unchanged — but if you choose the right form, the units you don't want cancel, and the units you do want are left standing. That's the entire method. You're not changing the amount; you're changing the clothes it wears.

💡 THE RULE THAT MAKES IT WORK
Set up each factor so the unit you want to get rid of is on the opposite side of the fraction line from where it starts — that's what lets it cancel, just like a number in algebra. If your units cancel down to the answer's unit, your setup is right. If they don't, stop — you've set it up backward, and no amount of arithmetic will fix it.
📚 PART 2 · A SINGLE-STEP CONVERSION, STEP BY STEP

Problem: A sample of water has a volume of 2500 mL. Convert this to liters.

THE SETUP — FOLLOW THE CANCELING UNITS
2500 mL  ×  1 L 1000 mL  =  2.5 L
↖  the two mL cancel (top × bottom)  ↗  →  only L survives
Why this answers the question: the problem asked “how many liters?”, and the surviving unit is L — that match is your evidence the setup is valid. Only then do the numbers: 2500 ÷ 1000 = 2.5.

Three moves, every time: (1) write what you're given, with its unit; (2) multiply by the conversion factor arranged so the starting unit cancels; (3) do the arithmetic and keep the surviving unit. Notice we wrote the equality 1 L = 1000 mL with the “mL” on the bottom because we needed it to cancel the mL we started with.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH SETUP CANCELS CORRECTLY?
For each conversion, pick the setup whose units cancel to give the right answer. (Don't calculate yet — just check the units.)
1. Convert 5.0 g to kg. (1 kg = 1000 g)
2. Convert 3.0 minutes to seconds. (1 min = 60 s)
3. Convert 45 cm to m. (1 m = 100 cm)
📚 PART 3 · CHAINING FACTORS: THE MULTI-STEP CONVERSION

The real power shows up when no single conversion factor gets you all the way. Then you chain factors, one after another, each one canceling the unit the last one produced — like stepping stones across a river. You don't need to see the far bank from the start; you just need each next stone.

▶  Worked example: how many seconds are in 2.0 hours?
We don't have a direct “hours → seconds” factor in mind, but we know two equalities: 1 h = 60 min and 1 min = 60 s. Chain them:

2.0 h × (60 min / 1 h) × (60 s / 1 min) = 7200 s

The h cancels with the first factor, leaving min; the min cancels with the second factor, leaving s. Arithmetic: 2.0 × 60 × 60 = 7200 s (or 7.2 × 10³ s).

Here is the habit that will save you all year: set up the whole chain and check the units before you touch the calculator. If the units cancel to the answer you want, press the buttons with confidence. If they don't, your reasoning is off and the arithmetic can't rescue it. Dimensional analysis is self-checking — that's why chemists love it.

✎ SAY WHY IT WORKS · A ONE-SENTENCE HABIT
After every multi-step conversion you set up — in this lesson, on the weekly assignment, and all year — write one sentence completing this stem in your notebook:
“I know my setup is reasonable because ______.”
A strong answer names the units as evidence — for the example above: “I know my setup is reasonable because the hours and minutes both cancel, leaving seconds, which is what the question asked for; and 7200 s for 2 hours is sensible since an hour already holds 3600 s.” The point is to defend the reasoning, not just report the number.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Flipping a factor upside down. If you write 2.0 h × (1 h / 60 min), the hours don't cancel (you'd get h²/min — nonsense). The cancellation check catches this instantly: if a unit you meant to remove is still there — or shows up squared — flip that factor over.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
Why write the conversion factor as a fraction at all?
Because a true equality (like 1 L = 1000 mL) written as a fraction equals 1, and multiplying by 1 changes the units without changing the amount.
⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
How do you know your setup is right before calculating?
Check the units: every unit except the answer's unit should cancel. If it does, the setup is correct; if not, a factor is flipped.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Dimensional analysis converts any unit to any other by multiplying by carefully chosen forms of 1. Arrange each factor so unwanted units cancel, chain as many as you need, and check the surviving units before you calculate. This one method — not memorized formulas — is how chemists do quantitative work.
