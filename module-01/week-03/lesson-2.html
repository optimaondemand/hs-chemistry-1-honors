📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A small lead fishing weight sinks instantly; a massive steel ship floats. How can the heavier object be the one that stays up?
The answer isn't weight at all — it's density, how much mass is packed into a given space. Density is the intensive property you met in Lesson 1.03, now made quantitative. It identifies substances, predicts what floats, and is your first real chance to put the factor-label method to work. By the end you'll calculate it, rearrange its formula, and use it to unmask an unknown material — exactly what this module's lab asks of you.

📚 PART 1 · WHAT DENSITY MEASURES
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
Which has more mass: a kilogram of feathers or a kilogram of lead? (Read it carefully — then notice what the question is really testing.)

Density is the amount of mass packed into a given volume — mass divided by volume. It answers “how tightly is the matter packed?” rather than “how much is there?” That's why it's an intensive property (recall Lesson 1.03): a gold coin and a gold bar have wildly different masses and volumes, but the same density. Because every pure substance has its own characteristic density, density is a kind of fingerprint — a number you can look up and use to tell substances apart.

density = mass ÷ volume        D = m / V
Common units: g/mL for liquids · g/cm³ for solids  (remember 1 mL = 1 cm³)

A few reference densities are worth carrying in your head. Liquid water is 1.00 g/mL — the natural benchmark. Aluminum is 2.70 g/cm³, iron about 7.87 g/cm³, lead 11.3 g/cm³, and gold a remarkable 19.3 g/cm³ — one of the densest everyday metals, which is part of why a small bar of it is so startlingly heavy.

📚 PART 2 · CALCULATING WITH DENSITY

The density formula has three quantities, so any one can be found from the other two. Cover the one you want with your thumb and the formula shows you what to do:

Find density
D = m / V
Divide mass by volume.
Find mass
m = D × V
Multiply density by volume.
Find volume
V = m / D
Divide mass by density.
▶  Worked example: identify an unknown metal
A shiny block has a mass of 86.4 g and a volume of 32.0 cm³. What metal is it?

D = m / V = 86.4 g ÷ 32.0 cm³ = 2.70 g/cm³ (3 sig figs — both inputs have 3).

Compare to the reference table: 2.70 g/cm³ matches aluminum. Density let us name a substance we never chemically tested — that's the power of an intensive property.

Mass is easy to measure on a balance. Volume is easy too for a neat cube or cylinder — just use geometry. But what about an irregular lump, like a crown or a rock? You use water displacement: drop the object into a known volume of water in a graduated cylinder, and the water level rises by exactly the object's volume. A reading of 25.0 mL that climbs to 32.0 mL tells you the object's volume is 7.0 mL (= 7.0 cm³).

💡 THEN · A FAMOUS STORY ABOUT A CROWN
A well-known story — told centuries after the fact, so treat it as legend more than documented history — has the ancient Greek thinker Archimedes asked by his king to determine whether a goldsmith had cheated by mixing cheaper silver into a golden crown, without melting it down. The insight credited to him is the one in this lesson: a given mass of pure gold always takes up a smaller volume than the same mass of any lighter metal, because gold is denser. By comparing the crown's volume (measured by the water it displaced) against pure gold's, the fraud could be exposed. Whether or not the tale is exact, the science is sound — and it's the very method you'll use in your lab.

📚 PART 3 · WHY DENSITY MATTERS IN THE REAL WORLD

Density quietly runs an enormous amount of the world. A hot-air balloon rises because heating the air inside lowers its density below the cooler air around it. Ice floats — an unusual and life-sustaining quirk, since most solids sink in their own liquid — which is why ponds freeze top-down and fish survive winter beneath. Oil spills spread across the sea because oil is less dense than water. And a steel ship floats because its hull encloses enough air that the ship's overall density (steel plus air) is less than water's, even though the steel alone would sink.

⚠️ MISCONCEPTION CHECK · “HEAVIER THINGS SINK”
The everyday rule “heavy sinks, light floats” is wrong, because floating depends on density (mass per volume), not mass alone. Line up four objects and the myth falls apart:
• A 60,000-ton steel ship floats, while a tiny steel bolt sinks — the far heavier object stays up. What differs is average density: the ship encloses air, so its mass spread over its whole volume is below 1.00 g/mL; the solid bolt is 7.87 g/mL throughout.
• A heavy oak log floats but a small aluminum coin sinks — again mass loses, density wins (oak < 1.00; aluminum = 2.70 g/cm³).
• An air-filled beach ball is easy to hold underwater but shoots back up: almost no mass in a large volume means very low density.
The right question is never “how heavy is it?” but “how does its density compare to the fluid’s?” Less dense than the fluid → floats; more dense → sinks — regardless of total mass.
⭐ THEN → NOW → FUTURE · ONE IDEA, TWENTY-THREE CENTURIES
Then: Archimedes reportedly used density to judge a crown’s composition without destroying it — the first time a measurable intensive property settled a real question about matter. Now: the same principle is a working tool everywhere — foundries verify alloys by density, recyclers separate plastics by floating them in liquids of tuned density, gemologists screen for fakes, and naval architects balance a hull’s average density to design ships that float. Engineers even build near-weightless aerogels and ultra-dense tungsten parts for aerospace and medical imaging. Future: materials scientists are now trying to engineer a material’s density to order — ultralight yet strong lattices for aircraft and batteries, printed structures whose density varies point by point. The open question Archimedes never got to ask: can we design exactly the density a job needs, atom by atom?

Why does an iron ship float when iron sinks?

How do you measure the volume of an irregular rock?
Water displacement: note the water level in a graduated cylinder, add the rock, and the rise in level equals the rock's volume (1 mL = 1 cm³).
