📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
When ice melts and when iron rusts, both are “changes” — so why does a chemist treat them as completely different events?
Melted ice is still water; rusted iron is no longer iron. That single difference — whether the identity of the substance survived — is one of the sharpest tools in chemistry. Today you'll learn to describe matter by its properties, to tell physical changes from chemical ones, and to sort properties into the two kinds that behave differently when you change the amount.

1
I will be able to identify a property or a change in matter as physical or chemical, and justify why.
2

📚 PART 1 · PROPERTIES: HOW WE TELL SUBSTANCES APART
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · OBSERVE FIRST
A silver spoon slowly darkens (tarnishes) over months. Before reading on — do you think the silver is still silver underneath? (No wrong answer — commit to a prediction.)
👀 LOOK BEFORE YOU LABEL
Here are four ordinary changes. Before any definitions, decide for each: is there evidence that a genuinely new substance formed — or is it the same stuff in a new form?
🧊 An ice cube melts into a puddle.
🌊 A spoon of salt dissolves and vanishes into water.
🔥 A sheet of paper burns, giving off smoke and leaving ash.
🌝 A copper roof slowly tarnishes green.
In your notebook, sort them into “same substance” and “new substance,” and jot the observation that decided each one. Notice that two of these can be reversed to recover the original (freeze the puddle, evaporate the water) while two cannot. Keep your reasons — the rest of the lesson gives you the vocabulary to name what you just found.

A property is any characteristic that helps you tell one substance from another. A physical property can be observed without changing what the substance is — color, hardness, density, melting point, boiling point, electrical conductivity. Some you can see at a glance (color); others show up only during a physical change, a change in form or state that leaves the substance's identity intact. Melting wax, dissolving sugar in coffee, and steam condensing on a lid are all physical changes: rearranged, restated, but chemically the same stuff.

A chemical property describes how a substance changes into a different substance — or refuses to. Flammability, the tendency to rust, acidity, and toxicity are chemical properties. You can only observe a chemical property by letting (or trying to let) a chemical change happen, which always produces one or more new substances with new properties. Iron rusting, a match burning, food rotting, copper dissolving in acid — in each, what you end with is genuinely different matter from what you started with.

💡 THE ONE QUESTION THAT DECIDES IT
Ask: “After the change, is it still the same substance?” If yes → physical. If a new substance with new properties has formed → chemical. Clues that point to chemical change: an unexpected color shift, gas bubbling out, a solid forming from liquids, light or heat released, or a smell that wasn't there before.
Physical change — identity survives
Ice → liquid water → steam · sugar dissolving · chopping wood · bending copper. Often reversible. Still the same substance.
Chemical change — new substance forms
Iron rusting · wood burning · bread baking · a banana browning. New properties appear. Usually not easily reversed.
💡 A LOOK BACK · WHEN CHEMISTRY LEARNED TO WEIGH
In the 1770s the French chemist Antoine Lavoisier insisted on doing something earlier investigators had mostly skipped: weighing everything, before and after, including the gases. When he heated metals in sealed vessels, the careful numbers told a story the eye could not — the metal gained exactly the mass that the air inside lost. Burning was not a substance escaping (the old “phlogiston” idea); it was the metal combining with a gas he named oxygen. By trusting the balance over the assumption, Lavoisier turned chemistry into a quantitative science — one where a chemical change is something you can measure, not just watch. You’ll practice that same discipline in this module’s lab.

✎ WRITE A CER · MAKE THE CASE (5–7 SENTENCES)
A shiny iron nail left outside for a month is now coated in reddish-brown flakes; the flakes crumble, don’t conduct or shine like the metal, and the nail weighs slightly more than before. In your notebook, argue whether this was a physical or a chemical change, in 5–7 sentences:
• Claim: state which kind of change it was.
• Evidence: give two specific observations from above that support your claim.
• Reasoning: explain how each observation connects to your claim — and rule out the competing explanation (that the nail is still just iron in a new form) by pointing to evidence that a new substance with different properties formed.
📚 PART 2 · EXTENSIVE vs. INTENSIVE PROPERTIES

Properties also split a second way — by whether they depend on how much matter you have. An extensive property depends on the amount: mass and volume are extensive, because a gallon of milk has more of both than a cup of milk. An intensive property does not depend on the amount: temperature, density, color, and boiling point stay the same no matter how big the sample is. Pour the gallon and the cup together (both at 20 °C) and the temperature is still 20 °C — it didn't add up.

This matters more than it first looks. Because intensive properties don't change with size, they are how we identify substances. Density is the star example: every pure sample of gold has the same density regardless of whether it's a coin or a bar. You'll use exactly this idea in this module's lab to identify an unknown material — and in Lesson 1.07 you'll calculate density yourself.

Tap each card to check yourself:

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
Is mass extensive or intensive?
Extensive. More matter means more mass — it depends on the amount. (Volume is extensive too.)
⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
Is density extensive or intensive?
Intensive. A small gold nugget and a large gold bar have the same density — it doesn't depend on amount, which is why it identifies a substance.

📚 PART 3 · PROPERTIES YOU CAN'T IGNORE: READING A HAZARD DIAMOND

Because chemical properties like flammability and reactivity can be dangerous, substances are labeled with a four-color hazard diamond (the NFPA 704 system). Each colored diamond rates one kind of hazard from 0 (none) to 4 (severe). Knowing how to read it is a real-world application of everything in this lesson — it is properties, turned into safety information at a glance.

FIRE
HEALTH REACT
SPECIAL
Red = flammability · Blue = health hazard · Yellow = reactivity · White = special hazards (oxidizer, reacts with water, corrosive, …)
⚠️ SAFETY
A high number on the red diamond means highly flammable; a 4 on the blue diamond means a brief exposure could be fatal. In any lab — physical or virtual — read the hazard diamond before you handle a substance, and follow the safety practices from your Module 0 lab-safety lesson and signed safety contract.
