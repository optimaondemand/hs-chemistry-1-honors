📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A reaction gives off heat — but where does that energy actually come from, when nothing was added but the reactants themselves? And could I predict, before ever running it, whether a reaction will warm its surroundings or chill them?
Last lesson you measured heat with a thermometer. Today you trace that heat to its source. Every chemical reaction is, underneath, a rearrangement of chemical bonds — old ones breaking, new ones forming — and bonds store energy. The heat a reaction releases or absorbs at constant pressure has a precise name, enthalpy change (ΔH), and the remarkable thing is that you can estimate it on paper before you ever step into a lab, just from a table of bond energies. By the end of this lesson you'll read an enthalpy diagram, tell an exothermic reaction from an endothermic one by the sign of ΔH alone, and predict the energy of a reaction from the bonds it breaks and makes.

📚 PART 1 · ENTHALPY: GIVING THE HEAT A NAME
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
When natural gas burns on a stove, the flame is hot — energy clearly pours out. Where did that energy come from, given that you only supplied gas and air? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

Most reactions in a lab or on a stovetop happen in containers open to the air — at constant pressure. The heat a reaction releases or absorbs under those everyday conditions is so important that it gets its own name: the enthalpy change, written ΔH. For our purposes the rule is simple: ΔH is the heat of the reaction at constant pressure. It is just the q you learned to measure last lesson, now tied specifically to a chemical change happening at ordinary pressure — which is why a calorimeter can measure it directly.

The sign of ΔH carries the whole story of direction. We define it from the system's point of view — the reaction itself:

EXOTHERMIC · ΔH < 0
The reaction releases heat. The system ends with less energy than it started with, so ΔH is negative. The surroundings warm up. (Burning fuel, neutralizing an acid.)
ENDOTHERMIC · ΔH > 0
The reaction absorbs heat. The system ends with more energy than it started with, so ΔH is positive. The surroundings cool down. (Cooking an egg, an instant cold pack.)

Reading an enthalpy diagram

An enthalpy diagram draws energy on a vertical axis and shows where the reactants and products sit. If the products are lower than the reactants, energy was released on the way down — exothermic, ΔH negative. If the products are higher, energy had to be absorbed to climb up — endothermic, ΔH positive. The vertical gap between them is ΔH.

Exothermic (ΔH < 0)
energy ↑
  reactants ——┐
           │ releases heat
           ┗— products (lower)
Endothermic (ΔH > 0)
energy ↑
           ┌— products (higher)
           │ absorbs heat
  reactants ——┘
▶  Think first, then check: A reaction has ΔH = +58 kJ. Is it exothermic or endothermic, and do the surroundings warm or cool?
A positive ΔH means the reaction absorbs heat — it is endothermic. Energy flows from the surroundings into the system, so the surroundings cool down. On a diagram, the products sit higher than the reactants.
💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY
Before anyone could estimate reaction heats from a table, someone had to show that those heats add up in a predictable way. Around 1840 — a few years before Joule pinned down the mechanical equivalent of heat — the chemist Germain Hess, working in St. Petersburg, measured the heat released by reactions with early calorimeters and noticed something reliable: the total heat of a reaction is the same whether it happens in a single step or is broken into several steps along the way. This is now called Hess's law, the law of constant heat summation. Its deeper meaning is pure energy accounting — the heat of a reaction depends only on the starting and ending substances, not on the route between them. That is exactly why you can add and subtract bond energies on paper to forecast a reaction's heat: the ledger balances no matter which path the reaction actually takes.
📚 PART 2 · WHERE THE ENERGY LIVES: BOND ENERGIES

Now to the source. A chemical bond is a pocket of stored energy. To break a bond, you must put energy in — bond-breaking is always endothermic. When a bond forms, energy is released — bond-forming is always exothermic. The amount of energy tied up in a particular bond is its bond energy, measured in kilojoules per mole (kJ/mol). A reaction, deep down, is just bonds breaking in the reactants and new bonds forming in the products — so the reaction's overall energy is a tug-of-war between the energy spent breaking and the energy returned forming.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
Breaking bonds…
…costs energy. You must put energy in to pull bonded atoms apart, so bond-breaking is endothermic. In the bond-energy sum, broken bonds count as a positive (energy spent).
⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
Forming bonds…
…releases energy. New bonds settle atoms into a more stable, lower-energy arrangement, so bond-forming is exothermic. In the sum, formed bonds count as energy returned (subtracted).
⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
The net result…
…decides the sign of ΔH. If forming the products releases more than breaking the reactants cost, energy is left over — exothermic. If breaking costs more, the reaction must absorb energy — endothermic.

That tug-of-war is captured in one estimate. Add up the energy needed to break every bond in the reactants, subtract the energy released when every bond in the products forms, and you get an estimate of the reaction's enthalpy change:

ΔH ≈ Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed)

Σ means “sum of.” A negative result means more energy was released than spent → exothermic. A positive result means the reverse → endothermic.

Worked: the synthesis of hydrogen chloride

Estimate ΔH for H2 + Cl2 → 2 HCl. First count the bonds. On the left you break one H–H bond and one Cl–Cl bond. On the right you form two H–Cl bonds (one in each HCl molecule).

Bonds broken (energy in): 1(H–H) + 1(Cl–Cl) = 436 + 243 = 679 kJ
Bonds formed (energy out): 2(H–Cl) = 2 × 431 = 862 kJ
ΔH ≈ 679 − 862 = −183 kJ (negative → exothermic)

The negative sign tells you, before you ever run it, that this reaction releases energy — forming the two H–Cl bonds gave back 183 kJ more than it cost to break the reactant bonds. That surplus is the heat you'd feel. Treat the number as an estimate: bond energies are averages, so bond-energy predictions land close to measured values but rarely exactly on them. Still, the sign — exothermic or endothermic — comes out reliably, and that alone is powerful.

📚 PART 3 · WHY BOND ENERGIES MATTER: THE ENERGY OF THE FUTURE

The energy locked in chemical bonds is what we burn for nearly everything — but breaking and re-forming chemical bonds is not the only way to release stored energy, and it is far from the most concentrated. The most energetic process humans know reaches past chemistry entirely, into the nucleus of the atom.

☀️ THEN → NOW → FUTURE · MEASURING ENERGY, FROM A CUP TO A STAR
Then. The calorimeter and the enthalpy accounting in these two lessons gave chemists their first way to put a firm number on the energy of a chemical change — to measure, to the joule, how much heat a reaction releases or absorbs. Energy became something you could enter on a ledger and check.

Now. The same principle — energy released when particles settle into a more stable, lower-energy arrangement — runs on a vastly larger scale inside the Sun through nuclear fusion, where light nuclei such as hydrogen are forced together into heavier ones and release enormous energy. For decades scientists and engineers have worked to recreate controlled fusion on Earth as a potential source of abundant, clean power, and several experimental approaches are being actively pursued today.

Future. The hard part is still open. Fusion demands temperatures far hotter than the Sun's core to overcome the repulsion between nuclei, and holding that super-hot matter steady long enough to release more energy than was put in — sustained, net-positive fusion at engineering scale — remains an unsolved challenge rather than a guarantee. Yet the accounting is the very one you practiced here: a more stable arrangement of particles sits at lower energy, and the difference is set free. (You'll meet the nuclear side of this story in the final module.)

🔬 HOW SCIENTISTS KNOW · A MODEL THAT ESTIMATES, NOT DECREES
Bond energies are averages, gathered across many molecules, so a bond-energy ΔH is an honest estimate, not an exact decree — it usually lands near the calorimeter's measured value but not on top of it. That gap is not a failure; it's a feature of using a model. Good scientists hold both numbers at once: the prediction the model makes and the measurement nature returns, and they trust the measurement when the two disagree. The same conservation of energy that let a thermometer identify a metal last lesson now lets a table of bond energies forecast a reaction's heat before it runs — two routes to the same conserved quantity, each checking the other.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Enthalpy change (ΔH) is the heat of a reaction at constant pressure: negative for exothermic reactions that release heat, positive for endothermic ones that absorb it. That energy lives in chemical bonds — breaking bonds costs energy, forming them releases it — so you can estimate ΔH on paper as Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed), and the sign reliably tells you which way the heat will flow. From a coffee-cup thermometer to a bond-energy table to the fusion reactions of the Sun, the same conserved quantity is being tracked. That is what it means to say chemistry — even its energy — can be measured.
