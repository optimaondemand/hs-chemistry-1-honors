📚 WHAT YOU'RE DOING
You'll complete your Gas & Energy Lab — a labeled model of everything you measured this module — using a PowerPoint deck (one slide per station) or a hand-drawn poster. In earlier weeks you diagrammed the gas-law apparatus (P, V, T), measured amounts & partial pressures, and the kinetic-molecular-theory particle picture. This week you add the calorimetry station: a coffee-cup calorimeter with water, a hot metal lowered in, and a thermometer, plus the two big ideas — q = m·c·ΔT and heat lost by the metal = heat gained by the water (conservation of energy). Then rehearse a short narration — the same walkthrough you'll record for the graded module project.
🎨 CHOOSE ONE FORMAT
Option A — Digital (PowerPoint)
Make a short deck: a setup slide (calorimeter + water + hot metal + thermometer, with start/final temps), an energy-flow slide (heat moving from metal to water, q = m·c·ΔT), and a conservation slide (heat lost = heat gained, with one worked number). Use shapes and arrows.
Option B — Hands-on (poster)
Draw the calorimeter and the heat flow on paper, label the start and final temperatures, write the q = m·c·ΔT equation and the conservation rule with one worked number, and photograph it into one document.
🔬 START AS A SCIENTIST · OBSERVE → QUESTION → PREDICT
This walkthrough follows the same investigative arc you use throughout the course — Observe → Question → Predict → Investigate → Analyze → Defend → Extend. Do the first three before you build your slides or poster, and write them down:
Observe. A hot metal is lowered into cooler water; over a minute or two the water warms and the metal cools until both settle at one shared temperature.
Question. How much energy actually moved from the metal to the water — and does every joule the metal loses show up in the water?
Predict. Commit before building: will |qmetal| come out exactly equal to qwater, a little less, or a little more? Say why in one sentence — you'll test this against your own worked number below.
📝 INVESTIGATE → ANALYZE → DEFEND → EXTEND (WHAT TO DO, IN ORDER)
Steps 1–3 are your Investigate (show and set up the measurement); step 4's worked number is your Analyze; the rehearsal in step 5 is where you Defend the conservation claim with evidence; and connecting the calorimetry station back to the whole four-station lab is your Extend.
1
Show the setup: a coffee-cup calorimeter holding water, a thermometer, and a hot metal lowered in. Label the water's start temperature and final temperature.
2
Draw an arrow showing heat flowing from the hot metal into the cooler water (never the reverse). Add the equation q = m·c·ΔT.
3
State the conservation rule clearly: heat lost by the metal = heat gained by the water (qmetal = −qwater), and write one sentence on why — energy is never created or destroyed (the first law).
4
Write one worked number: e.g. 100.0 g water warming 20.0 → 23.5 °C → q = (100.0)(4.184)(3.5) = 1,464 J gained by the water = 1,464 J lost by the metal. (Or use your own calorimetry numbers from Lesson 5.07 or the hands-on.)
5
Save your finished walkthrough (deck or photo document), then rehearse the narration out loud once — gas-law apparatus → measured amounts & partial pressures → the KMT particle picture → the calorimetry station & conservation of energy.

✎ CER REFLECTION (REQUIRED · 3–4 SENTENCES)
Before you submit, write a short Claim – Evidence – Reasoning reflection (on your last slide or below your poster, and in the Canvas comment box):

Claim. State what your calorimetry station demonstrates about energy (for example, that energy is conserved as heat moves from the metal to the water).
Evidence. Cite your worked number — the heat gained by the water and the heat lost by the metal.
Reasoning. Explain why the two are equal and opposite: connect it to the first law and q = m·c·ΔT. Then revisit your Predict step — did |qmetal| equal qwater, and what would a small gap in a real, imperfectly insulated cup tell you?
