📖 WHAT THIS BUILDS ON
From this week's lessons: q = m·c·ΔT (5.07), the calorimetry rule that heat lost by the metal = heat gained by the water (5.07), and conservation of energy (the first law) — now run across several trials in Excel, where the spreadsheet does the repetitive arithmetic so you can focus on the chemistry and on identifying each metal.
This is your week's “doing chemistry” work, and it rehearses exactly the calculations your module calorimetry lab report asks for.
📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
A chemist ran the same calorimetry experiment three times with three different mystery metals. Each time, a 50.0 g piece of metal was heated to 100.0 °C in a boiling-water bath and then dropped into 100.0 g of water at 20.0 °C in a coffee-cup calorimeter. Only the final equilibrium temperature differed. In Excel, you'll build a table that computes the heat the water gained, the heat the metal lost, and each metal's specific heat — then identify the metal by matching that value against a short reference table. Finally you'll analyze what the data reveals about conservation of energy.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
From nothing but a few temperature readings, can you measure how much energy moved — and use it to name a metal you never touched?
🔢 CONSTANTS & REFERENCE YOU'LL NEED
Specific heat of water: c = 4.184 J·g−1·°C−1.   Conservation rule: heat gained by water = heat lost by metal (qmetal = −qwater).

✎ FIRST · REASON IN WORDS, BEFORE ANY FORMULA
A calculator can only check thinking you have already done. So before you build a single Excel formula, look at the data and write one or two sentences in your spreadsheet (or notebook) answering this: all three trials use the same mass of metal, the same starting temperatures, and the same 100.0 g of water — only the final temperature differs (23.5, 24.1, 27.7 °C). Which trial's metal do you predict has the largest specific heat, and why? Reason it out in words using q = m·c·ΔT — do not compute anything yet.
▶  Reveal a model answer after you have written your own
Trial 3 should have the metal with the largest specific heat. Its water rose the most (20.0 → 27.7 °C), so its water gained the most heat — and by conservation that metal gave up the most heat. Since every metal has the same mass and cooled from 100 °C by a similar amount, the one that released the most energy per gram per degree must have the highest c. In words: bigger water temperature rise → more heat delivered → larger specific heat. (Your Excel work will confirm it points to aluminum.) The point of predicting first is that when your spreadsheet agrees, you understand why; when it disagrees, you have caught either a formula error or a gap in your reasoning.
📝 WHAT TO DO IN EXCEL
1
Enter the three-trial table with headers in row 1: Trial, Mass metal (g), Metal start (°C), Mass water (g), Water start (°C), Final temp (°C).
2
Add a ΔT water (°C) column: final temp minus water start, e.g. =G2-E2 (adjust the column letters to match yours). Fill down all three trials.
3
Add a q water (J) column — the heat the water gained: mass water × 4.184 × ΔT water, e.g. =D2*4.184*H2.
4
Add a q metal (J) column — the heat the metal lost. By conservation it's the negative of q water: =-I2 (point at your q water cell).
5
Add a ΔT metal (°C) column: final temp minus metal start, e.g. =G2-C2 (this will be negative — the metal cooled).
6
Add a c metal (J·g−1·°C−1) column: rearrange q = m·c·ΔT to c = q ÷ (m × ΔT). Use absolute values so the sign cancels, e.g. =ABS(J2)/(B2*ABS(K2)) (q metal ÷ (mass metal × ΔT metal)).
7
Add an Identity column: compare each computed c metal to the reference table and name the metal (the closest match). You can type it by eye, or use a lookup if you're comfortable with one.
8
In cells or a text box below the table, answer the three Analysis Questions below.
❓ ANALYSIS QUESTIONS (answer in your spreadsheet)
A. In every trial the water started at the same temperature and gained heat, yet the three metals reached very different final temperatures. Why does the metal with the smallest specific heat cause the smallest rise in the water's temperature? Tie your answer to q = m·c·ΔT.
B. Your q metal column is the exact negative of your q water column. Which law of nature guarantees that, and what does it mean in plain words for this experiment?
C. A coffee-cup calorimeter is not perfectly insulated — a little heat always escapes to the cup and the air. If some of the metal's heat leaks to the surroundings instead of the water, would your calculated specific heat for the metal come out a little too high or a little too low? Explain, and say what that tells you about trusting a single measurement.
▶  Check your thinking — answer key
Computed results (rounded):
• Trial 1 — ΔT water = 23.5 − 20.0 = 3.5 °C; q water = 100.0 × 4.184 × 3.5 = 1,464 J; q metal = −1,464 J; ΔT metal = 23.5 − 100.0 = −76.5 °C; c metal = 1,464 ÷ (50.0 × 76.5) = 0.383 → copper (ref 0.385).
• Trial 2 — ΔT water = 24.1 − 20.0 = 4.1 °C; q water = 100.0 × 4.184 × 4.1 = 1,715 J; q metal = −1,715 J; ΔT metal = 24.1 − 100.0 = −75.9 °C; c metal = 1,715 ÷ (50.0 × 75.9) = 0.452 → iron (ref 0.449).
• Trial 3 — ΔT water = 27.7 − 20.0 = 7.7 °C; q water = 100.0 × 4.184 × 7.7 = 3,222 J; q metal = −3,222 J; ΔT metal = 27.7 − 100.0 = −72.3 °C; c metal = 3,222 ÷ (50.0 × 72.3) = 0.891 → aluminum (ref 0.897).

A. For a fixed mass and a fixed starting temperature, a metal with a smaller specific heat carries less energy for each degree it cools. Even though all three metals cool by roughly the same amount, the low-c metal (copper) releases the least total heat, so by q water = m·c·ΔT the water's ΔT is smallest. Specific heat is the metal's “energy density per degree” — lower c means less heat handed to the water.
B. The first law of thermodynamics — conservation of energy. Energy is never created or destroyed, so every joule the metal loses must reappear in the water (assuming negligible loss to the cup and air). In plain words: the metal didn't make the water's heat appear from nowhere — it handed over exactly the energy it gave up.
C. The calculated c metal would come out a little too low. We assume all of the metal's heat went into the water, so we set |q metal| = q water. If some heat actually leaked to the surroundings, the water gained less than the metal truly lost, so our q (based only on the water) underestimates the metal's real heat output — and dividing a too-small q by (m × ΔT) gives a too-small c. That is exactly why the trial-1 value (0.383) sits just below copper's true 0.385: a real reminder that one imperfect measurement can mislead, and why we repeat trials and improve insulation.
