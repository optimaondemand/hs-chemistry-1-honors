📚 THE TASK
Energy is invisible, but it is never lost. When a hot object meets a cooler one, heat flows from the hot to the cold until both settle at one shared temperature — and every joule the hot object gives up is a joule the cold object takes in. That bookkeeping is the first law of thermodynamics, and a calorimeter is the simple instrument that lets us watch the energy move and add it up.
In this investigation, a hot piece of metal is dropped into cooler water in a coffee-cup calorimeter, and the whole system drifts to one final temperature. You are given the masses, the starting temperatures, and the final temperature. Your task is to write a lab report that works the energy all the way through:
From the data below, calculate the heat gained by the water, determine the heat released by the metal, find the metal's specific heat (and identify the metal), and account for energy with the first law — explaining why the heat lost should equal the heat gained, and why a real measurement comes out a little off.
You'll write it in Microsoft Word, organized into the standard lab-report sections listed further down.
🧪 THE INVESTIGATION · YOUR DATA
A student heated a piece of metal in a boiling-water bath, then quickly dropped it into water held in an insulated coffee-cup calorimeter and stirred until the temperature stopped changing. The principle is conservation of energy — the heat lost by the metal equals the heat gained by the water:
q = m · c · ΔT   and   qmetal = −qwater
(specific heat of water, c = 4.184 J·g⁻¹·°C⁻¹)
Quantity Value
Mass of metal50.0 g
Initial temperature of metal (boiling-water bath)100.0 °C
Mass of water100.0 g
Initial temperature of water20.0 °C
Final equilibrium temperature (both metal and water)23.5 °C
For identifying the metal, compare your calculated specific heat to these reference values (J·g⁻¹·°C⁻¹): copper 0.385 · iron 0.449 · aluminum 0.897 · lead 0.128.
⭐ WHY THIS MATTERS
This lab report is one of only two teacher-reviewed pieces in Module 5 (the other is your Gas Behavior & Energy Lab VR project). Your teacher reads it personally and responds to your reasoning and your error analysis — so make it your careful best, and show every step of your math.
🧠 YOUR REASONING TOOLKIT (the path through the calculation)
Use these steps in your own words and show the arithmetic for each — don't just copy the headings.
Step 1 — Find the heat gained by the water.
Use qwater = m · c · ΔT, where ΔT = Tfinal − Tinitial for the water. Keep the sign: the water warms up, so its q is positive.
Step 2 — State the heat released by the metal.
By conservation of energy, qmetal = −qwater. The metal loses exactly what the water gains, so its q is negative and equal in size.
Step 3 — Find ΔT for the metal.
ΔTmetal = Tfinal − Tinitial,metal. The metal cools, so this value is negative.
Step 4 — Solve for the metal's specific heat & identify it.
Rearrange q = m · c · ΔT to c = q ÷ (m · ΔT) using the metal's q, mass, and ΔT (use magnitudes so c comes out positive). Compare your value to the reference table to name the metal.
Step 5 — Account for the energy (first law).
Explain why the heat lost should equal the heat gained, then discuss why a real measurement comes out slightly off — where could energy have gone that the simple model ignores?
📝 STRUCTURE: WRITE THESE LAB-REPORT SECTIONS
1
Purpose. One or two sentences: what question is this investigation answering? (e.g., how much heat moves from a hot metal to cooler water, and what does that tell us about the identity of the metal?)
2
Procedure & Given Data. Briefly describe the coffee-cup calorimeter method and present the given data in a small table (the two masses, the three temperatures, the specific heat of water, and the q = mcΔT relationship).
3
Calculations (q = mcΔT). Show all work: the heat gained by the water, the heat released by the metal, the metal's ΔT, and the metal's specific heat. Label every number with its units and keep the signs straight.
4
Conservation / Energy Analysis. State your specific heat, identify the metal from the reference table, and explain how the first law of thermodynamics (qmetal = −qwater) governs the whole experiment — the energy books must balance.
5
Error Sources. Give at least two realistic reasons a real calorimetry result deviates from the ideal — and say, for each, whether it would make your calculated specific heat come out too high or too low. (Think: heat lost to the cup and the air, the metal cooling slightly during transfer, an imperfectly read thermometer, evaporation.)
6
Conclusion — make your argument (Claim → Evidence → Reasoning). Don't just restate numbers — argue from your data. Claim: name the metal and state its specific heat. Evidence: cite your own results — the heat gained by the water, the heat released by the metal, the metal's ΔT, and the specific heat you computed. Reasoning: use conservation of energy to link the evidence to the claim — explain why |qmetal| should equal qwater, why your calculated specific heat comes out close to but a little off from the accepted value because some heat left the system to the cup and the surrounding air, and what the whole experiment reveals: energy is never created or destroyed; it only moves, and the books must close.
✎ LENGTH & STYLE
Aim for roughly 500–700 words across the six sections, plus your worked calculations (which can be handwritten and photographed in, or typed). Use correct terminology — heat, specific heat, q = mcΔT, conservation of energy, first law of thermodynamics, exothermic/endothermic. Carry units through every step, keep your signs straight (the water gains, the metal loses), and keep a sensible number of significant figures (three is plenty here). The chemistry and the clear, complete math are what's graded — not polished prose.
⚠️ A NOTE ON USING AI TOOLS
An AI tool will happily produce a specific-heat number for this data — and it can quietly use the wrong ΔT, flip a sign, or apply water's specific heat to the metal, and still sound completely confident. If you consult one, treat it as a draft to check, never an answer to paste: re-run q = mcΔT yourself, confirm that qmetal = −qwater, and make sure its arithmetic matches yours before you trust a single digit. The instrument proposes; your calculation verifies. That is exactly the skill this report is built to grow.
