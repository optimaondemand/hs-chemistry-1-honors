Gas Mixtures & Dalton's Partial Pressures

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The air around you is not one gas but a crowd of them — nitrogen, oxygen, argon, carbon dioxide, water vapor — all pressing on you at once. When you read a single pressure on a gauge, whose pressure are you reading?
So far you have treated a gas as if the container held just one substance. But most real gas samples are mixtures. The breath you just took, the bubbles in a soda, a gas collected in the lab over water — all are blends. This lesson answers a measurement question: if several gases share a container, how does each one contribute to the total pressure, and how do you pull the part you care about out of the whole? The answer, first stated two centuries ago, is beautifully simple — and it has a practical payoff for any gas you collect in a real experiment.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to state Dalton's law of partial pressures — that the total pressure of a gas mixture is the sum of the partial pressures of its components — and use it to find a missing pressure.
2
I will be able to calculate the mole fraction of a gas in a mixture and relate it to that gas's share of the total pressure.
3
I will be able to correct for water vapor when a gas is collected over water by subtracting the water's vapor pressure from the total.

📚 PART 1 · DALTON'S LAW: PRESSURES SIMPLY ADD
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
Imagine a rigid sealed tank holding nitrogen alone that reads 0.60 atm on a gauge. Now you pump oxygen into the same tank until the oxygen, by itself, would read 0.40 atm. What does the gauge read with both gases inside? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

When two or more gases share a container and don't react, each one behaves as if it were alone — spreading to fill the whole space and striking the walls on its own schedule. The pressure a single gas in a mixture would exert if it occupied the container by itself is called that gas's partial pressure. Two centuries ago, John Dalton noticed the tidy consequence: the total pressure of a mixture is just the sum of the partial pressures of all the gases in it. This is Dalton's law of partial pressures.

DALTON'S LAW OF PARTIAL PRESSURES
Ptotal = P1 + P2 + P3 + …
each gas contributes its own pressure, independently

Why should pressures add so simply? Because, as you saw in the kinetic picture of gases, the molecules of a gas are mostly far-apart specks moving through empty space. One gas hardly notices another sharing the room; each just keeps bouncing off the walls at its own rate. The wall feels the strikes from all the gases at once, so the total push is the sum of every gas's individual push.

🔬 HOW SCIENTISTS KNOW · THE LAW YOU MEASURE vs. THE MODEL THAT EXPLAINS IT
Dalton's law — partial pressures simply add — is something chemists measured. Mix known gases, read the gauge, and the total comes out equal to the sum every time; that reproducible result is the law, and it stands on the measurement alone. The paragraph above is a different thing: a model (particles as far-apart specks that ignore one another) offered to explain why the pressures add rather than average. Ask yourself which is which. The additivity is the evidence you can check on any gauge; the “far-apart specks” picture is the theory earning its keep by making sense of that evidence — and, later, by predicting where it should break down when gases are crowded together.

Worked once: nitrogen and oxygen in a tank

A tank holds nitrogen at a partial pressure of 0.60 atm and oxygen at a partial pressure of 0.40 atm, with nothing else inside. The total pressure is simply their sum:

Ptotal = PN₂ + PO₂ = 0.60 atm + 0.40 atm = 1.00 atm

The law also runs in reverse, which is often the more useful direction. If you measured the total at 1.00 atm and knew oxygen contributed 0.40 atm, you could find nitrogen's share by subtraction: 1.00 − 0.40 = 0.60 atm. That subtraction trick — total minus the part you know — is the heart of every partial-pressure problem you'll meet, including the lab correction at the end of this lesson.

📚 PART 2 · MOLE FRACTION: EACH GAS'S SHARE

There is a second way to describe a gas's role in a mixture, and it ties straight back to Avogadro's law from the last lesson. The mole fraction of a gas is the fraction of all the molecules in the mixture that belong to that gas: its moles divided by the total moles. We write it with the letter x.

xA = moles of gas Atotal moles of gas

Here is the elegant part. Because equal numbers of gas molecules take up pressure in the same proportion (Avogadro's law again), a gas's mole fraction is also its share of the total pressure. So the partial pressure of any gas equals its mole fraction times the total pressure: PA = xA × Ptotal. A gas that is 60% of the molecules supplies 60% of the pressure.

Worked once: the same nitrogen–oxygen tank

Return to the tank where nitrogen contributes 0.60 atm and oxygen 0.40 atm, for a total of 1.00 atm. The mole fraction of nitrogen is its share of the pressure:

xN₂ = PN₂ ÷ Ptotal = 0.60 atm ÷ 1.00 atm = 0.60
check: xO₂ = 0.40 ÷ 1.00 = 0.40, and 0.60 + 0.40 = 1.00 ✓ (mole fractions in a mixture always add to 1)

Nitrogen makes up 60% of the molecules in that tank, so it supplies 60% of the pressure. Note the built-in honesty check: the mole fractions of all the gases must add up to exactly 1, because together they account for every molecule. If your fractions don't sum to 1, you've made an error — the accounting hasn't balanced.

💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY
In the early nineteenth century the English chemist John Dalton — the same Dalton who revived the atomic theory you met in Module 2 — studied the behavior of the atmosphere and the gases dissolved in it. From careful measurements he reasoned that each gas in a mixture acts independently of the others, so their pressures simply add. It is the same instinct that made his atomic theory powerful: treat matter as separate particles that keep their own identities. His law let chemists finally make sense of gases collected in the laboratory, where the gas you want is almost never alone.

📚 PART 3 · COLLECTING A GAS OVER WATER

Now the payoff that makes Dalton's law a working lab tool. A common way to capture a gas produced in a reaction is to bubble it up through water into an inverted, water-filled container — the gas displaces the water and collects at the top. It is cheap and simple, but it comes with a catch: as the gas bubbles through, it picks up water vapor. What you collect is never the pure gas; it's the gas plus some evaporated water sharing the same space.

By Dalton's law, the total pressure you measure is the sum of two partial pressures — the dry gas you want, plus the water vapor:

Ptotal = Pdry gas + Pwater vapor
so   Pdry gas = Ptotal − Pwater vapor

The water's contribution — its vapor pressure — depends only on the temperature, and chemists look it up in a table. At 25 °C, the vapor pressure of water is 23.8 mmHg. To find the pressure of the dry gas alone, you subtract that water-vapor pressure from the total. It's the same “total minus the part you know” move from Part 1, now doing real work.

Worked all the way through: a gas collected at 25 °C

You collect a gas over water at 25 °C, and when the levels are matched the total pressure inside equals the room pressure, 760 mmHg (1 atm). What is the pressure of the dry gas you actually produced?

Step 1 — identify the two partial pressures: the dry gas (unknown) and water vapor at 25 °C (23.8 mmHg).

Step 2 — subtract the water vapor from the total:
Pdry gas = Ptotal − Pwater vapor = 760 mmHg − 23.8 mmHg = 736.2 mmHg

So the dry gas you produced exerts 736.2 mmHg, not the full 760 — the missing 23.8 mmHg belongs to water vapor that rode along. Skip this correction and you would overstate how much gas you made by counting water as if it were your product. This is exactly the kind of honest accounting that self-government in measurement demands: report what you actually made, not what the raw gauge reading seemed to say.

▶  Think first, then check: A gas is collected over water at 25 °C and the total pressure is 745.0 mmHg. What is the pressure of the dry gas?
Subtract the water-vapor pressure at 25 °C: 745.0 − 23.8 = 721.2 mmHg of dry gas. Same move every time — total minus the water vapor.

Hand an AI tool a table of gas-collection data and it will happily report partial pressures, mole fractions, and corrected volumes in seconds. Useful — and exactly why you treat it like any instrument reading: worth having, not worth trusting blindly. The arithmetic is the evidence; the tool is only a second opinion.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

In a mixture, each gas acts as if it were alone, so the total pressure is the sum of the partial pressures — Dalton's law. A gas's mole fraction is its share of the molecules and therefore its share of the pressure (PA = xA × Ptotal), and all the mole fractions add to 1. The practical reward is the over-water correction: when you collect a gas through water, subtract the water's vapor pressure (23.8 mmHg at 25 °C) to get the dry gas alone. A single gauge reading hides a crowd — honest measurement means separating each gas's true contribution from the whole.
