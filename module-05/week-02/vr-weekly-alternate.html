📚 WHAT YOU'RE DOING
You'll continue your Gas Behavior & Energy Lab showcase — the project you build across the module — using a PowerPoint deck (one slide per build) or a hand-drawn poster. Last week's build (1 of 4) drew the gas-law apparatus and demonstrated Boyle's law. This week you add the gas-stoichiometry station — the 2 : 1 : 2 volume ratio for 2 H2 + O2 → 2 H2O drawn to scale — and a partial-pressure jar showing two gases whose partial pressures add to a total. This is Build 2 of 4; later builds add KMT particle annotations and a calorimetry setup before you record a short narrated walkthrough.
🎨 CHOOSE ONE FORMAT
Option A — Digital (PowerPoint)
Add a new slide to your project deck titled “Build 2 — Gas Stoichiometry & Partial Pressures.” Draw the three volume blocks in the 2:1:2 ratio for 2 H2 + O2 → 2 H2O, and a jar holding two gases each labeled with a partial pressure, plus a text box showing the pressures sum to the total.
Option B — Hands-on (poster)
Add a Build-2 panel to your poster: draw the 2:1:2 volume blocks and the two-gas jar, write the partial pressures and their sum by hand, then photograph the panel into your project document.
📝 WHAT TO DO, IN ORDER
You'll move through an investigator's arc — Observe → Question → Predict → Investigate → Analyze → Defend → Extend — then finish with a short written reflection. Draw first, but reason as you go.
1
Observe. Open your Week-1 project file (deck or poster) and look at what you drew last week — the gas-law apparatus. Add a new Build 2 — Gas Stoichiometry & Partial Pressures section, leaving room for the KMT-particle build you'll add next week.
2
Question. Write the guiding question at the top of the new section: when gases react or mix, does the space they take up tell you how many molecules are involved? Everything you draw next is a way of answering it.
3
Predict. Before you draw, write two predictions and initial them: (a) for 2 H2 + O2 → 2 H2O, what will the volume ratio of the three gases look like? (b) When two gases share a container, will the total pressure be the sum, the average, or the larger of the two? Commit before you find out.
4
Investigate. Draw the gas-stoichiometry station for 2 H2 + O2 → 2 H2O: three volume blocks where the H2 and H2O blocks are visibly twice the size of the O2 block, so the 2 : 1 : 2 volume ratio shows. Then draw a partial-pressure jar holding two gases (for example, N2 and O2), each labeled with its partial pressure — e.g., “P(N2) = 0.60 atm” and “P(O2) = 0.40 atm.”
5
Analyze. Caption the station “equal volumes → equal moles, so volume ratio = mole ratio” and add the Dalton's-law sum beside the jar: Ptotal = P(N2) + P(O2) = 0.60 + 0.40 = 1.00 atm. Return to your prediction note and mark where you were right and where you were surprised.
6
Defend. Write a short reasoning caption that argues from your drawing: why does a volume ratio equal a mole ratio, and why do two gases' pressures add instead of averaging? Point to your blocks and jar as the evidence — you are making an argument, not just stating a rule.
7
Extend. Add a short note connecting the panel to the wider world: name one real place gases are metered by volume (for example, ammonia synthesis or medical gas mixtures), and write one question you'd want to test next.
8
Save. Save your updated showcase (deck or photo document). Keep this file — you'll add KMT particle annotations and a calorimetry setup in Weeks 3–4.
