📖 WHAT THIS BUILDS ON
From this week's lessons: the molar volume of a gas at STP (22.4 L/mol) and gas-volume ratios as mole ratios (5.03), and Dalton's law of partial pressures plus the over-water correction (5.04) — now run across several trials in Excel, where the spreadsheet does the repetitive arithmetic so you can focus on the chemistry.
This is your week's “doing chemistry” work, and it rehearses exactly the gas calculations the rest of the module leans on.
📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
You'll build two tables in Excel. In Part A, you process six gas-stoichiometry trials — three where a measured volume of oxygen reacts (2 H2 + O2 → 2 H2O), and three where a measured mass of limestone decomposes (CaCO3 → CaO + CO2) — computing the volume of product gas at STP. In Part B, you process gas mixtures and gas-collection data — totaling partial pressures, finding mole fractions, and correcting for water vapor. Then you'll analyze what the data reveal.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
Across real trials, how do a measured volume, a measured mass, and a measured pressure each become a count of gas — and what gets left out if you skip a correction?
🔢 CONSTANTS YOU'LL NEED
Molar volume at STP: 22.4 L/mol.   Molar mass: CaCO3 = 100.09 g/mol.   Mole ratios: O2 : H2O = 1 : 2 and CaCO3 : CO2 = 1 : 1.   Water vapor pressure at 25 °C: 23.8 mmHg.   (1 atm = 760 mmHg.)
✍️ BEFORE YOU CALCULATE · PUT THE REASONING IN WORDS
Chemistry is reasoning first, arithmetic second. Before you build a single formula, write two or three sentences (in your notebook or a text box in the spreadsheet) answering each of these in plain words — no numbers yet:
1. For a volume trial (1–3), how does a measured volume of O2 become a volume of H2O vapor? Why can you use the coefficient ratio directly, without ever converting to moles?
2. For a mass trial (4–6), why does finding the CO2 volume take an extra step that the volume trials don't? What is that step, in words?
3. For the gas collected over water, what will you do to the measured total pressure, and why — what is in that container besides the gas you want?
▶  Check your reasoning — model answer (read only after you've written yours)
1. Both O2 and H2O are gases at the same temperature and pressure, so by Avogadro's law equal volumes hold equal numbers of molecules — the volume ratio is the mole ratio. That lets me multiply the O2 volume straight by the coefficient ratio (2 H2O per 1 O2) without going through moles at all.
2. In the mass trials the reactant CaCO3 is a solid, not a gas, so there is no volume-ratio shortcut. I have to cross the mole bridge: grams → moles (divide by molar mass), moles CaCO3 → moles CO2 (1:1 ratio), then moles → volume (multiply by 22.4 L/mol). The extra step is converting the solid's mass to moles before I can reach a gas volume.
3. I will subtract the water vapor pressure (23.8 mmHg at 25 °C) from the measured total, because a gas collected over water shares the space with evaporated water. The gauge reads dry gas plus water vapor, and only the dry-gas part is my product.
This written reasoning is part of your “Analysis + self-check” work — it is scored under that rubric row, not as separate points.

📝 WHAT TO DO IN EXCEL
1
Part A — volume trials (1–3). Add a mol O₂ column with a formula like =11.2/22.4 (volume ÷ molar volume). Then a mol H₂O column = mol O₂ × 2 (the 1:2 ratio). Then a volume H₂O at STP (L) column = mol H₂O × 22.4.
2
Part A — mass trials (4–6). Add a mol CaCO₃ column = mass ÷ 100.09. Then mol CO₂ = mol CaCO₃ × 1 (the 1:1 ratio). Then volume CO₂ at STP (L) = mol CO₂ × 22.4.
3
Sanity-check Trials 1–3 a second way. Since both reactant and product are gases at the same conditions, the volume ratio equals the mole ratio: O₂ volume × 2 should equal your H₂O volume. Add a Volume-ratio check column (=O2_volume*2) and confirm it matches.
4
Part B — mixtures (M1–M3). Add a Total P (atm) column = P(N₂) + P(O₂) (Dalton's law), and a mole fraction N₂ column = P(N₂) ÷ Total P. A formula like =B2/(B2+C2) works.
5
Part B — over-water (W1–W3). Add a P(dry gas) (mmHg) column = total − 23.8 (the water vapor pressure at 25 °C). A formula like =D5-23.8 works. Fill down all three collections.
6
In cells or a text box below the tables, answer the three Analysis Questions below.
❓ ANALYSIS QUESTIONS (answer in your spreadsheet)
A. In Trials 1–3 you found the H₂O volume two ways — through the mole bridge (÷22.4, ×2, ×22.4) and through the volume ratio (×2). Did the two methods agree? Explain why the volume-ratio shortcut works for these trials but would not work for Trials 4–6.
B. For mixtures M1–M3, do the mole fractions of N₂ and O₂ in each sample add up to 1? Show one example, and explain why this must always be true for a complete gas mixture.
C. For collections W1–W3, how much pressure did the water vapor account for in each case, and was it the same number every time? If a student forgot the over-water correction, would they report too much or too little dry gas — and why does that matter for an honest result?
▶  Check your thinking — answer key
Part A results (rounded to 3 sig figs):
• Trial 1 — mol O₂ = 11.2/22.4 = 0.500; mol H₂O = 1.00; volume H₂O = 22.4 L. Volume-ratio check: 11.2 × 2 = 22.4 L ✓
• Trial 2 — mol O₂ = 5.0/22.4 = 0.223; mol H₂O = 0.446; volume H₂O = 10.0 L. Volume-ratio check: 5.0 × 2 = 10.0 L ✓
• Trial 3 — mol O₂ = 22.4/22.4 = 1.00; mol H₂O = 2.00; volume H₂O = 44.8 L. Volume-ratio check: 22.4 × 2 = 44.8 L ✓
• Trial 4 — mol CaCO₃ = 10.0/100.09 = 0.0999; mol CO₂ = 0.0999; volume CO₂ = 2.24 L.
• Trial 5 — mol CaCO₃ = 50.0/100.09 = 0.500; mol CO₂ = 0.500; volume CO₂ = 11.2 L.
• Trial 6 — mol CaCO₃ = 25.0/100.09 = 0.250; mol CO₂ = 0.250; volume CO₂ = 5.60 L.

Part B results:
• M1 — Total P = 0.60 + 0.40 = 1.00 atm; x(N₂) = 0.60/1.00 = 0.60 (x(O₂) = 0.40).
• M2 — Total P = 1.50 + 0.50 = 2.00 atm; x(N₂) = 1.50/2.00 = 0.75 (x(O₂) = 0.25).
• M3 — Total P = 0.80 + 0.20 = 1.00 atm; x(N₂) = 0.80/1.00 = 0.80 (x(O₂) = 0.20).
• W1 — P(dry gas) = 760.0 − 23.8 = 736.2 mmHg.
• W2 — P(dry gas) = 745.0 − 23.8 = 721.2 mmHg.
• W3 — P(dry gas) = 800.0 − 23.8 = 776.2 mmHg.

A. Yes — both methods give the same H₂O volume for Trials 1–3. The volume-ratio shortcut works only because both the O₂ and the H₂O are gases measured at the same temperature and pressure, so equal volumes mean equal moles (Avogadro's law) and the volume ratio equals the coefficient ratio. In Trials 4–6 the reactant CaCO₃ is a solid, not a gas, so its mass has no volume-ratio relationship to the CO₂ gas; you must cross the mole bridge through grams → moles → volume.
B. Yes. Example (M2): x(N₂) = 0.75 and x(O₂) = 0.25, and 0.75 + 0.25 = 1.00. The mole fractions of all gases in a mixture must sum to 1 because together they account for every molecule present — there is nothing left over. (M1: 0.60 + 0.40 = 1; M3: 0.80 + 0.20 = 1.)
C. The water vapor accounted for 23.8 mmHg in every case — the same number, because vapor pressure depends only on temperature (here, 25 °C), not on how much gas was collected. A student who forgot the correction would use the full total (e.g., 760 instead of 736.2 mmHg), reporting too much dry gas — they would be counting water vapor as if it were product. Honest measurement means subtracting what isn't yours.
