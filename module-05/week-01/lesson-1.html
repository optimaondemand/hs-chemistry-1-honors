Gas Pressure & Measurement

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Right now, a column of air dozens of kilometers tall is pressing down on you — and on every surface around you — yet you feel nothing unusual. How could anyone measure the weight of something as thin and invisible as air?
You have spent two quarters learning what matter is and how it bonds and reacts. Now we turn to a state of matter that hides its workings in plain sight: gases. A single driving question runs beneath this whole module: why do gases behave so differently from the solids and liquids you already know — filling any container they are put in, shrinking under a push, swelling when warmed? Answering that takes measurement, and measurement of a gas begins with pressure. A gas has no shape of its own and seems to weigh almost nothing, yet it pushes — on the walls of its container, on a piston, on the mercury in a tube. That push, spread over an area, is pressure, and learning to measure it is the first move in making the behavior of gases quantitative. This lesson defines pressure precisely, tells the story of the instrument that first captured it, and gives you the unit conversions every gas-law calculation in this module depends on. By the end you'll be able to read a pressure in any of five units and convert cleanly among them.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain pressure as force divided by area, and describe how a barometer uses a column of liquid to measure the pressure of the atmosphere.
2

3
I will be able to convert a pressure measurement from one unit to another using these equivalences as conversion factors.

📚 PART 1 · WHAT PRESSURE IS: FORCE SPREAD OVER AREA
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
Press your thumb flat against a wall, then press the sharp point of a pencil against the same wall with the same push. The point digs in; your thumb does not. Same force — so what changed? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

Pressure is one of the cleanest ideas in chemistry: it is simply a force divided by the area over which that force acts.

THE DEFINITION OF PRESSURE
Pressure = Force ÷ Area
the same push, spread thin or concentrated, gives a small or a large pressure

This is why a sharp knife cuts and a dull one does not, why snowshoes keep you on top of soft snow, and why a thumbtack pushes into a board with the gentle pressure of a finger. In each case the force is ordinary; it's the area that does the work. For a gas, the force comes from countless molecules striking a surface and bouncing off. Each tiny collision is a push; added up over a whole wall, those pushes become a steady, measurable pressure. The molecules of the air around you are doing this constantly — against your skin, against the ceiling, against the inside of a balloon. We don't notice because the air inside us pushes back with equal force. But the push is real, and it is large.

How large? The column of air stretching from the ground to the edge of space has weight, and that weight, divided by the area it rests on, is atmospheric pressure. At sea level it amounts to roughly 10 newtons of force on every square centimeter — about the weight of a small car spread across a tabletop. The trouble for early scientists was not believing that air had weight; it was finding an instrument that could capture the number.

▶  Think first, then check: Two boxes weigh the same, but one rests on a small foot and one on a wide base. Which one presses on the floor with greater pressure?
The box on the small foot. Same force (their weights are equal), but the smaller contact area means a larger force-per-area — a higher pressure. Pressure depends just as much on where the force lands as on how big it is.
📚 PART 2 · THE BAROMETER: WEIGHING THE AIR

The instrument that solved the problem is the barometer, and the idea behind it is beautifully simple. Take a long glass tube, seal one end, fill it completely with liquid mercury, and turn it upside down into an open dish of mercury without letting any air in. The mercury in the tube starts to fall — but it does not all run out. It stops, leaving a tall column standing in the tube with an empty space (a vacuum) above it.

Why does the column stop falling? Because the atmosphere is pushing down on the mercury in the open dish, and that push holds the column up. The weight of the standing mercury column exactly balances the weight of the air pressing on the dish. So the height of the column becomes a direct measure of atmospheric pressure: when the air pushes harder, the column rises; when the air pushes less — on a stormy day, or high on a mountain — the column falls. At sea level, the mercury settles at a height of about 760 millimeters. That single number is the anchor of every pressure unit you are about to meet.

The key picture: the air's push on the dish ≡ the weight of a 760-mm mercury column.
More air push → taller column · less air push → shorter column. The barometer turns an invisible force into a height you can read with a ruler.
💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY
In 1643, the Italian physicist Evangelista Torricelli — once an assistant to Galileo — built the first barometer to settle a stubborn puzzle: water pumps of the day could not lift water higher than about ten meters, no matter how good the pump. Torricelli suspected the limit had nothing to do with the pump and everything to do with the air pushing the water up from below. To test it with a shorter, more manageable instrument, he used mercury, which is about fourteen times denser than water, so its column would stand only about three-quarters of a meter tall. When the mercury stopped at roughly 760 mm and left a vacuum above it, he had done two remarkable things at once: he had measured the pressure of the atmosphere, and he had produced one of the first sustained vacuums ever made. He reportedly wrote that we live “submerged at the bottom of an ocean of air” — a phrase that captures the whole insight. The unit torr is named in his honor.
🔬 NATURE OF SCIENCE · MEASURE vs EXPLAIN
Torricelli's barometer measures the atmosphere's push to the millimeter, and gives a reliable, repeatable number. Does that measured number, by itself, tell you why the air pushes at all? (No wrong answer — commit, then read the responses.)

🌟 THEN · NOW · FUTURE — THE BAROMETER'S LONG REACH
Then: Torricelli's mercury column (1643) first turned the invisible weight of air into a number a person could read off a ruler. Now: that same measurement is the backbone of weather and climate monitoring — electronic pressure sensors in ground stations, balloon-borne radiosondes, aircraft, and satellites report atmospheric pressure continuously, and falling or rising pressure is one of the strongest signals forecasters use to track storms and fronts. Long records of these readings help scientists study how the atmosphere is changing over time. Future: researchers are still working to model the atmosphere well enough to predict extreme weather further ahead and to untangle the feedbacks that drive long-term climate — open problems where better and denser pressure data keep mattering. A measurement first made with a glass tube of mercury is still being refined nearly four centuries later.
📚 PART 3 · THE UNITS OF PRESSURE — AND HOW TO CONVERT

Because pressure was first measured with mercury columns, with barometers, and later with industrial gauges, several units grew up side by side — and a chemist needs to move among them fluently. The good news: they are all just different ways of writing the same sea-level pressure. One standard atmosphere is the reference point, and everything else is measured against it.

ONE STANDARD ATMOSPHERE, FIVE WAYS
1 atm = 760 mmHg = 760 torr = 101.3 kPa = 14.7 psi
(mmHg and torr are the same thing; kPa is the SI unit; psi is the everyday pressure-gauge unit)

Each unit has a home. mmHg ("millimeters of mercury") comes straight from the barometer's column height. torr is identical to mmHg, renamed for Torricelli. The kilopascal (kPa) is the SI unit, built on force per area (a pascal is one newton per square meter). psi ("pounds per square inch") is what you read on a tire gauge. Converting between them is pure dimensional analysis — exactly the unit-cancelling habit you built with the mole in the last module. Write the equivalence as a fraction, arrange it so the unit you start with cancels, and multiply.

Worked once: 2.0 atm into mmHg and into kPa

To mmHg (use 1 atm = 760 mmHg):
2.0 atm × (760 mmHg / 1 atm) = 1520 mmHg  (atm cancels, leaving mmHg)

To kPa (use 1 atm = 101.3 kPa):
2.0 atm × (101.3 kPa / 1 atm) = 202.6 kPa

Worked the other direction: 380 mmHg into atm

Use the same equivalence, flipped so mmHg cancels:
380 mmHg × (1 atm / 760 mmHg) = 0.50 atm

Notice the discipline at work: in every case the starting unit cancels and the unit you want is left standing. If your units don't cancel, the conversion factor is upside down — flip it. This careful accounting of units is the same self-government the quantitative side of chemistry always asks for: watch every unit, and the arithmetic takes care of itself.

🤖 AI AS AN INSTRUMENT · NOW
Ask an AI tool to convert a pressure and it will answer instantly — convenient, and exactly why you should treat it like any instrument: useful, but to be verified. Pressure conversions are a place where a tool can quietly use a slightly different value (some sources round 1 atm to 760.0 mmHg, others carry more digits; kPa is sometimes given as 101.325 instead of 101.3). The reliable move is to set up the conversion yourself, watch your units cancel, and use the tool only to confirm your number. The unit-cancelling is the evidence; the tool is a second opinion, not the judge.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Pressure is force spread over area, and a gas exerts it through the endless collisions of its molecules. Torricelli's barometer turned that invisible push into a height you can read, fixing the standard atmosphere at a 760-mm mercury column. From that one anchor flow all the units — 1 atm = 760 mmHg = 760 torr = 101.3 kPa = 14.7 psi — and converting among them is just careful, unit-cancelling arithmetic. Hold onto this: pressure is the first measurable handle on a gas, and every gas law in this module begins with a pressure you can name and convert.
