📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Squeeze a sealed syringe and it pushes back harder. Warm a sealed bottle and it strains at its cap. A gas seems to have a will of its own — but is its behavior predictable? Can a few simple rules tell you exactly what a gas will do?
In Lesson 5.01 you learned to measure a gas's pressure. A gas, though, is described by four quantities at once — its pressure (P), its volume (V), its temperature (T), and the amount of gas present (in moles, n). Change one and the others respond. Over the 1600s and 1700s, careful experimenters discovered that these responses follow clean, repeatable patterns — the gas laws. These laws are also the sharpest answer to the module's driving question — why gases behave so differently from solids and liquids: unlike a solid or a liquid, a gas visibly changes its volume and pressure when you squeeze it or warm it, and the gas laws say by exactly how much. This lesson walks through those laws one relationship at a time, then shows how they all fold into a single master equation, PV = nRT, that links all four quantities at once. With it, you can predict the state of any gas sample from measurements you can actually make.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to state and apply Boyle's, Charles's, and Gay-Lussac's laws and the combined gas law to predict how a gas responds when conditions change.
2

3

📚 PART 1 · THE THREE TWO-VARIABLE GAS LAWS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
You seal some air in a syringe, plug the tip, and press the plunger to squeeze the gas into half the space. What do you predict happens to the gas's pressure? (No wrong answer — just commit before you read on.)

Three classic gas laws each hold two of the four quantities fixed and watch how the other two relate. Each was discovered the same way: hold the amount of gas constant, vary one thing, measure the other, and look for the pattern.

Boyle's law — pressure and volume (at constant T)

For a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature, pressure and volume are inversely related: squeeze the volume down and the pressure climbs, in exact proportion. The product P × V stays constant, which gives the working form P1V1 = P2V2.

Worked: A gas at 1.0 atm fills 2.0 L. Compressed to 0.50 L at the same temperature, what is its new pressure?
P2 = (P1V1) / V2 = (1.0 atm × 2.0 L) / 0.50 L = 4.0 atm
The volume dropped to one-quarter, so the pressure rose four-fold — exactly the inverse trade-off.
⚡ PREDICT FIRST · BEFORE THE NEXT LAW
Now change temperature instead of pressure. You warm a sealed balloon (its pressure free to stay constant) from 300 K to 600 K. Before you read the law, predict what happens to the balloon's volume. (No wrong answer — commit first.)

Charles's law — volume and temperature (at constant P)

For a fixed amount of gas at constant pressure, volume and Kelvin temperature are directly related: warm the gas and it expands, in proportion to the absolute temperature. The working form is V1/T1 = V2/T2. The catch — and it is non-negotiable — is that temperature must be in Kelvin, never Celsius. (K = °C + 273.)

Worked: A gas occupies 2.0 L at 300 K. Heated to 600 K at constant pressure, what is its new volume?
V2 = (V1 × T2) / T1 = (2.0 L × 600 K) / 300 K = 4.0 L
Double the Kelvin temperature, double the volume. (Had you used Celsius, the ratio would have been meaningless.)

Gay-Lussac's law — pressure and temperature (at constant V)

For a fixed amount of gas in a rigid container, pressure and Kelvin temperature are directly related: heat a sealed, rigid can and its internal pressure rises. The working form is P1/T1 = P2/T2 — again, Kelvin only.

Worked: A rigid tank holds gas at 1.0 atm and 300 K. Heated to 450 K, what is the new pressure?
P2 = (P1 × T2) / T1 = (1.0 atm × 450 K) / 300 K = 1.5 atm
💡 THEN · A MOMENT IN HISTORY
These laws bear the names of the people who measured them. Robert Boyle, working in England, published his pressure–volume relationship in 1662 after careful experiments with trapped air in a J-shaped tube. More than a century later, in France, Jacques Charles investigated how gases expand on heating (in the 1780s, the era of the first hot-air balloons), and Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac extended that work into the early 1800s, pinning down the pressure–temperature link and refining the role of absolute temperature. Notice what these are: empirical laws — faithful summaries of what gases do, drawn from measurement. They came long before anyone could explain why gases behave this way (that explanation, the kinetic-molecular theory, waits for Lesson 5.05). A law that reliably describes a pattern is genuine knowledge, even before the underlying mechanism is understood — one of the quiet lessons in how science actually advances.

📚 PART 2 · COMBINING THE LAWS — AND ADDING AVOGADRO

The three laws above are really three faces of one relationship. Stitch them together and you get the combined gas law, which handles any change in which P, V, and T all vary while the amount of gas stays fixed:

THE COMBINED GAS LAW
P1V1 / T1 = P2V2 / T2
temperatures in Kelvin, always

You can recover any single law from this one by holding a variable constant. Hold T fixed and the T's cancel, leaving Boyle. Hold P fixed and you get Charles. Hold V fixed and you get Gay-Lussac. One equation, three special cases — tidy, and a relief for your memory.

There is still one quantity the combined law leaves out: the amount of gas. That gap is filled by Avogadro's law: at the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of any gas contain equal numbers of molecules — so volume is directly proportional to the number of moles. Add a gas and it takes up more room; the more moles, the more volume. With Avogadro's law in hand, all four quantities — P, V, T, and n — can finally be united in a single equation.

📚 PART 3 · THE IDEAL GAS LAW: PV = nRT

All four quantities come together in the single most useful equation in this module, the ideal gas law. It describes a sample's complete state at one moment — no “before and after” needed:

THE IDEAL GAS LAW
PV = nRT
R = 0.08206 L·atm·mol−1·K−1

Here P is pressure, V is volume, n is the number of moles, T is the Kelvin temperature, and R is the ideal gas constant — a single number that makes the equation balance for any gas. The value of R depends on the units you use; with the value above, pressure must be in atm, volume in liters, amount in moles, and temperature in Kelvin. Match those units and R = 0.08206 does the rest. To solve for any one variable, just rearrange the equation algebraically before you plug in.

Worked: the volume of one mole at STP

Chemists agree on a reference set of conditions called STP — standard temperature and pressure — defined as 273 K (0 °C) and 1 atm. What volume does exactly 1.00 mol of an ideal gas occupy there? Solve PV = nRT for V:

V = nRT / P = (1.00 mol × 0.08206 × 273 K) / 1.00 atm = 22.4 L

That result is worth memorizing: one mole of any ideal gas occupies 22.4 liters at STP. This molar volume is a shortcut you'll lean on constantly in the next lesson's gas stoichiometry — it connects moles (the counting unit from Module 4) directly to a volume you can read off a measuring cylinder.

STP, at a glance: T = 273 K · P = 1 atm · molar volume = 22.4 L/mol. (One mole at STP — whether H2, O2, or CO2 — fills the same 22.4 L, because volume depends on the number of molecules, not their identity.)

▶  Think first, then check: How many moles of gas are in 2.0 L at 1.0 atm and 273 K?
Rearrange PV = nRT to n = PV / RT: n = (1.0 atm × 2.0 L) / (0.08206 × 273 K) = 2.0 / 22.4 = 0.0893 mol. (Sensible check: 2.0 L is just under one-tenth of the 22.4 L a full mole would fill at STP — so a bit under 0.1 mol is right.)

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

A gas is fully described by P, V, T, and n, and these four are not independent — change one and the others answer in predictable ways. Boyle, Charles, and Gay-Lussac each captured one pairing; the combined gas law unites the first three, and Avogadro's law brings in amount, so that everything folds into one master equation, PV = nRT, with R = 0.08206 and temperature always in Kelvin. At STP (273 K, 1 atm), one mole of any ideal gas fills 22.4 L. Hold onto this: with PV = nRT you can predict the state of a gas from measurements you can actually take — and in the next lesson, you'll use it to count gas by its volume.
