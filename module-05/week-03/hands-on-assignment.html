📖 WHAT THIS BUILDS ON
From this week's lessons: the kinetic-molecular theory, average kinetic energy and absolute temperature, Graham's law of effusion, and the conditions where real gases deviate from the ideal gas law.
This is your week's “doing chemistry” work — nine problems (plus a short “explain in words” warm-up) that turn the theory into numbers, predictions, and a data-based argument. No spreadsheet needed; a calculator with a square-root key is enough.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
Given two gases at the same temperature, can I predict which moves faster, by how much, and where the ideal model can — and can't — be trusted?
🧮 YOUR TOOLKIT (USE THESE EXACT VALUES)
Graham's law of effusion: rate1 / rate2 = √(M2 / M1)  —  the lighter gas (smaller M) is faster
Average kinetic energy ∝ absolute temperature (K): at one temperature, all gases share the same average KE
Molar masses (g/mol): H2 2.016 · He 4.00 · CH4 16.04 · N2 28.02 · O2 32.00 · CO2 44.01 · Cl2 70.90
Real gases deviate most at: high pressure and low temperature

🧪 PART A · GRAHAM'S-LAW RATIOS (problems 1–4)
✎ EXPLAIN BEFORE YOU CALCULATE (do this first)
Before you touch the formula, write one or two sentences in words (no equation): for two gases at the same temperature, why does the lighter gas effuse faster? Naming the reason before you compute keeps the numbers meaningful — you should always be able to say what a calculation means.
▶  Check your thinking
At one temperature, every gas has the same average kinetic energy. Since kinetic energy is ½mv², a lighter molecule must move at a higher speed to carry that same energy — so it reaches and slips through the pinhole more often. The formula rate₁/rate₂ = √(M₂/M₁) just puts a number on that idea.
1. How many times faster does helium (He) effuse than oxygen (O2)? Let He be gas 1.
2. How many times faster does hydrogen (H2) effuse than carbon dioxide (CO2)? Let H2 be gas 1.
3. How many times faster does methane (CH4) effuse than oxygen (O2)? Let CH4 be gas 1.
4. How many times faster does nitrogen (N2) effuse than chlorine (Cl2)? Let N2 be gas 1.
▶  Check your thinking — Part A answer key
Use rate1/rate2 = √(M2/M1) — masses flip relative to the rates.
1. √(32.00 / 4.00) = √8.00 = 2.83 → He effuses 2.83× faster than O2.
2. √(44.01 / 2.016) = √21.83 = 4.67 → H2 effuses 4.67× faster than CO2.
3. √(32.00 / 16.04) = √1.995 = 1.41 → CH4 effuses 1.41× faster than O2.
4. √(70.90 / 28.02) = √2.530 = 1.59 → N2 effuses 1.59× faster than Cl2.
Each answer is greater than 1 because the lighter gas (gas 1) wins — the sanity check holds every time.
🧬 PART B · RANK THE SPEEDS (problems 5–6)
5. These gases are all at the same temperature: O2, He, CO2, H2, N2. Rank them from fastest to slowest average molecular speed, and state the single rule you used.
6. Two flasks at the same temperature hold helium and chlorine (Cl2). (a) Which gas has the higher average kinetic energy? (b) Which gas has the higher average speed? Explain how both answers can be true at once.
▶  Check your thinking — Part B answer key
5. At one temperature, lighter = faster. Order the molar masses: H2 2.016 < He 4.00 < N2 28.02 < O2 32.00 < CO2 44.01. So fastest → slowest is H2 > He > N2 > O2 > CO2. Rule used: at the same temperature all gases share the same average kinetic energy, so the lightest molecules move fastest.
6. (a) Equal — same temperature means the same average kinetic energy for both. (b) Helium has the higher average speed. Both hold because KE = ½mv²: helium is far lighter than Cl2 (4.00 vs. 70.90 g/mol), so it must move faster to carry the same energy.
🔥 PART C · KINETIC ENERGY & TEMPERATURE (problem 7)
7. A sample of neon gas is warmed from 200 K to 400 K. (a) What happens to the average kinetic energy of its particles, and by roughly what factor? (b) Explain, using the kinetic-molecular theory, why warming the gas in a rigid sealed container raises its pressure. (c) Why must temperature be in kelvins, not Celsius, for the kinetic-energy relationship to work?
▶  Check your thinking — Part C answer key
7. (a) Average kinetic energy is proportional to the absolute temperature, and 400 K is twice 200 K, so the average kinetic energy roughly doubles (the particles move faster). (b) Faster particles strike the rigid walls harder and more often; since the volume can't change, the pressure rises (this is Gay-Lussac's law explained by the theory). (c) The Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero, the point of zero molecular motion, so kinetic energy is truly proportional to it. Doubling 200 K to 400 K doubles the energy; doubling a Celsius value has no such meaning because its zero is arbitrary.
❄️ PART D · IDEAL vs. NON-IDEAL (problem 8)
8. For each condition below, predict whether the gas will behave nearly ideal or will deviate noticeably from PV = nRT, and give a one-line reason for each.
  (a) Carbon dioxide compressed to very high pressure at room temperature.
  (b) Helium at low pressure and high temperature.
  (c) A gas cooled to a temperature just above where it would condense to a liquid.
  (d) Then name the two ideal-gas assumptions that fail in non-ideal conditions.
▶  Check your thinking — Part D answer key
8. (a) Deviates noticeably — very high pressure crowds the particles so their own volume and attractions matter. (b) Nearly ideal — low pressure and high temperature keep particles far apart and fast-moving; this is the model's home turf. (c) Deviates noticeably — near condensing, slow particles feel one another's attractions strongly. (d) The two failing assumptions: that particles have no volume of their own (fails at high pressure) and that particles have no attraction for one another (fails at low temperature). Van der Waals corrected for each.

Check yourself: did your reasons connect “crowded” to particle volume and “cold/slow” to attractions?
📊 PART E · ANALYZE THE DATA, THEN WRITE (problem 9)
9. An unknown light gas and oxygen (O2) are released through identical pinholes under the same temperature and pressure. Measured effusion times:

  •  unknown light gas: fully effuses in 30. s
  •  oxygen (O2): fully effuses in 85 s

(a) Use the times to find the rate ratio (ratelight / rateO₂). Remember: the faster gas takes less time, so rate is inversely proportional to time.
(b) In 2–3 sentences, interpret the data: what do these times tell you about the light gas's molar mass compared with oxygen, and — using Graham's law — argue whether the light gas could be helium. Support your claim with the number you found.
▶  Check your thinking — Part E answer key
9. (a) Because rate is inversely proportional to time, ratelight/rateO₂ = tO₂/tlight = 85 / 30 = 2.83. The light gas effuses about 2.83× faster than oxygen.
(b) A sample write-up: “The light gas is 2.83 times faster than oxygen, so by Graham's law it must be much lighter. Setting 2.83 = √(MO₂/Mlight) gives 2.83² = 32.00/Mlight, so Mlight = 32.00 / 8.0 ≈ 4.0 g/mol. That matches helium (4.00 g/mol), so the data are consistent with the light gas being helium.” Notice how the measured ratio (2.83) is the same number as the He-vs-O₂ ratio you found in problem 1 — the experiment and the theory agree.

Data-analysis habit: you turned raw times into a rate ratio, then used it as evidence to support a claim about identity. That "claim → evidence → reasoning" move is exactly what a lab report does.
