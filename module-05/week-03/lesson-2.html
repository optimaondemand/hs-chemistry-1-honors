Lesson 5.06
Non-Ideal (Real) Gas Behavior
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
PV = nRT has worked flawlessly for two weeks. So here is an uncomfortable question for a careful scientist: when does it stop working — and how would you know before it embarrasses you?
Every powerful model is built on simplifying assumptions, and the ideal gas law is no exception. Last lesson's kinetic theory openly assumed that gas particles have no volume of their own and feel no attraction to one another. Those assumptions are almost true — true enough that the gas laws hold to high precision in ordinary conditions. But “almost” is a word a measurer must respect. In this lesson you'll find the conditions where real gases drift away from the ideal prediction, see why they do, and learn how chemists patch the model to account for it. The deeper lesson is about the nature of models themselves: knowing where a model breaks down is not a weakness of the model — it is part of understanding it.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify the conditions — high pressure and low temperature — under which real gases deviate most from ideal behavior.
2
I will be able to explain which two ideal-gas assumptions — zero particle volume and zero intermolecular attraction — fail in real gases, and what each one corrects.
3
I will be able to describe, in general terms, how the van der Waals corrections account for particle volume and attraction, and explain why knowing a model's limits is part of using it well.

📚 PART 1 · WHERE THE IDEAL MODEL STRAINS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
The ideal-gas picture pretends gas particles are points with no size and no attraction for one another. Under which conditions do you think that pretense would matter most — that is, when would real particles behave least like ideal points? (No wrong answer — just commit.)

A gas that obeys PV = nRT perfectly is called an ideal gas. No real gas is perfectly ideal — but under ordinary conditions, real gases come remarkably close, which is exactly why the gas laws have served you so well. The interesting question is where, and why, real gases start to disagree with the ideal prediction. The answer, confirmed by careful measurement, is two-cornered: real gases deviate most at high pressure and at low temperature.

WHERE REAL GASES DEPART FROM PV = nRT
HIGH pressure  ·  LOW temperature
crowded together and moving slowly — the conditions near becoming a liquid

Notice that these are precisely the conditions that push a gas toward becoming a liquid — crowd the particles, slow them down, and eventually they condense. That is the clue to why the ideal model strains: the two assumptions the kinetic theory made for convenience are the very things that begin to matter as a gas approaches its liquid. In ordinary air at room temperature and pressure, particles are far apart and moving fast, so the assumptions hold and PV = nRT is excellent. Drive the gas into the high-pressure, low-temperature corner and the cracks appear.

⚡ TRY IT · PREDICT THE DEVIATION
For each set of conditions, predict whether a real gas will behave NEARLY IDEAL or will DEVIATE NOTICEABLY from PV = nRT. (Remember: crowded + slow = trouble.)
1. A gas at low pressure and high temperature (like ordinary warm air).
2. A gas at very high pressure and room temperature (compressed into a steel cylinder).
3. A gas at very low temperature, close to the point where it would condense to a liquid.
4. A gas at standard temperature and pressure (273 K, 1 atm).
📚 PART 2 · THE TWO ASSUMPTIONS THAT FAIL

The ideal gas law strains in exactly two places, and each one traces back to one of the convenient assumptions from last lesson. Naming them is the whole content of “non-ideal” behavior.

1. Particles do take up space
The ideal model treats particles as volumeless points. In reality each particle has a real, if tiny, size. At low pressure that size is negligible against all the empty space — but squeeze the gas to high pressure and the particles' own volume becomes a noticeable fraction of the container. The space truly available to move in is less than the measured volume.
2. Particles do attract one another
The ideal model assumes particles ignore each other entirely. In reality they feel weak attractions. At high temperature the particles move too fast to be caught — but cool the gas and these attractions tug particles inward slightly as they approach the walls, so each one strikes a bit more gently. The measured pressure is lower than the ideal prediction.

These two effects pull in opposite directions on the numbers — the finite size of particles tends to make a real gas push harder than ideal, while the attractions tend to make it push softer — and which one wins depends on the conditions. The takeaway you need is the cause-and-effect link: finite particle volume matters at high pressure, and intermolecular attractions matter at low temperature. Both are simply the real properties of real particles asserting themselves once the gas is crowded or cooled enough to notice.

🔗 A LOOK AHEAD
Those weak attractions between particles have a name and a story of their own — they are the intermolecular forces, and they are the reason gases can condense into liquids and solids at all. You'll study them in depth in the next module, where the same attractions that spoil ideal-gas behavior turn out to explain boiling points, surface tension, and why water is liquid at room temperature while similar-sized molecules are gases. For now, just hold the idea that real particles are not indifferent to one another.
▶  Think first, then check: You need to use PV = nRT for a gas, and you want the answer to be as accurate as possible. Should you run it on the gas at high pressure and low temperature, or at low pressure and high temperature?
Low pressure and high temperature. Those are the conditions where particles are far apart and moving fast, so their finite size and mutual attractions are negligible and the gas behaves almost ideally — PV = nRT is at its most accurate. At high pressure and low temperature the assumptions fail and the ideal prediction drifts off. Knowing this is what it means to use a model well: you apply it where it holds and you stay alert where it doesn't.
📚 PART 3 · PATCHING THE MODEL: VAN DER WAALS

A good scientist who finds a model's limits doesn't throw the model away — they fix it. In the late 1800s the Dutch physicist Johannes van der Waals did exactly that. He kept the ideal gas law as the backbone and added two correction ideas, one for each failed assumption. You won't compute with his full equation in this course, but you should recognize the two corrections by what they do:

The two van der Waals correction ideas (conceptual)
▶  A volume correction — subtract the space the particles themselves occupy, because the room truly available to move in is less than the container's measured volume. (This matters at high pressure.)
▶  A pressure correction — add back a little to account for the attractions that make particles strike the walls more gently, so the “true” pressure is a touch higher than measured. (This matters at low temperature.)

Each correction carries a constant that is different for every gas, measured experimentally — large, attractive molecules need bigger corrections; tiny, weakly-attracting ones like helium need almost none. The beauty of the patch is that it dials smoothly back to the ideal gas law: at low pressure and high temperature both corrections shrink to nearly nothing, and van der Waals's equation becomes PV = nRT again. The fix doesn't replace the ideal model; it extends it into the corner where the model alone would fail, and reduces to it everywhere else.

📜 HISTORY OF SCIENCE · VAN DER WAALS AND THE REAL GAS (1873)
When Johannes Diderik van der Waals took up the problem in the Netherlands, the ideal gas law already worked well — but precise measurements showed real gases drifting from it under pressure, and no one had explained the drift from first principles. In his 1873 doctoral thesis he asked what the ideal model left out, and answered with two physical corrections: gas particles occupy real volume, and they attract one another. Building those two ideas into the equation reproduced the measured deviations the ideal law had missed — the deciding evidence that his physical picture was right. His work tied the behavior of gases to forces between molecules at a time when the very existence of molecules was still debated, and it later helped guide the liquefaction of gases once thought “permanent,” such as oxygen and nitrogen. It is a model of how science advances: not by discarding a good law, but by finding precisely what it neglects.
🔬 HOW SCIENTISTS KNOW · THE POWER AND LIMITS OF A MODEL
The ideal gas law is a model — a deliberate simplification of reality. That it is “not perfectly true” is not a flaw to be embarrassed by; it is the normal condition of every scientific model. What makes the ideal gas law good is that it is accurate over a huge useful range, it is built from a clear physical picture, and — crucially — it tells you in advance where it should fail: high pressure, low temperature, near condensation. A model you understand is one whose edges you can name. Van der Waals didn't prove the ideal law wrong; he showed precisely how far it reaches and built a careful extension for the rest. That is how scientific understanding usually grows — not by tearing down what works, but by mapping its boundaries and reaching past them with evidence.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

The ideal gas law is a model, and like every model it has limits. Real gases deviate from PV = nRT at high pressure and low temperature, because under those conditions the two convenient assumptions fail — particles really do take up space (matters at high pressure) and really do attract one another (matters at low temperature). Van der Waals patched the model with a volume correction and a pressure correction that fade back to the ideal law in ordinary conditions. Knowing where a model breaks down is not its weakness — it is the mark of truly understanding it. Carry that habit forward: the same intermolecular attractions you just met as a nuisance become the heart of the next module.
