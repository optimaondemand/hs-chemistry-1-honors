📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You wake up, glance outside, and announce, “It rained last night.” You weren't awake to see it — so how do you know?
You just did science without calling it that. You noticed something, gathered evidence, and reasoned your way to a claim. This whole course runs on that exact move — getting from what you observe to what you can defend. This lesson gives you the two tools you'll use all year: the empirical-questioning arc (the rhythm every lesson follows) and CER — Claim, Evidence, Reasoning — the format for writing a scientific answer that holds up. Get comfortable with these now, and every lesson and every writing assignment ahead will feel familiar.

📚 PART 1 · THE RHYTHM OF EVERY LESSON: FROM PHENOMENON TO EXPLANATION
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · PREDICT FIRST
You step outside in the morning and see wet pavement everywhere. Before you decide why it's wet, what should a careful thinker do first? (No wrong answer — just commit to a stance.)

Science is less a fixed set of facts than a way of moving from a puzzling thing in the world to an explanation you can defend. Across this course, every lesson follows the same investigative rhythm. It has a name — the Empirical-Questioning Arc — and it is the single framework this whole course is built on. Learn its seven moves now and you'll recognize them in every chemistry lesson ahead. You won't always see each step labeled, but the lesson is always walking this path:

Read the arc and you'll notice one thing it never does: it never starts with the answer. It begins with a phenomenon — something you notice — moves through evidence, and only then arrives at an explanation. That direction, phenomenon → evidence → explanation, is the single most important habit in this course. Front-loading the conclusion (“it's salt because the teacher said so”) skips the part that makes it science.

Make this arc your reusable mental model. Copy its seven moves into your science notebook, and when a lesson or an investigation feels confusing, ask yourself which step you're on — am I still observing, or have I jumped ahead to explaining? Every module of this course, from measuring matter to modeling the atom, is another trip around this same arc.

The scientific method, drawn as a loop

You've probably seen the “scientific method” drawn as a numbered list. It's more honest to draw it as a loop: you observe and ask a question, propose a tentative explanation (a hypothesis), test it with an experiment, analyze what happened, and either build toward a broader law or theory — or, just as often, go back and ask a sharper question. Science doesn't end; it circles. We'll develop this much further in Module 1; for now, just hold the shape of it.

Observe & question → Hypothesize → Experiment → Analyze → Law / Theory — and then the loop turns again, because every answer raises a new question.
📚 PART 2 · CER: HOW TO WRITE A SCIENTIFIC ANSWER

When you have to write down a scientific answer, this course uses a simple, powerful structure called CER — Claim, Evidence, Reasoning. You'll write CER responses all year, so learn it once and reuse it forever. Here is what each part does:

C
Claim
Your answer to the question — one clear statement. (“Yes, it rained last night.”)
E
Evidence
The specific observations or data that support the claim. Concrete and checkable.
R
Reasoning
The logic that links the evidence to the claim — why this evidence actually supports this answer.

Worked all the way through: “Did it rain last night?”

Back to the morning question from the top of the lesson. You weren't awake to watch it happen, so you have to reason from what you can see. Here is the full CER:

Claim — Yes, it rained last night.
Evidence — There are puddles on the sidewalk, the parked car is wet all over, and the sky is still gray.
Reasoning — Rain falls from clouds and wets surfaces that are exposed to the open sky. The combined pattern — wet ground, a wet car, and overcast clouds — fits “it rained” better than the alternatives. A lawn sprinkler, for instance, would wet the grass but not the car parked on the street or the whole sidewalk, and it wouldn't explain the gray sky.

Notice what the reasoning did that the evidence alone could not: it explained why the evidence points to rain and ruled out a competing explanation. Evidence without reasoning is just a pile of facts; reasoning is the part that turns those facts into an argument. Good scientific writing always closes that gap explicitly — it never makes the reader guess how the evidence connects to the claim.

A preview of chemistry CER: “Is this cube pure gold?”

The rain example works with everyday observation. Chemistry CER works exactly the same way, just with measured evidence about matter — the kind you'll gather starting in Module 1. Suppose you're handed a shiny metal cube and asked whether it's pure gold. You measure its mass and volume and calculate its density (mass per unit volume — a property every pure substance has a fixed value for). Here's the CER:

Claim — The cube is not pure gold.
Evidence — Its measured density is about 10.5 g/cm³. Pure gold has a density of 19.3 g/cm³; pure silver is about 10.5 g/cm³.
Reasoning — Density is an intrinsic property — it's the same no matter how big or small the sample is — so it can be used to identify a material. The cube's density (10.5) doesn't match gold (19.3), so the cube can't be pure gold; it matches silver far better. Ruling out a competing explanation: someone might object, “Maybe the cube is just a small piece of gold.” But size can't be the answer — density doesn't change with size, so a smaller gold cube would still read 19.3. The evidence points to “not gold,” not to “less gold.”

That's the whole move you'll make all year: a claim about matter, measured evidence, reasoning from a property to a conclusion — and honesty about the alternatives. The chemistry gets deeper, but the shape never changes.

🔬 HOW SCIENTISTS KNOW · THE OLDEST MOVE IN SCIENCE
CER isn't a school invention — it's the bones of how scientists have argued for centuries. When a chemist claims a gas was produced in a reaction, no one takes their word for it; they expect the evidence (a measurement, a color change, a captured sample) and the reasoning that ties that evidence to the claim. The scientists you'll meet this year — people investigating the nature of matter long before the tools were good — won and lost arguments on exactly this basis: not who spoke loudest, but whose claim the evidence best supported. That is the “honesty” thread of this course in action: evidence over preference.
📚 PART 3 · WEAK vs. STRONG: WHAT MAKES A CER GOOD

The format alone doesn't make a CER strong. A weak CER states a claim and then leans on opinion (“because I think so”); a strong CER backs the claim with specific evidence and spells out the reasoning. Compare these two answers to the same question — “Did it rain last night?”

❌ WEAK CER
“It rained last night. I just have a feeling it did. It probably rains a lot this time of year, so it makes sense.”
Why it's weak: the claim rests on a feeling, not observation. There's no specific evidence you could check, and no reasoning linking anything to the claim.
✅ STRONG CER
“It rained last night. There are puddles on the sidewalk and the parked car is wet all over. Rain wets surfaces exposed to the open sky, and that pattern fits rain better than a sprinkler, which wouldn't wet the street or the car.”
Why it's strong: a clear claim, specific checkable evidence, and reasoning that explains the link and rules out a rival.
⚡ TRY IT · CLAIM, EVIDENCE, OR REASONING?
Each snippet below is one piece of a CER response about absorbent paper towels. Decide whether it is the Claim, the Evidence, or the Reasoning. Instant feedback on each.
1. “Brand B held 40 mL of water while Brand A held only 25 mL on the same-size sheet.”
2. “Holding more water on an equal-size sheet means the towel absorbs more, and using equal sheets makes it a fair comparison.”
3. “Brand B is the more absorbent paper towel.”
4. “Rain wets surfaces exposed to the open sky, so a wet car parked outside fits rain better than a sprinkler.”
🤖 AI AS AN INSTRUMENT · DRAFT, THEN VERIFY
An AI tool can draft a tidy-looking CER in seconds — and that is exactly the trap. AI writes confident sentences whether or not the evidence is real: it can invent a measurement, misstate a result, or offer reasoning that doesn't actually hold. Treat it like any instrument: useful, but to be checked. If you ever use AI to help structure a response, you remain responsible for verifying every piece of evidence against what actually happened and confirming the reasoning is sound. The evidence is yours to stand behind; the tool is only a second pair of hands, never the source of truth.
▶  Think first, then check: A classmate writes only “Brand B is more absorbent because it's better.” Which CER part is missing or weak?
The Claim is fine (“Brand B is more absorbent”), but the Evidence is missing entirely — no measurements or observations — and the Reasoning is empty (“because it's better” just restates the claim). A strong version would add the specific amounts of water each brand held and explain why holding more on an equal sheet means more absorbent.

💪 THE BIG IDEA TO CARRY FORWARD

Science moves from phenomenon to evidence to explanation — never the other way around — and the empirical-questioning arc is the rhythm every lesson follows. When you write that thinking down, you use CER: a clear Claim, specific Evidence, and Reasoning that explains why the evidence supports the claim. A weak answer leans on opinion; a strong one stands on checkable evidence and explicit logic. You'll use this exact structure in every writing assignment ahead.
